# Writing Is Cheap. Knowing Is Not.

### An Epistemic Futures Manifesto

*From the Epistemic Futures Summit, MIT, September 23–24, 2026. Drafted by a writing committee of participants (Kenneth Cukier, Kara Miller, Geoffrey Bilder, Henry Farrell) and circulated to all participants for comment. This is not a consensus document. Where participants asked for their disagreement to be recorded, it is recorded, by name.*

---

We are scholars, technologists, publishers, librarians, journalists, and builders of open infrastructure. We do not agree about what today's AI systems are. We do agree on a good deal about what they are doing to the way people make, check, keep, and share knowledge, and about what should be done.

This is not a statement of alarm. It is not a celebration either. It is a set of commitments. Much of what follows is not new: some of us have been arguing for it for twenty years. What is new is that we can no longer afford to put it off.

Nothing about this is inevitable.

## What we believe

**1. The crisis is older than the machines.**
AI did not break our knowledge institutions. Publish-or-perish incentives, a publishing business that profits from volume, evaluation by counting papers and citations, and the gutting of local journalism and library budgets were doing that already. AI makes all of it faster and cheaper. So we treat AI as an accelerant, not the culprit. The most urgent fixes are to our own incentives, and chasing the newest shiny object makes them harder, not easier.

People like to say we have been here before: with writing, with print, with the web. That is true, but the lesson is not reassuring. Trust in print did not appear by itself. It was built over decades by people who policed printers, founded journals, and invented peer review, and it has needed constant, paid, unglamorous maintenance ever since. Some of today's panic is just panic; every decade has its "crisis of literacy." But the institutions that do the checking are also under direct political and financial attack right now, and that is part of the picture, not background to it.

**2. Whatever these systems are, they are made from us.**
We disagree, sometimes sharply, about whether large models understand anything and whether "intelligence" is the right word. We agree that they are built from the accumulated work of human writers, researchers, editors, and volunteers, and that they answer to whoever owns them. We will speak about them precisely. We will describe what a system does, how often it fails, and how it fails, rather than borrowing authority from words like "knows," "reasons," or "understands" when making claims about reliability. We will not relabel systematic error as a charming quirk. We will publish a short shared glossary with the roadmap. Judge these tools as you would any powerful technology: on evidence, on fitness for the task, and on who controls them.

> *Dissent — Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton, Peter Salib:* The capabilities are real and growing. To treat these systems as merely "statistical" is itself an imprecise claim, and not the neutral default this section presents it as. Governance built on the assumption that these are "just" text predictors will be overtaken by events. Agüera y Arcas and Bratton add that calling this intelligence is not anthropomorphism.

**3. Writing is now cheaper than reading.**
Fluent, well-referenced, scholarly-shaped text now costs almost nothing to produce, while checking it costs what it always did. Every signal of trust that rested on the effort of production is failing: polish, volume, citation counts, the recommendation letter, the look of peer review. We will not mourn fluency as a gatekeeper. It always rewarded the right schooling and native English as much as good ideas. But we have to replace it with signals that are expensive to fake: where a claim came from, who stands behind it, what they risk if it is wrong, whether its data and methods can be checked, and how it has fared over time. Trust will come from context, not content. We oppose using "AI detectors" to police authors. They do not work, and they fall hardest on non-native speakers.

> *Dissent — Leslie Chan:* New trust signals, identifiers, and standards tend to become compliance regimes, and scholarship that does not fit them becomes invisible. Most of that scholarship is from the Global South. The aim should be to change the evaluation regime, not to make more work legible to it.

**4. Authority is earned in public, by people who can be corrected.**
No one checks everything themselves; knowledge lives in communities. The question is which cues reliably point to real knowledge behind a claim. Repeating a claim is not independent evidence, and the trappings of scholarship can be faked. Earned authority is corrigible: it shows its sources, has something at stake, and corrects itself in the open. A machine has no stake in being right. It can route people to authority but cannot be one. So we ask for **no answers from nowhere**: tools that synthesize knowledge must show named, checkable sources, including where those sources disagree.

Human judgment is not a clean gold standard either. It carries bias, and people working alongside a biased system adopt its bias. Judgment needs structure: blinding to cues that should not matter, conflict-of-interest rules, audits, and diverse reviewers. Better signals will not, on their own, win back people who have stopped trusting the institutions that send them. Trust also depends on belonging and on people feeling they have some agency.

**5. Human judgment is the scarce ingredient. Pay for it.**
Reviewers, editors, librarians, curators, data stewards, Wikipedians, open-source maintainers, and research-integrity sleuths do the work that every AI system depends on, most of it unpaid and unseen. They are the real "annotators" of human knowledge. That role belongs to people and communities first. Publishers and platforms share it only to the extent that they earn it, by correcting the record promptly and in the open. The labor should be counted, credited, and paid.

> *Dissent — Sherry Turkle:* "Keeping humans in the loop" already assumes the machine belongs in the loop. In some domains, including therapy, intimacy, and children's companionship, AI should not be present at all.
>
> *Dissent — David Weinberger:* Humans sometimes degrade the loop. What matters is that people stay in a genuine dialogue with these systems, not that a human signs off at a checkpoint.

**6. Free to read is not free to strip.**
The scholarly record, Wikipedia, public media, and open data are being used as free raw material. Anyone who builds on them should attribute what they use, report how they use it, and pay to sustain it, meaning the infrastructure and the people behind it as well as the content. The content can be free; the servers, the editors, and the reviewers are not. The people who make this knowledge should bargain collectively, not one by one. Payment should follow actual use. One-off bulk licensing deals entrench the largest publishers and rarely reach the people who did the work. The present arrangement, in which AI firms return some value as subsidized services they can withdraw at any time, is a quasi-enclosure. It is not a settlement.

> *Dissent — Jason Priem, Selena Deckelmann:* Reciprocity, yes; permission, no. Openly licensed knowledge must stay open to every use, machine use included. Charge for services and infrastructure, and never put gates on the knowledge itself. A permission regime would re-enclose the record and hand control to whoever already holds the most rights.
>
> *Dissent — Amy Brand:* This does not go far enough. Use of scholars' work to train commercial models should be opt-in by default, attributed, and renegotiable, at least until the long-term effects are understood. Retrieval that cites its sources is preferable to training that absorbs them without attribution.
>
> *Dissent — Benjamin Bratton:* Royalty and copyright schemes are the wrong tool. The public value of the record is realized by spreading it into models and to everyone. Any dividend should come through AI, for example a public wealth fund, not by walling the record off.

**7. Open is necessary, not sufficient.**
Knowledge infrastructure must be open in ways that guarantee exit: open data, open code, and governance that no single company, funder, or country can capture. Labels such as "nonprofit," "shared," or "community" guarantee none of that on their own. Open, machine-readable records mean that no one has to depend on a single AI vendor. Open-weight and public models do the same for the models themselves. But availability is not access, and access is not understanding. Knowledge that exists only in English, only online, only behind a login, or only in a form experts can use is not yet shared. Our goal is knowledge that people everywhere can find, read in their own language, question, and use.

**8. The quiet risk is sameness.**
The loud risk is a flood of junk. The quieter one is narrowing. Early evidence suggests AI tools raise individual scientists' output while shrinking the range of questions science as a whole asks. Models converge on the typical, filter out the surprising, rarely propose that nothing is there, and increasingly learn from their own output. Negative results, dead ends, and minority views are exactly what these systems cannot supply and what the record most lacks. We will build for divergence: tools that show disagreement rather than one balanced-sounding answer, funding for the unfashionable, and a real home for null results. Where errors cannot be caught quickly, as in much of science, the checks have to be built before the output is trusted.

**9. Competence before reliance.**
Tools that do our thinking for us can make us better thinkers or worse ones. What decides it is whether we learned the skill first, and whether we check the output and rebuild the reasoning or simply accept it. Close reading, sustained argument, and the ability to notice when something is wrong are the capacities that make human judgment worth anything. Education, and research training in particular, should build them before handing people the tools.

> *Dissent — Steven Sloman:* Individuals never held most of their knowledge; ignorance is our natural state, and relying on others is how human cognition works. The real dangers are the illusion that a tool's access is our own understanding, and the loss of the oversight skills needed when systems fail. Outsourcing by itself is not the danger.

**10. Build, and hold power to account.**
We are not calling for refusal, and we do not want to scold the hundreds of millions of people who already find these tools useful. We are calling for better alternatives, built by communities, libraries, universities, and public institutions, that people can choose on the merits. The black box that matters most is often the business model and the people running it. Transparency should cover money flows, data provenance, and failure modes, not only model weights. Responsibility for what an AI system does must rest with people and organizations that can be held to account.

> *Dissent — Adam Becker:* Large language models should play no part in scholarly evaluation or synthesis. They cannot distinguish true from false, and they were built on uncompensated work, heavy energy use, and exploited moderation labor. There is no evidence they accelerate science, and a manifesto that promises to "build" with them concedes too much.
>
> *Dissent — Peter Salib:* Accountability that runs only to human principals will fail once AI agents act at scale. The law may need to give such agents a regulated legal identity, and possibly limited legal rights, as a leash and not as a gift.

## What we will do first

Session 5 named three interventions as the most important. The full roadmap, with responsible actors and timelines, will follow.

1. **Stop counting papers.** Funders and universities should stop using publication and citation counts in hiring, promotion, and funding, and should say so in policy rather than only in pledges. They should reward sharing data, reviewing, and correcting the record instead. This would remove the demand that paper mills and machine-generated papers now exploit.

2. **A reciprocity compact for the knowledge commons.** AI developers that draw on the scholarly record, Wikipedia, and public media should report use in auditable, event-level form and pay into the shared infrastructure and communities that maintain those sources: identifiers and metadata, retraction data, open indexes, encyclopedias, public-interest newsrooms. The terms should be negotiated collectively by the communities that produce the knowledge.

3. **An open provenance and correction layer.** Every tool that summarizes or synthesizes the literature should show where each claim came from, whether the source has been corrected or retracted, and what contradicts it. That requires open retraction and integrity data, a funded science of verification, and a public home for negative results. Correction should be routine, fast, and paid: bounties for sleuths, retractions within weeks, and investigation reports made public by default.

## What we do not know

We could not answer these questions, and we want them funded rather than argued over. Is individual cognitive atrophy real at the scale of whole populations, or are we repeating Socrates' complaint about writing? Can automated review recognize a genuinely new idea? What should "open" mean when the largest reader is a machine? Who pays for the commons once the traffic that funded it disappears? How much of the published record is unreliable?

## Who was not in the room

This conversation was dominated by North America and by English speakers. The next one needs scholars and knowledge holders from the Global South and from Indigenous communities; students, teachers, and early-career researchers; the volunteers and sleuths who maintain the commons; economists and labor organizers; disabled people; and the funders and policymakers who can actually act on this.

What we are asking for is modest, and much of it is overdue. We are asking that knowledge be treated as something people make, maintain, and pay for, and not as a resource found lying on the ground.
