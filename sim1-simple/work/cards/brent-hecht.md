# Brent Hecht — position card

## 1. Role at summit + bio

**Session 5, "Interventions and Futures"** (with Bilder, Shaub, Dash, Oransky). He took the seat an earlier draft gave Clara Collier. His attendance is confirmed; the `attendance: unconfirmed` flags in the item files are stale (`brent-hecht/INDEX.md`).

Hecht is **Partner, Director of Applied Science at Microsoft** (Experiences & Devices, 2023–present; Director 2019–23). He leads research on AI in productivity products ("Copilot is a lot of what we do these days") and co-edits Microsoft's *New Future of Work* reports (2023, 2024, 2025). He is also an Affiliate Associate Professor at Northwestern, where he leads the People, Space, and Algorithms group. He was a founding member of the ACM FAccT executive committee and was named an ACM Distinguished Member in 2025 (`by/2025--brent-hecht-curriculum-vitae.md`).

His research runs from Wikipedia's cultural biases, which he describes as helping to uncover "algorithmic bias," to how much search engines and AI depend on Wikipedia and other user-generated content. From there he developed, with his student **Nick Vincent**, "data leverage," "data strikes," "data dividends" and "data labor." In 2018 he led the ACM Future of Computing Academy proposal that peer reviewers require papers to disclose negative impacts. He will speak as a responsible-AI insider at a major AI company who openly calls the current LLM–content relationship unsustainable.

**Check requested by the brief:** the corpus does **not** show him as a coauthor of the 2025 Microsoft/CMU CHI paper on generative AI and critical thinking. His CV (2025) does not list it. Do not attribute it to him. His documented statements on critical thinking come from the *New Future of Work Report 2023*, which he co-edited (see below).

## 2. Core recent positions

- **The LLM paradigm undermines its own supply chain.** "The dominant LLM paradigm threatens Wikipedia, large portions of the content ecosystem, and ultimately itself." (`brent-hecht/by/2024--can-wikipedia-come-to-ais-rescue-again-keynote-slides.md`, 2024)
- **A new "grand bargain" with content producers is needed**, and "Scientific papers" are named as one of the supply categories. The old bargain rested on information asymmetry and an implicit non-compete, and both are gone. "We need a new Grand Bargain with content producers." (same, 2024)
- **The "paradox of reuse" and "doom loop" now apply to everyone.** "the paradox of reuse in twenty fifteen with Wikipedia are now the world's problems." (`brent-hecht/av/2024--keynote-presentation-brent-hecht-can-wikipedia-come-to-ais-r.md`, 2024, ASR transcript)
- **Data strikes are more or less guaranteed.** Either producers win concessions, or they go under. For "Professional Content (e.g. Books, Journalism, Science)," the losing outcome is that "Content producers go out of business and are unable to produce content." (keynote slides, 2024)
- **The public has leverage.** "data leverage refers to influence that members of the public have over tech companies because important computing technologies rely on the public's data contributions." (`brent-hecht/by/2021--data-leverage.md`, 2021) He lists four kinds of leverage: legal, policy, reputational and data (strikes plus "conscious data contribution") (slides, 2024).
- **One simple policy fix could reorder the field.** "[Clarify] that 'fair use' under copyright law does not allow for training a model on content without the content owner's consent, at least for commercial purposes." (slides, 2024, quoting a Vincent & Li op-ed approvingly)
- **AI faces a legitimacy crisis, with the English Enclosures as the analogy.** "What happens if the dominant technology has such a wide mismatch between value accretion and value creation?" (slides, 2024). He also cites a risk of "data colonialism" for low-resource languages.
- **"Open" is a tactic, not a value.** "Some things that are values of the community look more like tactics through an AI lens, in particular 'open'". On the commons: "the lumber mills that are closest to the forest… right now, those lumber mills are companies like mine" (slides and transcript, 2024).
- **Provenance and citation are the scholarly values AI should be pushed toward.** "Current LLM paradigm does not prioritize citation/provenance, but there are technical paths forward". Non-parametric, just-in-time and per-use models show "The current LLM paradigm is not the only one" (slides, 2024).
- **Peer review is the gatekeeping lever for societal impact.** "because peer reviewers are the gatekeepers to all scholarly computer-science research, we're talking about how gatekeepers open the gate." (`brent-hecht/by/2018--nature-qa-ethics-of-computer-science.md`, 2018). The field's rosy framing is "a serious and embarrassing intellectual lapse" (`brent-hecht/by/2021--it-s-time-do-something-mitigating-negative-impacts.md`).
- **Generative AI is a general-purpose technology, and people should augment rather than replace.** "betting long on human labor has been a good bet for the last 300 years". He also criticizes OpenAI's goal of automating all work: "a goal that we should be discussing more as a populace" (`brent-hecht/av/2024--ai-myths-with-brent-hecht-microsoft.md`, 2024, ASR).
- **Critical thinking and metacognition should be handled through design, not abstention** (co-edited NFW 2023). "Reconceptualizing AI systems as 'provocateurs' in addition to 'assistants' can promote critical thinking in knowledge work". Also: "Overreliance on AI leads to poorer performance than either the human or the AI acting alone" and "Generative AI demands greater metacognition from users but also has potential to support it" (`brent-hecht/by/2023--microsoft-new-future-of-work-report-2023.md`).
- **Create the future rather than predict it.** "instead of trying to predict the future, our energies are better spent trying to create a future that we want." Plan for low, medium and high disruption (`2024--ai-myths…`, ASR).

## 3. Relation to the concept note

- **Strongly endorses the infrastructure and unpaid-labor paragraph.** The note says the scholarly record is treated "as a free input rather than something costly to produce and maintain," and asks "who pays to sustain the labor." That is his research program. He measured the monetary value of Reddit moderators' volunteer work (`by/2022--measuring-monetary-value-online-volunteer-work.md`), and his slides argue the falling labor share of revenue is partly "because we're just not paying most of our labor, e.g. done by our quality and relevance assessors, reviewers."
- **Endorses and extends "ultimate annotators."** He already puts "Scientific papers" in the content-producer supply chain and says a trusted community gains leverage "if acting as a community, partnering with other open data communities." He would turn the annotator question into leverage and bargaining, not just recognition.
- **Offers an economic version of "model collapse."** His doom loop is about human contribution drying up: AI reduces visits, which reduces participation, which removes the content AI needs. He treats synthetic data as only "a partial solution" (keynote Q&A). He would welcome the note's "vicious circle" and stress the supply side of it.
- **Would resist the note's rhetorical temperature (inference from role and stated theory of change).** His stated aim is the "intersection" of "Stuff that is great for the world" and "Stuff that is great for Microsoft," and he frames reputational leverage as "We all want trust… lots of opportunities for win-wins." He is unlikely to sign on to "Bond villains" language or blanket charges of surveillance capitalism against the industry.
- **Pushes back on "keep them open" as an unexamined good.** He asks what "open" is for in the AI era and questions whether maximal openness, like the Internet Archive's lending, serves long-term knowledge production (keynote Q&A, 2024).
- **Partly resists the "misnomer" and deflationary framing.** He reports 25–50% productivity gains in lab studies and RCT evidence, and treats GenAI as a general-purpose technology like electricity. He agrees with the note's skepticism of hype and AGI timelines ("very famous computer scientists making very, very inaccurate arguments").
- **Endorses "human judgment is scarce" and the atrophy worry**, but his fix is designed "appropriate reliance" and AI-as-provocateur, not keeping humans away from AI.

## 4. Predicted contributions in Session 5

**Interventions he would push hardest:**
1. **A new grand bargain for the scholarly record.** Consent-based terms for AI training and retrieval on scholarly content, with money flowing back to producers and maintainers. It would be backed by collective action across scholarly, Wikipedia and open-data communities, and by a simple policy ask (consent for commercial training under fair use).
2. **Provenance-first AI.** Scholars and publishers would use their leverage to push AI toward retrieval and attribution paradigms that "attribute and pay for that content" (keynote transcript). Citation and verifiability become the market signal.
3. **Using peer review as the lever.** He would extend his 2018/2021 proposal: reviewers require disclosure of societal impacts and, by extension, of AI's role. He would also advocate "provocateur" AI designs in scholarly workflows that protect critical thinking.

**Open questions:** Can the economics work? He says "I need some help… on the economics front." What should "open" mean now? How can collective data strikes survive antitrust law? Can a market "still work" under the mismatch between who creates value and who captures it? How do we avoid data colonialism when building resources for low-resource languages? How do we measure the value that volunteers and reviewers contribute?

**Who's missing** (grounded):
- economists (keynote transcript)
- labor unions and labor-movement scholars ("You have to be a student of the labor movement")
- social scientists, whom he says computer science must engage (`2018--nature-qa…`)
- legal scholars on labor law, contract law and unjust enrichment (slides)
- the volunteer communities themselves: Wikipedians, OpenStreetMap contributors, Reddit moderators, Stack Overflow users
- speakers of under-resourced languages

## 5. Likely dissents to record

- **On openness:** he may dissent from any plank that treats maximal openness as a good in itself without a sustaining economic model. Basis: keynote Q&A on the Internet Archive; slides, "values vs. tactics."
- **On blanket anti-industry or "no AI" language:** "throwing out everything and saying we're not gonna use computer science anymore, that's not gonna be… the optimal solution" (`brent-hecht/av/2025--jo-guldi-and-brent-hecht-maps-computers-and-other-abstractio.md`; that recording's date is uncertain and may predate 2025). He will probably want nuance, not a formal dissent. **Medium confidence.**

## 6. Tensions and alliances

- **Nick Vincent:** closest ally. Vincent was his PhD student and coauthored the whole data-leverage line. Expect them to reinforce each other across sessions.
- **Selena Deckelmann (Wikimedia):** natural ally. His 2024 keynote was addressed to the Wikimedia research community, urging it to use its leverage, and it praised the Wikimedia Enterprise feed as resources flowing back from AI.
- **Anil Dash:** they agree on the Wikipedia doom loop (`anil-dash/by/2026--wikipedia-at-25.md`). They diverge on the Internet Archive and "open," and on posture toward "Big AI," which Dash attacks and where Hecht works (`anil-dash/by/2026--endgame-open-web.md`).
- **Ivan Oransky:** they would clash over how much peer review can carry. Hecht wants reviewers to take on societal-impact assessment. Oransky argues review is already "peer-review light" and unpaid, with a volume that "cannot possibly be met by qualified people" (`ivan-oransky/by/2024--freakonomics-can-academic-fraud-be-stopped.md`). They could agree on paying and recognizing reviewers.
