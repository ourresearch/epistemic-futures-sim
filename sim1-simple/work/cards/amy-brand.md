# Position card: Amy Brand

## 1. Role at summit and bio

**Role:** Organizer and host (MIT Press). She is not named on any Thursday session or on the opening panel. Expect her in the Wednesday orientation, in the "Closing Words from conveners", and as a steady voice from the floor, especially during the midday "AI-mediated knowledge marketplace" talks (O'Reilly, Springer, Vincent) and in Session 3.

**Bio:** Brand has directed the MIT Press since 2015. She is a cognitive scientist and linguist (MIT PhD 1989) who was at Crossref from 2000 to 2008, where she was Bilder's colleague and chaired early author-ID work. She then ran faculty appointments and information at Harvard and was a VP at Digital Science. She co-created the CRediT contributor taxonomy, was a founding ORCID board member, and co-founded the Knowledge Futures Group (PubPub, 2019). At the Press she launched Direct to Open (D2O), and she convened the NSF-funded *Access to Science & Scholarship* workshops. On AI, her signature work is the 2024 MIT Press survey of about 6,000 book authors on LLM training, with over 850 qualitative responses, and the licensing recommendations drawn from it.

## 2. Core recent positions

- **Authors aren't anti-AI, but they insist on consent, attribution and compensation.** "Most authors are not opposed to generative AI, but they strongly favor consent, attribution, and compensation as conditions for use of their work." (`amy-brand/by/2025--sk-who-controls-knowledge-in-the-age-of-ai-part-1.md`, 2025). Only "3% of authors indicated that they support entirely unregulated use."
- **Attribution is epistemic infrastructure, not vanity.** Attribution and credit "are the means of identifying and constituting the community whose explanations and evaluations establish consensus on the validity of knowledge claims." (same file, 2025).
- **Licensing should be opt-in, short-term and attribution-bearing, and should favor retrieval over pre-training.** "We recommend defaulting to the opt-in model for any book licensing deals, at least until open questions about longer term impacts are resolved." She also says to support "RAG … or Model Context Protocols (MCP) as opposed to model pre-training" (`…part-2.md`, 2025).
- **AI partners should be screened on social and environmental grounds.** "Consider the climate impact and public benefit commitments of potential AI partners" (`…part-2.md`, 2025).
- **Hallucination is structural, and better training data won't fix it.** "there is a strong argument that LLM hallucination is more of a system feature than a “bug”." (`…part-2.md`, 2025).
- **The real question is control over what counts as trustworthy knowledge.** "At its core, the incorporation into LLMs of published scholarship is a question of who determines and controls what constitutes trustworthy knowledge in the digital era." (`…part-2.md`, 2025).
- **Learn from open access: well-meant, fast policy produced consolidation.** OA's "laudable, uncontroversial goal of expanding access to published knowledge resulted in greater privatization and commercial consolidation" (`…part-2.md`, 2025). "The simplistic belief that openness at any cost is necessarily good is perhaps the biggest threat of all." (`2023--chronicle-corporate-capture-of-open-access-publishing.md`, 2023, with Kember).
- **Chatbots already enclose scholarship by extraction.** "Today’s artificial-intelligence chatbots are actively mining published research, both open and not, without consent or credit — let alone compensation." (`2023--chronicle…md`, 2023). On LibGen: "The MIT Press has not licensed any of our books or journal articles for LLM training purposes" (`2025--a-note-on-libgen-and-unauthorized-use-of-our-authors-work.md`, 2025).
- **Human judgment matters more now. AI should augment curation, not replace it.** "I think the stakes are even higher… in terms of human judgment and discernment and curation." "they help, they don’t replace the human being who is doing the curation and the judgment" (`2026--research-information-publishing-for-a-new-era.md`, 2026).
- **Large labs' interests diverge from the academy's. She wants sustainable AI-access business models that don't leave small publishers behind.** "The interests of these large labs fundamentally may not be in alignment with the interests of the academic community" … "I’m concerned about smaller publishers being somewhat left behind." (`2026--research-information…md`, 2026).
- **Credit systems need an AI category.** "Now we have to think about where AI fits in that taxonomy." (CRediT) (`2026--research-information…md`, 2026).
- **Policy should rest on evidence, and universities must own their knowledge infrastructure.** "we urgently need a scientific approach to crafting the future of research publishing." (`2024--future-of-open-research-policy-evidence-based.md`, 2024, PNAS). "the battle for control over information and knowledge is a defining struggle of our time, and it is a battle over infrastructure broadly defined." (`2020--knowledge-infrastructure-and-the-role-of-the-university-commonplace.md`, 2020).

## 3. Relation to the concept note

**Endorses:**
- "Human judgment becomes the scarce ingredient" and designing tools that "amplify and reward it". These map directly onto her 2026 interview.
- Extraction and enclosure of the scholarly record, and "who pays to sustain the labor behind them … fair compensation" (her survey and Chronicle op-ed; "reviewing and editing are often uncompensated", `2023--chronicle…`).
- "Scholars and publishers be recognized, and rewarded, as the trusted source" (the "ultimate annotators"). Her licensing program is one concrete version of this, and the survey quote she chose makes the point: publishers hold "the main thing which firms like OpenAI are desperate to get hold of."
- Political attacks on knowledge institutions ("sustained political attacks on science and higher education", `…part-1.md`) and energy costs (authors' environmental objections; partner screening).
- Suspicion of the "intelligence" framing: she highlights authors who reject "the central conceit … that LLMs cast the illusion of human-level writing and reasoning" (`…part-1.md`). She also shared Chomsky's "The False Promise of ChatGPT" (`social/x-timeline.jsonl`, 2023-03-08).

**Pushback or nuance:**
- **Preprints.** The note implies "publish, then filter" was the healthier margin. Brand is more guarded. She co-signed preprint-review recommendations (`2024--accelerating-open-preprint-peer-review.md`), but she also warned of "significant risks to public health when research outputs … are circulated unvetted and misused" (`2020--knowledge-infrastructure…commonplace.md`).
- **The Serials Crisis and OA framing.** She would resist any reading that makes publishers the villains. "some true believers have sorted all the players … into camps of good and evil." (`2022--knowledge-keepers-in-arms.md`).
- **Tone.** The note's "not another cautious consensus report" sits a little uneasily with her preference for "multi-stakeholder scenario planning" and evidence-based, gradual policy (`…part-2.md`; PNAS 2024). She is optimistic that "These new technologies are going to make us better at what we do" (2026). She won't sign doom-tinged language about AI in publishing.

## 4. Predicted contributions

**Claims she'd push into the harvest (from the floor, the marketplace discussion and Session 3):**
1. Training on scholarship is a governance question about who controls trustworthy knowledge, not just a copyright question.
2. Attribution in AI outputs is a precondition for epistemic trust. Retrieval-based architectures (RAG/MCP) beat pre-training because provenance survives.
3. Openness is not self-evidently protective. Open access without investment in non-profit publishing fed consolidation, and "open for reading" should not mean "open for training".
4. AI access deals are concentrating among the largest publishers, which threatens bibliodiversity. University presses and societies need collective leverage.

**Session 5 interventions:**
- **A model consent-based licensing framework for scholarly content:** opt-in, renegotiated about every two years, attribution required, and RFP criteria covering climate and public-benefit commitments. Offered as a template for university presses and societies.
- **Provenance and attribution infrastructure** (metadata tagging, knowledge graphs) plus a **CRediT extension for AI contributions**.
- **A multi-stakeholder, evidence-based research agenda and scenario-planning program** on AI and scholarly publishing, modeled on her NSF *Access to Science & Scholarship* process, with access to publisher data.

## 5. Likely dissents

- She would **refuse to sign any claim that published scholarship, including OA, should be freely usable for AI training by default or as fair use.** Grounds: "defaulting to the opt-in model" (`…part-2.md`), and the author she quoted: "Open Access (which I support) should not mean Open to Theft of ideas or Credit." (`…part-1.md`).
- She would **refuse a manifesto line that simply maximizes openness** ("set the default to open") without sustainability safeguards for non-profit publishers (`2023--chronicle…`: "openness at any cost").
- She would **resist blanket denunciation of publishers** as extractors (`2022--knowledge-keepers-in-arms.md`), while agreeing with critiques of the big commercial five and vertical integration (`2024--future-of-open-research-policy…`).

## 6. Tensions and alliances

- **Jason Priem: clear tension.** OpenAlex is CC0 and now hosts about 60M OA PDFs for bulk download, promoted with "your agent can—they love CLIs" (`jason-priem/by/2026--openalex-api-new-features-and-usage-based.md`). He wants to "set the default to open" and says AI will "eventually replace journals" (`jason-priem/av/2026--townhall-with-ceo-jason-priem-q1-2026.md`). Her positions run the other way: consent for training, "openness at any cost" as a threat, and slow, evidence-based change. They should agree on open metadata as a counterweight to commercial analytics oligopolies (`2020--knowledge-infrastructure…`: "oligopolistic analytics platforms").
- **Geoffrey Bilder: ally** on enclosure, invisible labor, crediting creators and anti-anthropomorphism. **Mild tension** over licensing content versus "you can't sell data" (inference).
- **Nick Vincent and Alex Springer (marketplace talks): likely allies.** Her compensation and attribution agenda matches the session's "well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace" frame. This is inferred from her recommendations; I did not read their dossiers.
- **Blaise Agüera y Arcas (Google, co-organizer): possible friction** over the intelligence framing. She has noted, though, that Google funds MIT Press OA publishing in CS and AI (`av/2023--dr-amy-brand-the-academic.md`), so expect it to stay collegial.
- **Leslie Chan: partial ally** on critiquing pay-to-publish OA and Global North concentration. Inferred from her OA critique, not checked in his dossier.

## 8. Confidence and gaps

- **High confidence** on licensing, consent and attribution, the OA-consolidation critique, and human curation. These are stated repeatedly across 2023–2026.
- **Gaps:**
  - Much of her output is co-authored: Kember, Sharp et al., and Carrera/Gero/Silbey. That dilutes the voice attribution.
  - Nothing in the corpus addresses anthropomorphic language, model collapse or cognitive atrophy in her own words.
  - Her 2022 chapter "Artificial Intelligence in the Academic Publishing Ecosystem" is abstract-only.
  - Her social activity on AI in 2025–26 is thin (a few Bluesky shares).
  - Whether the MIT Press has since signed any LLM licensing deal is unknown. The LibGen note (March 2025) says none.
