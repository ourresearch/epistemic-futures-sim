# Ivan Oransky — position card

## 1. Role at summit + bio

**Session 5, "Interventions and Futures"** (with Bilder, Shaub, Dash, Hecht). He will be the room's authority on how the formally vetted literature fails.

Oransky is an M.D. who never practiced; he has been a science and medical journalist for about 25 years (Reuters Health, Scientific American, MedPage Today). In 2010 he co-founded **Retraction Watch** with Adam Marcus, and later the Center for Scientific Integrity. Its database of more than 59,000 retractions became fully open after **Crossref** acquired it in 2023. He is **editor-in-chief of *The Transmitter*** (Simons Foundation), Distinguished Journalist in Residence at NYU, and a volunteer board member of the **PubPeer Foundation**. He co-writes STAT's "Watchdogs" column. In 2025 he received the Council of Science Editors' highest award (`ivan-oransky/by/2025--rw-cse-award-acceptance-speech.md`; `by/2025--brownpoliticalreview-lifting-the-veil-interview.md`). He describes his Retraction Watch work as unpaid volunteer work (`av/2025--retractions-whistleblowers-and-the-quest-for-scientific-trut.md`).

## 2. Core recent positions

- **Worry about AI and paper mills distracts from the root cause, which is incentives.** "Obvious but apparently unpopular take: They're just a distraction from a symptom of publish-or-perish incentives in academia coupled with publishers' insatiable need for volume." (`ivan-oransky/social/x-timeline.jsonl`, 2023-02-07)
- **Funding detection instead of reform is buying flood insurance.** "Investing in ChatGPT detection… instead of addressing the incentives for relentless growth in scientific publishing is like buying more flood insurance and sump pumps…" (`social/x-timeline.jsonl`, 2023-07-11)
- **Watch out for fixation on new technology.** "we all get fixated on new technology because we're humans and we like shiny objects. That actually makes solving the bigger picture problem much more difficult." If incentives changed, "maybe there wouldn't be the need to… fraudulently use these techniques, including ChatGPT." (`by/2025--brownpoliticalreview-lifting-the-veil-interview.md`, 2025)
- **AI won't solve fraud detection; go upstream.** "AI is not the answer to finding research fraud and misconduct… The solution is to go way upstream again and actually look at the demand." He compares it to building sewage plants at the river mouth versus stopping pollution upstream (`av/2025--retractions-whistleblowers…`, 2025, ASR).
- **He still sees AI as an unusually severe threat.** "The rapid encroachment of artificial intelligence into the production of journal articles poses perhaps the largest threat yet to the integrity of scientific research. Given recent history, we have no reason to be optimistic…" (`by/2025--jlme-universities-misconduct-reports-public.md`, 2025). In his view AI is an accelerant, not the cause.
- **Stop evaluating people by paper and citation counts.** As a hypothetical NIH director: "We are no longer going to allow people… to judge grant applications and create priorities based on publishing and how many times things have been cited" (BPR 2025). "A good place to start is by deflating the importance of citations in the promotion, funding, and hiring of scientists." He adds that "any metric really can be gamed" (`by/2023--bmj-bibliometrics-rankings-unreliable-science.md`).
- **Publisher business models drive volume.** "Publishers earn more from publishing more. It's a volume play." (`by/2024--freakonomics-can-academic-fraud-be-stopped.md`, 2024)
- **Peer review is overloaded, unpaid and thinned out.** The required review hours produce "a number that cannot possibly be met by qualified people… none of them are paid… it's become really peer-review light" (Freakonomics 2024). In 2016 he argued for "paying reviewers for their time" (`by/2016--stat-is-the-peer-review-system-sustainable.md`).
- **Retractions are far too few.** The rate is about 0.2%, and he thinks it should be about 2% ("one in 50"). Sleuths should be paid: "Sleuths should be compensated and given access to tools… Publishers could create a cash pool to pay them, similar to the 'bug bounties'…" (`by/2022--nature-retractions-increasing-not-enough.md`)
- **Transparency should be the default.** "We recommend universities universally make investigation reports available as a default" (JLME 2025). He wants an NCAA-style common standard across institutions and a stronger ORI with subpoena power (`by/2024--science-rooting-out-scientific-misconduct.md`).
- **Correction is part of science and should be normal.** "The paper is not sacred": post-publication review "should become part of the scientific record" (`by/2011--nature-the-paper-is-not-sacred.md`). "we need to normalize retractions" (`av/2025…`, ASR). Retraction data should flow into reference managers and submission workflows (Nature 2022).
- **Reform institutions; don't tear them down.** "I've never been a fan of destroying institutions because I don't think… that anything will be rebuilt, let alone something better." Scholarly integrity is being "weaponized" (BPR 2025; CSE 2025: "retractions are being weaponized… even science itself is under attack").

## 3. Relation to the concept note

- **Strongly endorses** "AI didn't create these problems," the point that fraud and irreproducibility "occurred largely inside the traditional system of peer review," and the note's "perverse incentives inside our own institutions." This is his thesis, and he will be the loudest voice in the room for "incentives, not AI."
- **Endorses "writing is cheaper than reading"** and the unpaid-labor paragraph, backed by his arithmetic on reviewer hours and the $4.5B estimate of their value (STAT 2016). He would redirect the focus: the flood is driven by **demand**, meaning publish-or-perish plus publishers' volume model, not by AI supply.
- **Pushes back on "AI… could also help us fix them" and "the disruption… may be precisely the opening we need."** He calls himself "not all that optimistic" and rejects a "technological utopian strain of thought" that says tools will "work itself out" (BPR 2025). Detection tools are "flood insurance." He would resist any manifesto plank that leans on AI to police AI.
- **Pushes back on scholars and publishers as trusted "ultimate annotators" before accountability.** Publishers "frequently act as if admitting to accepting flawed papers threatens their reputations and bottom lines" (Nature 2022). Journals are "invariably more interested in protecting their reputations… than in correcting the record" (`by/2023--guardian-far-more-scientific-fraud-than-admitted.md`). He would ask whether the record they curate can be trusted first. This is an inference from those statements; no direct comment on the "annotator" framing exists.
- **Would welcome the "AI detectors" critique**, since he sees detection as the wrong target. He might partly resist the celebration of fluency's debasement. He links "writing and critical thinking" and says "If you don't know how to do both of them, I don't see how you do anything but plagiarize" (BPR 2025). That is an atrophy concern that fits the note's cognitive-atrophy section.
- **The "redesign" language (inference):** his institutionalist line ("never been a fan of destroying institutions") makes him wary of "reinvention" language that ignores how fragile science is politically.

## 4. Predicted contributions in Session 5

**Interventions he would push hardest:**
1. **Decouple publication and citation counts from hiring, promotion and funding.** Funders are the lever (NIH), and signing DORA should come with enforcement ("very few walk the walk," BMJ 2023). He would reward data sharing and correcting the record instead.
2. **Fund correcting the record as infrastructure.** Pay sleuths (bug bounties). Aim for retractions within two months (per the UK Commons committee he testified to). Make misconduct reports public by default. Give ORI budget and subpoena power. Keep retraction data open (Crossref) and integrated into tools, reference managers and submission checks.
3. **Open the "black box" of peer review and pay or recognize reviewers.** Journals should disclose review practices (`by/2017--stat-peer-review-is-a-black-box-lets-open-it-up.md`); reviewers should be compensated (STAT 2016).

**Open questions:** What is the true misconduct rate? He puts about 2% as a floor. Can AI-generated or paper-mill content be detected at all, or is it an arms race? Will shareholder-driven publishers ever give up volume? How do we protect integrity work from political weaponization? How do we protect whistleblowers from lawsuits?

**Who's missing** (grounded):
- **sleuths and whistleblowers**, "most of them are not paid" (`av/2025…`)
- **researchers in high-pressure publish-or-perish systems**, such as China's cash-per-impact-factor bonuses (Freakonomics 2024) and the Vietnam integrity movement (`by/2024--rw-meet-the-founder-of-a-100-000-strong-facebook-group.md`)
- **funders**, especially NIH
- **university research-integrity officers and ORI**
- **accountability journalists.** He wants the "Venn diagram surrounding good science journalism and accountability journalism" to be "oval rather than a figure eight," and he now trains other reporters (BPR 2025). Hecht separately calls the press "the gatekeepers" for research that is never peer-reviewed (`brent-hecht/by/2018--nature-qa-ethics-of-computer-science.md`).

## 5. Likely dissents to record

- **Against any AI-detection or AI-policing intervention presented as a primary fix.** Basis: X 2023; av 2025, "AI is not the answer."
- **Against an optimistic "AI as opening" framing that underplays incentive reform.** Basis: BPR 2025, "not all that optimistic."
- **Possibly against endorsing publishers as trusted stewards without accountability commitments** such as retraction timeliness and transparency. Basis: Guardian 2023; Nature 2022.

## 6. Tensions and alliances

- **Geoffrey Bilder:** alliance. Bilder co-authored the 2023 Crossref announcement of the Retraction Watch acquisition (`geoffrey-bilder/by/2023--crossref-news-crossref-retraction-watch.md`), and Oransky credits Crossref with making the data open and sustainable (`av/2025…`). They share an agenda of open integrity metadata.
- **Brent Hecht:** tension over peer-review capacity. Hecht's proposal gives reviewers more to judge; Oransky says review is already "peer-review light" and unpaid. They can agree on paying reviewers and making review count.
- **Anil Dash:** both oppose hype, and "shiny objects" matches Dash's "normal technology." Oransky is more pessimistic about AI in publishing, and Dash more optimistic that alternatives can be built.
- **Organizers' framing (Bilder and Shaub as co-leads):** he will support the "what was already broken" axis and act as a check on techno-solutionist interventions.

## 7. Distinctive vocabulary

"publish or perish"; "a volume play"; "peer-review light"; "paper mills" ("scientific chop shops"); "sleuths"; "whack-a-mole"; "go way upstream" (the sewage metaphor); "shiny objects"; "plagiarism arms race"; "the most likely outcome for anyone who has committed misconduct is a long and fruitful career" (Freakonomics 2024); "normalize retractions"; "slow, opaque and inconsistent"; "Goodhart's law"; "bug bounties"; "sunlight is the best disinfectant"; "Now is not the time to fade" (CSE 2025); "the paper is not sacred"; "hijacked journals."

## 8. Confidence and gaps

**High confidence** on incentives, retraction and transparency positions, and on "AI is a symptom or distraction." Also high on "AI is a serious accelerant," given the JLME 2025 line. The two coexist in his writing.

**Gaps:**
- His 2024–26 X output in the corpus is sparse (5 posts in 2025–26), and the sharpest AI lines are from 2023.
- The WaPo 2024 "epidemic of scientific fakery" op-ed is not held.
- 83 of 104 STAT pieces are unfetched.
- No Transmitter editorial on AI was found.
- No direct statement on LLMs citing retracted papers or on AI-mediated synthesis. That is a natural extension of his "unknowing citation" work, but it is unverified.
