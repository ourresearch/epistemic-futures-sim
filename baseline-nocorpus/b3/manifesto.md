# Cheap to Write, Costly to Know: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*Epistemic Futures Summit, MIT, September 2026*

AI did not break human knowledge. The system that produces, vets, and preserves it was already strained before generative AI arrived. Incentives rewarded volume over rigor. Much of a publicly funded scholarly record sat behind paywalls. Peer review let fabrication and irreproducible findings through. Platforms hollowed out the business models that paid for journalism and publishing. What AI changes is the price of plausibility. Scholarly-shaped text, images, and data are now nearly free to produce, while checking them remains as slow and costly as ever. Writing has become cheaper than reading.

We are scholars, technologists, publishers, journalists, and stewards of open knowledge. We met to separate genuine disruption from old failures that are accelerating and from moral panic, and to say what we will build. This is not a consensus report. Where we disagreed, we say so.

## Principles

**1. Diagnose before prescribing: most of this crisis is old, and AI is its accelerant.**
Paper mills, citation gaming, the serials crisis, the collapse of local news, and the replication crisis all predate large language models. What is new is scale. Incentives that paid for volume now meet a machine that produces volume for free. Some responses are pure panic, and they do harm. The clearest example is the "AI detector," which falsely accuses non-native writers while catching little. Blaming AI alone lets our institutions off the hook for failures they built. Insisting nothing has changed ignores a shift in cost that makes those failures intolerable. Every intervention should state which of the three it addresses: something already broken, something breaking, or something AI now breaks faster.

**2. Describe these systems precisely: they are neither oracles nor mere parrots.**
Most of us found it useful to treat large models as cultural and social technologies. Like markets, bureaucracies, and libraries, they compress and recombine what enormous numbers of people have produced, and they make it usable at scale. That framing avoids two errors. One credits a chatbot with knowing and intending. The other dismisses real capabilities as autocomplete. Words like "knows," "reasons," and "hallucinates" smuggle in conclusions and should be used carefully wherever the stakes are high. Claims about capability need evidence, not marketing. An industry that spends billions on promotion, and can generate its own advocates on demand, should not set the vocabulary the rest of us use to judge it.

**3. Invest in verification, not production.**
If writing is cheaper than reading, the bottleneck is evaluation, and that is where money and prestige must move. Funders, publishers, and universities should pay for review, replication, curation, correction, and retraction as core infrastructure, not as volunteer afterthoughts. Corrections must propagate: a retracted paper should be flagged in citation indexes, discovery tools, and the AI systems that summarize the literature. AI can help where checks are fast and decisive, such as detecting duplicated images, verifying citations, screening statistics, and supporting the lateral reading that good fact-checkers do. It must not be the final judge where scientific feedback loops run for years. As automation grows, human judgment becomes the scarce ingredient.

**4. Stop paying for volume.**
"Publish or perish," impact factors, and output counts trained a generation to maximize quantity. That was corrosive before AI. It is now suicidal, because anything that can be counted can be machine-produced. Hiring, promotion, and grant committees should consider a small number of chosen works, read in full. They should explicitly credit shared data, code, replications, reviews, and public corrections of one's own errors. A workshop cannot undo fifty years of perverse incentives. But the flood of plausible, unvetted work gives institutions both a reason and a cover to finally change them. Funders hold the strongest lever and should use it first.

**5. Credibility must be earned through traceable process, not polish or pedigree.**
Fluency no longer signals effort, and we will not mourn it much. It always marked privilege as much as merit, and it excluded capable people who wrote in a second language. Better signals track the data, the methods, and the record. They record who did which part of the work, including what machines did. They record where the data came from, what was checked, by whom, and how errors were handled. Credentials and institutions still matter as accountable reputations with something to lose. Our own credibility judgments are biased, however, and new signals must be tested so they do not simply launder old prejudices. Public literacy should teach people to check context and sources, not to eyeball surface features.

**6. Keep the commons open, and require those who draw on it to give back.**
Wikipedia, open metadata, preprint servers, archives, and the scholarly record are being scraped at industrial scale. Nonprofits absorb the infrastructure costs while the traffic and credit that sustain them are diverted. The answer is not enclosure. Bilateral licensing deals between large publishers and AI firms threaten to privatize a record that was largely publicly funded and freely authored. We favor openness with reciprocity:

- attribution that survives synthesis;
- machine-readable provenance;
- collective rather than bilateral bargaining;
- maintenance contributions from the heaviest users.

An AI marketplace that routes value back to the people who create knowledge is better business as well as better ethics.

**7. Pay for the human labor that makes knowledge trustworthy.**
Reviewers, editors, curators, librarians, archivists, Wikipedians, fact-checkers, and data annotators do the work that makes knowledge reliable. That work is too often unpaid, underpaid, or invisible. The AI industry's own demand for human annotation shows that careful human judgment is valuable. Scholars and curators should be recognized as the source of that judgment, with credit, careers, and compensation to match. Recognition must not mean reducing them to piecework labelers for a few firms. The aim is to make stewardship a respected profession, not a new gig economy.

**8. Build tools that leave people more capable, and protect the diversity of inquiry.**
Some tools make us smarter even when we put them down. Others leave us helpless without them. AI tools should be judged by what their users can do afterward, not only by what they output. Reading closely, reasoning at length, and writing an argument from scratch are how people learn to think, and education must protect them. There is a collective risk too. Early evidence suggests AI-assisted science boosts individual careers while narrowing what science as a whole studies. Homogenized inquiry, like models trained on their own output, degrades the system from inside. To counter both, we should preserve a human-authored record that people and future systems can tell apart from machine output, and fund work away from the crowd.

**9. No single company or state should own the infrastructure of knowing.**
A handful of firms control the leading models, the clouds they run on, and increasingly the interfaces through which people find anything at all. Meanwhile, some governments are defunding public media, pressuring universities, and dismantling the watchdogs meant to hold power accountable. Accuracy is not the whole governance problem; power is. We call for:

- open and public-interest models alongside commercial ones;
- interoperable systems and portable data;
- open scholarly infrastructure;
- genuine sovereignty for communities and regions, especially in the Global South, over their own knowledge.

Pluralism is not inefficiency. It is how a knowledge system survives the failure of any one part.

## What We Commit To Do

- **Vocabulary.** Publish within a year a shared, plain-language glossary for describing AI systems and their use in research and journalism, and use it ourselves.
- **Provenance.** Adopt and extend contributor-role and provenance standards, building on CRediT, persistent identifiers, and open attribution protocols, to disclose who and what did each part of the work.
- **Incentives.** Press our institutions and funders to reward review, replication, data sharing, and correction, and to stop counting outputs.
- **Open infrastructure.** Support the open infrastructure we depend on, including Wikimedia, OpenAlex, preprint servers, and correction databases, with money, code, and advocacy. Oppose deals that enclose the publicly funded record.
- **Evidence.** Measure before we claim. Publish evidence on what AI tools do to learning, science, and trust, including negative results.
- **Inclusion.** Reconvene with those who were missing: researchers and stewards from the Global South, librarians, educators, students and early-career scholars, and data annotators.

## Noted Dissents

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas** rejects Principle 2's "cultural technology" framing. He argues it quietly settles an open empirical question. In his view, these models exhibit real, if unfamiliar, forms of intelligence and understanding. Telling institutions they are merely compressions of human text will leave them unprepared for what these systems can already do.

**Benjamin Bratton** objects to the manifesto's anthropocentrism. Knowledge has always been distributed across people, instruments, and infrastructures. Framing the task as "sustaining human knowledge" against machine encroachment misreads a planetary shift in how intelligence is organized. The question is how to compose human and artificial cognition well, not how to defend a boundary between them.

**Peter Salib** argues the governance principles treat AI purely as a tool. Increasingly autonomous agents will act within the knowledge economy, making claims, transacting, and causing harm. Legal frameworks for AI systems as actors, including liability and possibly rights, belong on this agenda now.

**Adam Becker** thinks the document still concedes too much to industry framing, including "opportunity" language borrowed from vendors. The evidence for scientific benefit is thin, while the costs in energy, labor, and fraud are concrete. The burden of proof belongs with the companies, and the manifesto should say so plainly.

**Leslie Chan** warns that compensation schemes and the "annotator" framing risk entrenching Northern publishers and platforms as toll collectors on knowledge. The priority should be epistemic justice and community governance, not new revenue streams for incumbents.

**Steven Sloman** considers the cognitive-atrophy worry overstated. Individuals have always known little on their own and relied on communities of knowledge. The real question is whether AI systems can become reliable, accountable members of those communities.

**Adrian Johns** doubts the historical arc invoked here. Trust in print took centuries of piracy, litigation, and conflict to build. It was not designed at meetings. New signals of credibility will emerge messily, and we should be humbler about engineering them.

**Ivan Oransky** is skeptical that publishers deserve the role of trusted curators. Much of the fraud he documents passed through their peer review. Profitable publishers should earn any new reward by fixing their record, not simply by holding it.

**Sherry Turkle** finds Principle 8 too mild. Chatbots that simulate empathy should be kept out of roles as children's tutors and companions. A system that pretends to care is not a neutral tool, and education should say so.
