# Writing Is Now Cheaper Than Reading: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*Epistemic Futures Summit, MIT, September 2026*

We came to MIT skeptical of AI's promoters, and in several cases skeptical of each other. We did not agree on what these systems are, how capable they will become, or whether the word "intelligence" belongs to them. We did agree on something more useful. The institutions that produce, vet, and preserve knowledge were strained long before generative AI. AI now acts on that strain faster than our institutions can respond. And the people and practices that make knowledge trustworthy are being consumed faster than they are being replenished. This is not a consensus report. It sets out the positions most of us would defend, the commitments we are prepared to act on, and, at the end, the places where some of us think the rest are wrong.

## Principles

**1. AI did not break the knowledge system. It is widening cracks that were already there.**

Before we prescribe, we must sort. Publish-or-perish, the serials crisis, irreproducible findings, and paper mills are decades old. The collapse of news business models, platform mediation, and political attacks on science and public media were already under way. What is genuinely new is scale: plausible, unvetted work now costs almost nothing to produce. Calling everything an "AI crisis" lets institutions off the hook for failures they built. Calling nothing new is complacency. Some alarm is plain moral panic, and we should test claims about harm as rigorously as claims about benefit. Each problem needs its own diagnosis before it gets a remedy.

**2. Describe what these systems do in the knowledge ecosystem, not what they "are."**

We did not settle whether large models think, and we do not need to. Like markets, bureaucracies, and libraries, they are social and cultural technologies. They aggregate and recombine human knowledge and reshape who finds it, who uses it, and who trusts it. Precision matters. Say "generated," "retrieved," "summarized," or "fabricated" when that is what happened. Distinguish demonstrated capability from the perpetual promise of what is five years away, and be alert to the fact that the industry's marketing budgets now write much of our vocabulary. A shared, plain-language glossary is a concrete deliverable, not a pedantic one.

**3. Evaluation is now the bottleneck. Fund it, and stop rewarding volume.**

Fluent, well-referenced text used to be a rough signal that someone had done the work. That signal is gone. Any institution that treats counts of papers, citations, or grants as a proxy for quality has built an attack surface, and paper mills are already exploiting it at industrial scale. Hiring, promotion, and funding should assess a few contributions in depth. They should give credit for data, code, replications, peer review, and corrections. Correction must also work: a retraction should propagate to citing papers, indexes, and the AI systems that ingested the original.

**4. Credibility should rest on inspectable process and track record, not polish or pedigree.**

No one knows much alone. We rely on communities of knowledge, and the practical skill is knowing whom to trust and why. Good signals can be checked: open data and methods, transparent review, disclosed use of AI, and a visible record of correcting errors. Readers need tools and habits for checking context, not just verdicts. The cheapening of fluency is also an opening. Polished prose was always partly a marker of privilege, and non-native writers stand to gain. We must not replace it with worse gates, such as discriminatory "AI detectors" or a retreat into credentialism.

**5. Provenance and attribution are infrastructure. Build them in, not bolted on.**

Citation, persistent identifiers, and imprints were humanity's last great harder-to-fake signals. We should extend them. That means machine-readable provenance for claims, datasets, and generated outputs, and AI answers that link to their sources, send readers and credit back to them, and let errors be traced and fixed. Attribution is more than courtesy. It is the plumbing through which correction, compensation, and accountability can flow. Standards bodies, publishers, and AI developers can build this now, using identifier systems that already work.

**6. The knowledge commons must be open and reciprocal: open to all, not free to strip-mine.**

Wikipedia, open repositories, bibliographic databases, and archives are the foundations AI is built on. Industrial-scale crawling now imposes real costs on the volunteers and nonprofits who maintain them. Paywalls are not the answer. Reciprocity is. Organizations that extract at scale should honor terms of use, return attribution and traffic, and contribute to maintenance. Communities of contributors should be able to bargain collectively. Governance must include the Global South and communities whose knowledge has long been taken without consent. Openness without sustenance collapses.

**7. The human labor behind trustworthy knowledge must be made visible and paid.**

Reviewers, editors, librarians, curators, Wikipedians, fact-checkers, data stewards, and journalists supply the judgment that everything else depends on. The emergence of paid annotation shows that the AI economy already knows careful human judgment is valuable. We accept that lesson but reject its reduction. Scholars are not a supply chain for training data. Their work should be recognized in career systems, and not only priced by model developers. Peer review and curation deserve formal credit. The people who do this work should not be the last to benefit from it.

**8. Keep knowledge plural. Monoculture is a nearer danger than collapse.**

Whether models degrade by training on their own output is still debated. Homogenization is already observable. AI-assisted research drifts toward popular topics and established methods, and a few models in a few languages increasingly mediate what billions of people encounter. Science advances through dissent, eccentricity, and neglected questions. We should sustain multiple models, including public and community-governed ones. We should also invest in non-English and underrepresented corpora, preserve clearly human-origin archives, and protect research that no ranking algorithm would surface.

**9. Concentrated control of epistemic infrastructure is a political problem, and it needs public answers.**

Whoever controls search, models, and the scholarly record shapes what societies can know. Even as that control concentrates in a few firms, some governments are stripping power from regulators and pressuring universities, libraries, and public media. Propaganda thrives where trust has collapsed and institutions are hollowed out. Markets alone will not correct this. We call for independent auditing of AI systems used in knowledge work, for publicly funded AI options, and for renewed defense of the public-interest institutions under attack.

**10. Human capacity is in scope: design tools that strengthen thinking rather than replace it.**

Cognitive atrophy is not a fixed fate, but it is a serious risk. Some tools leave us more capable when we put them down, and some leave us less. We should build and buy the first kind. Education must redesign assessment around reasoning, argument, and conversation rather than products that can be generated. In science, where feedback loops take years, human judgment becomes more valuable, not less, as surrounding work is automated. Success should be measured in learning and understanding, not engagement.

## What We Commit To Do

We commit to:

- **Publish a plain-language glossary** for describing what AI systems do in knowledge work, within six months.
- **Convene publishers, identifier organizations, and AI developers** to pilot an open provenance and attribution standard for generated outputs.
- **Draft a reciprocity compact** for commons institutions, setting out terms under which large-scale extraction returns attribution, traffic, and funding.
- **Advocate within our own universities, presses, and funders** for assessment that reads rather than counts, and that credits review, data, replication, and correction.
- **Circulate an open research agenda** to funders, covering homogenization, cognitive effects, provenance, and the economics of stewardship.
- **Report back publicly within a year** on what we have built, and on what failed.
- **Reconvene with the people this room lacked.** That means researchers and knowledge workers from the Global South, early-career scholars, students and teachers, annotators, librarians, and policymakers.

## Noted Dissents

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas** holds that Principle 2 dodges a real question. He believes these models already understand in meaningful senses. On his view, framing them only as social technologies understates what is happening and will leave institutions unprepared for systems that genuinely contribute ideas.

**Benjamin Bratton** rejects the humanist frame of "sustaining human knowledge." He argues that intelligence is becoming planetary and composite, and that insisting on keeping humans "in the loop" may preserve the wrong loop. For him, the task is designing new human-machine epistemic institutions, not defending old ones.

**Peter Salib** believes the manifesto underestimates how quickly capability is advancing. He expects AI systems to act as autonomous agents that conduct research and hold goals of their own. He argues that governance should plan now for their legal and institutional status rather than treat them as tools.

**Adam Becker** finds the document too conciliatory toward industry. He warns that reciprocity deals and co-designed standards with firms whose valuations depend on hype invite capture. He would demand independent evaluation first and state plainly that claims about artificial general intelligence are marketing.

**Jason Priem** warns that licensing and payment schemes for training could re-enclose the commons. He argues that scholarly metadata and open-access literature should remain freely machine-readable for any use, training included, and that commons institutions should be sustained by direct public and philanthropic funding.

**Leslie Chan** objects that the "annotator" and marketplace framing still commodifies scholarship. It treats knowledge, much of it from the Global South, as input to be priced. He points out that the room was overwhelmingly North American, and argues that sovereignty and self-governance, not compensation, should be the goal.

**Sherry Turkle** considers Principle 10 too accommodating. She notes that "complementary" design is a phrase the industry has already adopted. She argues that some developmental, educational, and relational spaces should be protected as AI-free, and that the burden of proof belongs on the tools.

**Adrian Johns** cautions against the reassuring historical arc in which crises reliably produce durable new institutions. Trust in print took more than a century to establish, amid piracy, conflict, and bitter disputes. Nothing guarantees that this transition will resolve well, or that it will resolve at all.
