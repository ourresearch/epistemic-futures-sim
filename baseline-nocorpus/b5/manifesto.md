# Cheap to Write, Costly to Trust: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

In September 2026, some three dozen scholars, technologists, publishers, journalists and open-knowledge stewards met at MIT. We asked how human knowledge can be sustained, curated and trusted now that AI systems produce, summarize and mediate so much of it. We did not agree on everything, and we did not try to. What follows is what most of us were willing to sign. The sharpest disagreements are recorded at the end.

Our starting point is simple. AI did not break the knowledge system. Much of it was already cracking: perverse incentives, captured infrastructure, overloaded reviewers and eroding public trust. But AI has made plausible, unvetted work nearly free to produce, and that turns dysfunctions we once tolerated into urgent ones. We reject both complacency and doom. We came to build.

## Principles

**1. Sort the crisis before solving it: most of what is breaking was broken before AI.**
Paper mills, citation gaming, the serials crisis, the replication crisis and the collapse of news business models all predate ChatGPT. Treating every failure as an AI failure lets the institutions that created these problems off the hook, and it invites fixes aimed at the wrong target. We distinguish three things:

- pre-existing structural failures now accelerating, such as fraud, publish-or-perish and overloaded review;
- genuinely new problems, such as text that costs nothing to produce, synthetic output feeding back into models, and a few firms mediating what billions read;
- plain moral panic.

History helps here. Print did not arrive trusted. That trust was built, contested and fought over for centuries. Every intervention we propose should say which of the three kinds of problem it addresses.

**2. Call these systems what they do, not what their marketing says they are.**
Most of us found it useful to treat large models as cultural and social technologies. They are powerful ways of compressing, recombining and redistributing what people have written, closer kin to markets, libraries and bureaucracies than to oracles or colleagues. This framing does not settle what they are. It keeps attention on who builds them, what they are trained on and whom they serve. Words like "knows," "reasons" and "hallucinates" smuggle in conclusions and soften errors. An industry that spends billions on marketing, and can generate its own advocates on demand, should not set everyone else's vocabulary. A shared, plain glossary is something this group can actually produce, and we will.

**3. Writing is now cheaper than reading. Design for the reader.**
For centuries, fluent, well-referenced prose served as a rough proxy for effort, and therefore for worth. That signal is gone. Its loss is not all loss. Fluency always rewarded elite schooling and native English as much as good ideas, and the "AI detectors" built to defend it falsely accuse the very people it once excluded. The bottleneck is now evaluation: reviewer time, editorial attention and reader trust. New signals must be harder to fake and closer to substance, such as open data and code, disclosed methods, verifiable provenance and records of correction. Every new signal will also carry its own biases. Each must be audited for whom it excludes before it hardens into a gate.

**4. Authority belongs to accountable processes and communities, not to documents or credentials alone.**
No individual knows very much. Knowledge lives in communities that divide cognitive labor and check one another. A claim earns credibility from the process behind it: whether it can be traced, contested and corrected, and whether its authors pay a price for being wrong. Credentials still matter when they track that accountability, and they become mere gatekeeping when they do not. Readers need habits suited to this world: checking context before going deep, reading laterally, knowing what to ignore. Used carefully, AI tools can help by surfacing context and counter-evidence quickly. But a fluent summary is not verification, and people routinely overestimate what they understand. Institutions should make their processes visible rather than simply asking to be trusted.

**5. Fix the incentives, or AI will industrialize the worst of scholarship.**
When careers are measured in paper counts and citation totals, a tool that makes papers cheap becomes a tool for fraud at scale. Paper mills, fabricated images and tortured phrases were already flooding journals, and generative AI lowers their cost to nearly zero. Detection is an arms race we will lose if it is our only defense. Hiring, promotion and funding bodies should stop counting outputs and instead assess a small number of contributions in depth. Review, replication, data curation, correction and retraction are scholarly work, and they must be credited and paid as such. Institutions that reward volume are subsidizing the very problem they complain about.

**6. Human judgment is the scarce ingredient. Build tools that strengthen it rather than replace it.**
These tools shine where errors can be caught quickly and deterministically, as in much of software. Much of science and public knowledge is the opposite: feedback arrives over years, and a flawed result can travel far before anyone notices. In those domains, judgment grows more valuable as the surrounding work is automated. Some tools leave us more capable when we set them down; others leave us dependent. We should prefer the first kind and design for it in classrooms, labs and newsrooms. Cognitive atrophy belongs in scope, as a question to study and a risk to design against, not a panic to indulge. The same holds for models degrading on their own output. Keeping verified human work identifiable protects both people and machines.

**7. The knowledge commons is infrastructure. Fund it and govern it as a public good.**
Every AI system and every scholar stands on the same foundations: identifiers, metadata, archives, open corpora, Wikipedia and the scholarly record. Much of this runs on volunteer labor and short grants while being harvested at industrial scale. Critical infrastructure should be open and community-governed. It should be built so it can be forked if its stewards fail, and made machine-readable so that claims, data and provenance travel together. But "open" is not the same as fair. Communities in the Global South have too often supplied knowledge and data to systems they neither govern nor benefit from. Sovereignty over one's own knowledge, including the right to decide how it is used, is part of what stewardship means.

**8. No use without attribution; compensation must reach the people who did the work.**
AI systems that draw on human knowledge should say where it came from. That attribution should be checkable and should send readers back to the sources. It is not a courtesy: it is how knowledge stays correctable and how its makers stay visible. Where money changes hands, it must reach the authors, reviewers, editors, curators and volunteers who produce and maintain that knowledge. It should not stop with the rights holders who happen to sit between them and the buyer. Collective arrangements give creators leverage that individual licensing cannot. We rejected the idea that scholars should become the AI economy's piece-rate annotators. We are stewards of vetted judgment, and we should negotiate as such.

**9. Concentration is the central governance risk. Insist on plurality, transparency and public options.**
When a handful of firms own the models, the compute and the interfaces through which people meet knowledge, their defaults become everyone's epistemology. Early evidence already suggests that AI tools expand individual scientists' reach while narrowing what science as a whole studies. We want:

- many models, including public and nonprofit ones;
- disclosure of training sources;
- independent research access for auditing;
- liability that follows the firms deploying these systems.

AI's energy, water and material costs belong on the same ledger. "Social permission" must be earned with evidence of value, not assumed. Governance should be judged by whether it keeps the knowledge ecosystem diverse and contestable.

**10. Knowledge institutions need independence from platforms and from states.**
Public media, universities, libraries, Wikipedia and statistical agencies are being squeezed from two sides. Platforms absorb their audiences and revenue, while governments defund them, intimidate them or seek to control them. Epistemic resilience is democratic resilience, and propaganda thrives where trusted institutions have been hollowed out. Defending these institutions does not mean defending them uncritically. It means insisting that the capacity to find, test and publish the truth cannot depend on the goodwill of any single company or any single government.

## What we commit to do

- Publish, within six months, a plain-language glossary for describing AI systems and their outputs, and use it ourselves.
- Develop and pilot open standards for the provenance and attribution of scholarly and public-interest content used by AI systems, building on existing persistent-identifier infrastructure.
- Press our own universities, publishers, funders and platforms to stop counting outputs in assessment, and to credit review, data curation, replication and correction.
- Deliver an intervention roadmap and a research agenda, including funded study of cognitive atrophy, research homogenization and model degradation.
- Convene again with the people who were missing: librarians, researchers and publishers from the Global South, early-career scholars and students, data workers, and people who build frontier models.
- Report publicly, one year from now, on what we did and did not do.

## Noted dissents

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas** considers Principle 2 its own kind of imprecision. These systems exhibit real, if unfamiliar, forms of intelligence. Insisting they are "merely statistical" will leave institutions unprepared for what they can already do. He also regards model collapse as overstated, since curated synthetic data can improve models rather than degrade them.

**Benjamin Bratton** objects to the manifesto's human-centered premise. Knowledge has always been co-produced with technical systems, and defending a human perimeter against machines misreads the moment. The real design task is new forms of human–machine cognition, not keeping humans "in the loop" by default.

**Peter Salib** argues that Principle 9 governs today's tools rather than tomorrow's. Increasingly autonomous systems pursuing goals will need legal frameworks the manifesto ignores. Those frameworks may eventually include duties, and perhaps rights, for AI systems themselves.

**Jason Priem** warns that Principle 8 invites enclosure. Pay-to-train regimes will entrench incumbent publishers and wealthy AI firms, and they will quietly paywall the commons. He supports attribution without reservation, but holds that knowledge should stay maximally open, including to machines.

**Leslie Chan** notes that the document still speaks from the North Atlantic. Sovereignty appears as one sentence rather than an organizing principle. The standards and infrastructures the commitments name were largely designed without the communities most affected by extraction.

**David Weinberger** hears Socrates in Principle 6. Knowledge has long lived in networks rather than heads, and every new medium has been accused of rotting minds. Mourning lost capacities risks overlooking the new ones people are building.

**Adam Becker** rejects the preamble's framing of AI as an opportunity to rebuild. That framing concedes the industry's own narrative. The burden of proof belongs with the firms, not with the institutions they are disrupting.

**Peter Pomerantsev** finds the manifesto too centered on scholarly publishing. The larger danger is the information war over democratic publics, where AI-generated persuasion is already deployed at scale and where universities are only one front among many.
