# Cheap to Write, Costly to Know: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge in the Age of AI

We came to MIT as publishers, librarians, scientists, historians, journalists, technologists and critics of technology. We did not agree on what large AI models are, and we do not pretend to agree now. We did agree on this: the institutions that produce, vet and preserve knowledge were strained long before generative AI. The strains came from volume, from perverse incentives, from the collapse of the business models behind journalism and publishing, and from a replication crisis inside the peer-reviewed literature itself. AI did not cause these failures. It removes the friction that let us tolerate them. When plausible, scholarly-shaped text costs nothing, every dysfunction that rewards volume over rigor becomes more dangerous. We reject both complacency and doom. What follows are principles to build by, and commitments we are prepared to make. This is not a consensus document. Where we split, we say so.

## Principles

**1. AI is an accelerant, not the origin. Diagnose before you prescribe.**
Some of what is called the AI crisis is new, some is old failure running faster, and some is moral panic. Paper mills, citation gaming and reviewer exhaustion predate chatbots. So do the collapse of local news and the politicization of expertise. Treating these as AI problems invites AI-shaped fixes, such as detectors and bans, that leave the incentives untouched. They also create new injustices, as when "AI detection" tools falsely flag non-native writers. Three things are genuinely new: fabrication at scale and near-zero cost, answer engines that intercept audiences, and the concentration of the means of synthesis in a handful of firms. Every intervention should say which kind of problem it addresses.

**2. Writing is now cheaper than reading. Invest in evaluation, not production.**
The central asymmetry of this moment is that generating scholarly-looking work scales almost without limit while evaluating it does not. Any system that rewards output volume invites a flood. That includes publication counts, impact factors and the grant metrics built on them. Funders, universities and publishers must shift reward from producing claims to checking them. That means paid peer review, funded replication, recognized post-publication critique, and real support for the sleuths and correction infrastructure that now run on volunteer labor and personal risk. Reviewing, correcting and retracting are not overhead on knowledge production. They are knowledge production.

**3. Fluency is no longer a signal. Build signals that are harder to fake.**
For centuries, polished prose stood in for effort and competence. That signal was always partly a marker of privilege, and its loss need not be mourned. But something must replace it. Credibility should attach to what can be inspected:

- the provenance of data and text
- disclosed methods and tools
- a source's track record over time
- accountable authorship
- openness to correction

Authority earned this way differs from gatekeeping because it is transparent about how it was earned and can be lost. We favor machine-readable provenance and contribution records over stylistic policing. These signals should travel with a claim wherever it is reused, summarized or retrieved.

**4. Knowledge is a social achievement, and so is trust.**
No individual knows most of what they rely on. Knowledge lives in communities, institutions and the practices connecting them: libraries, journals, encyclopedias, newsrooms, labs. Large models are best understood in the same register, as cultural and social technologies that recombine and redistribute what people have collectively written. They are closer to markets, bureaucracies and libraries than to either oracles or toys. So the question is not whether an individual should trust "the AI." It is how these systems reshape the institutions through which trust is built and checked. Teaching people to read laterally and verify sources matters more than ever, but it cannot substitute for healthy institutions.

**5. Describe these systems by what they do, not by how they are sold.**
Our vocabulary is doing work for marketers. Words like "intelligence," "knows," "reasons" and "hallucinates" smuggle in conclusions that remain disputed. Equally, "just autocomplete" understates capabilities that are real and consequential. The room did not settle what these systems are. It did agree that institutions should describe them operationally: what they were trained on, what they retrieve, how and how often they fail, and who is accountable. A shared, precise vocabulary is itself a buildable public good. Language shapes delegation, because we let systems do what we believe they can do. We need words that let a provost, an editor and a judge ask the same concrete questions.

**6. Those who build epistemic infrastructure owe transparency and accountability.**
A few firms now mediate how billions of people encounter knowledge. They hold power once spread across publishers, libraries and newsrooms, without inheriting those institutions' obligations. Black-box systems cannot be audited, corrected or contested. We call for:

- disclosure of training sources and data practices
- citation and linking in generated answers
- published error rates for high-stakes uses
- independent access for researchers
- clear liability when systems cause harm

Some opacity is inherent to the technology. Much more is a business choice. Governance should target the second without waiting to solve the first.

**7. Keep human judgment where feedback is slow, and design tools that strengthen it.**
AI has taken hold fastest where errors are caught quickly and cheaply, as in software. Much of science, medicine, law and policy is the opposite: feedback arrives over years, and a flawed result can spread long before anyone notices. There, accountable human judgment becomes the scarce ingredient, not an obsolete one. We distinguish tools that leave us more capable when switched off from tools that leave us less capable. Cognitive atrophy is in scope. Sustained reading, argument and conversation erode without use, and education must protect them deliberately. The aim is not ceremonial "humans in the loop" but humans who remain answerable and competent.

**8. The knowledge commons is infrastructure. Open must not mean free to extract.**
The scholarly record, Wikipedia, open metadata, public archives and datasets are shared infrastructure. They are maintained by people whose labor is mostly unpaid or invisible. AI developers treat this commons as free raw material while diverting the traffic, donations and attention that sustain it. We reject enclosure by any party, commercial publishers included, and we reject extraction without reciprocity. Openness remains the right default, but the commons needs governance:

- machine-readable terms of use
- obligations to attribute and link back
- maintenance contributions from those who profit most
- community-controlled metadata, identifiers and indexes that no vendor can capture

**9. Credit and compensation must flow back to the people whose work makes knowledge.**
A market for careful human judgment already exists at the heart of the AI economy. It is called annotation, and it is badly paid. Researchers, editors, reviewers, curators, journalists and librarians produce the vetted knowledge that models depend on. That contribution should be attributable at the point of use, through answers that cite and link their sources. It should also carry value back to its producers, through licensing, collective bargaining over data, and open protocols for attribution and payment. The goal is not to turn the commons into a toll road. It is to make visible, and sustainable, the labor that trustworthy knowledge requires.

**10. Plurality is a safety property. Resist epistemic monoculture.**
When a few firms own the models, the compute and the interfaces, errors and biases become correlated across whole societies. When scientists converge on AI-tractable questions, the frontier narrows even as productivity rises. We favor many models, archives, languages and institutions. That includes public and non-profit AI options and regional and sovereign infrastructure. It also includes deliberate support for knowledge traditions of the Global South, which are least represented in training data and most exposed to extraction. Diversity of sources and methods is how knowledge systems find their own mistakes.

**11. Public-interest knowledge institutions deserve public investment and protection.**
Libraries, public media, university presses, encyclopedias and archives are under attack from two directions. Answer engines intercept their audiences, and some governments strip their funding and seek to control what they say. Their decline is not a market correction. It is the loss of the institutions that verify, correct and preserve. We call on philanthropy, and on governments that still value independent knowledge, to fund them as infrastructure. We call on AI providers to sustain them in proportion to the value they draw.

## What we commit to do

- Publish, within weeks, an intervention roadmap with named responsible actors, and a list of open research questions for funders.
- Build and adopt open protocols for provenance, attribution and contribution that travel with content into AI systems. We will extend existing open infrastructure rather than create new silos.
- Pilot evaluation-first incentives in our own institutions: count and reward review, replication and correction, and fund research-integrity work.
- Draft and openly publish a shared operational vocabulary for describing AI systems.
- Press AI developers for training-source disclosure, citation and link-back, and report publicly on who complies.
- Advocate for sustained funding of Wikipedia, public media, libraries and open scholarly infrastructure.
- Reconvene with those this room lacked: Global South scholars, early-career researchers, students, annotators, librarians and policymakers.

## Noted dissents

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas:** Principle 5 still leans toward treating machine understanding as illusion. These systems model the world and reason in meaningful, measurable senses. Refusing the word "intelligence" is its own unexamined assumption, and a diagnosis built on it will misjudge both risks and opportunities.

**Benjamin Bratton:** The document's human-centered frame is parochial. The task is not to defend human judgment against machines but to design a planetary human–machine epistemic system. "Keeping humans central" is nostalgia dressed as principle, and it cedes that design work to others.

**Adam Becker:** The manifesto concedes too much to industry framing. Much of the "opportunity" language and many capability claims are marketing. Nothing here should imply that current systems are epistemic actors, or that knowledge institutions should reorganize around vendors' products.

**Mahzarin Banaji:** Principle 7 overrates human judgment. Decades of evidence show it is systematically biased, often invisibly to the judge. What must be protected is accountability and correctability, whoever or whatever judges. Well-audited machines will sometimes judge more fairly than people.

**Leslie Chan:** Principle 9's compensation frame risks recasting scholarship as training data and the commons as a licensing market. Such markets have historically enriched large Northern publishers and platforms, not authors or Global South communities. Reciprocity should be governed collectively, not priced.

**Adrian Johns:** The historical arc of panic followed by durable new institutions is too tidy. Print's credibility took centuries, was bitterly contested, and depended on piracy and exclusion. Its success was never guaranteed. We should not assume history bends toward repair.

**Steven Sloman:** Concern about cognitive atrophy (Principle 7) is overstated. Humans have always outsourced most of their knowing to others. The real danger is miscalibrated trust in what we depend on, not the loss of individual capacities.
