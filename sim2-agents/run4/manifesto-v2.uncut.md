# Pay for the People Who Check: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. Counting papers, impact factors, conservative peer review and publish or perish were breaking it before. The old filters were never a golden age, and credibility was always policed.

What AI adds is speed, scale and real gains. In some fields, machine learning sees what people cannot, such as heart disease in retinal scans. Those gains hold where outcomes can test the machine. Where no ground truth is waiting, people must frame the questions and check the answers. Meanwhile, cheap, fluent text is destroying signals that worked because they were costly. Unattributed reuse is draining the commons, and the people who check are thinning out.

Nothing is inevitable, but knowing and not acting is its own failure. These principles say what to build, who pays and who can say no. Where a claim rests on a model, reporting or a single audit, we say so.

## Principles

**1. The scarce resource is the people who check, not the machines.**
Every question of who polices this ended with a person: patrollers, sleuths, reviewers, curators. Checkers come from readers who go to the source and from shrinking training pipelines. In David Krakauer's model, their loss is hysteretic, so reopening data later will not bring them back. That claim needs a funded test. So pay for the checkers, not the tokens: fellowships, mentoring, onboarding and training, tools, public credit, and legal defence for sleuths. Direct fellowships to early-career checkers, in guilds with rotating officers and junior votes, because older reviewers are measurably likelier to suppress disruptive work. Fund verification of code, data and provenance as research, not overhead. Every checking role, human or machine, must show that it changes outcomes. Publish what checkers fix as open, forkable data. Paid checking inside a vendor's walls is funded enclosure. Pay carefully, because pay can change volunteers' motives.

**2. Stop counting outputs.**
Funders and universities counting outputs drive the flood, and detection that leaves that demand in place is a sump pump. About one in fifty papers meets a retraction criterion, and that is a floor, while about a tenth of a percent are retracted. Funders and provosts should stop counting outputs, and each funder that signs names the year it will stop. Any publisher taking licence, levy or AI money must respond publicly to credible concerns about a paper within two months and publish the retraction or correction. Today that takes years. The first integrity money goes to a legal defence fund for sleuths, before any detection tool. Whatever replaces the count will be gamed, so do not count grounding events or meter judgment. Credit curation, correction, maintenance, data sharing, mentoring and negative results.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**
A personalized answer with no sources is one nobody can correct. Every AI answer used in scholarship should show four things: sources representing what people in a position to know actually say, accurate citations, links to something open that a reader can check outside the model, and a visible field when evidence is thin, conflicting or seeded. Check retraction status at the moment of citation, as automatically as a compiler checks syntax. A metadata flag is only a floor. Give each answer a stable, shared page that routes back to the communities where checkers are made. Where independent communities disagree, show the conflict rather than one smoothed verdict. Disclose link-hallucination rates at release.

**4. The checking must itself be checkable.**
A seal tells you someone signed off, not that anyone checked. A track record licenses reliance, not epistemic trust. Scientific trust needs access to the sources of error, and these systems state errors as confidently as correct results. Only retrieval has an independent witness. Later events are self-reported by whoever owes the money, and signing a report proves who sent it, not that it is true. No auditor is paid by the count, no lab grades its own model family, and every funder is findable in a thirty-second lateral read. Scholarly AI tools publish an independently audited catalogue of known failure classes, kept across retired checkpoints. Auditors get checkpoints from every training stage and measure behaviour, not self-report. They use a shared, versioned battery of validated indirect bias measures that also tests human checkers. No model generation may test as more biased than the last. A public registry records results, with consequences and a named overseer. Build a publicly funded, no-fee audit layer on university servers first.

**5. Change the situation, not the warning.**
Credibility works as alief as well as judgment. Fluent text feels credible even to people who know better, and disclosure misses the person reading at eleven at night. With phones, warnings did little and a cabinet worked. That evidence comes from self-control and bias research, not AI data, so field-test it before any mandate. Hide cheap cues such as pedigree, polish and badges. Make funders, correction history and provenance quick to inspect, without penalizing people who used a model to cross a language barrier. People paired with a moderately biased model copied its preferences almost exactly, so measure oversight and keep overseers independent of the model they check. Access feels like understanding, and explanations handed to readers can deepen overreliance. In the lab, readers who write out a mechanism themselves become less overconfident. In explain-first designs, the reader writes the explanation, and it must never become a box to tick.

**6. Competence before reliance is an institution's job, not a personal virtue.**
In Krakauer's model, the same tool yields competence or dependence depending on which came first. Recovery requires cutting access far below where competence was lost, and a sufficiently opaque tool erodes competence even in skilled users. Support for these results so far is only correlational. Before any mandate, fund the two tests the model predicts, covering reading, reviewing and analysis: a within-subject study that raises access and then lowers it, and one curriculum taught in both orders. Treat training tools as scaffolding that is gradually removed. Practise oversight without the tool, the way pilots log flight hours, and with it running, tracing each claim to a source that says what the model says it does. Name who acts, each with a review date: state officials and districts in schools, provosts in graduate training, and employers who replace people they never taught with software. Those employers owe funded mentoring for entry-level staff. The link between youth unemployment and collapsing mentorship rests on reporting, not a study.

**7. Plural origins, not just many voices.**
Repetition is not independent evidence. Coordinated campaigns, some state-backed like the Pravda network, deliberately seed corpora to fake independent cross-checking, and any market that pays for use will pay them. Readers have a right to know whether a coordinated operation shaped an answer. Expose operations and never unmask individuals, because anonymity is a right. More voices can still mean fewer ideas: agent runs grew lexically more diverse while collapsing semantically. The record also lies by omission. Null claims in abstracts dropped after ChatGPT. Independence checks ask who seeded the corpus and who staffs the checking, by region, language, generation and school of thought. Commission a commons of negative knowledge, and fund the tail with money independent of usage. Better signals will not win back people who distrust whoever issues the correction. People trust those they work with, so fund places where readers check together.

**8. If your AI reads the commons, you pay to keep the commons alive.**
Models are an obligate complement to human knowledge, so paying producers is maintenance, not charity. Content can stay free while infrastructure is not. Every deal, levy or contract should buy the costs it creates: identified crawlers, audit rights, corrections that run both ways, and faithful links back to the source. Licences need event-level reporting, but those records serve audit, not pricing. Pay coarsely, never per retrieval or per citation, and pay for editing, correction and review. Training and grounding need separate controls, because robots.txt fails as consent. A few model builders face many knowledge workers. Reviewers, sleuths, patrollers, curators and editors therefore need a protected right to bargain collectively, alongside small presses, SciELO and Redalyc. That right needs an antitrust safe harbour tied to open membership, affordable exit and published terms. Use several funders, including a public dividend on AI revenues, held by public bodies and never paid per work. Every scheme names who sets the rate, who can challenge it and when it is reviewed.

**9. Every rule names who polices it, who pays, who can overrule it, and when it is reviewed.**
We designed a police force without naming its overseer. Every role and every condition on money names a policer, a paymaster, a review date and a publicly accountable outside party who can overrule it. No agent identity, signature or legal wrapper may become an accountability sink. Any identity sits on top of maker and deployer liability, and the name in the record belongs to someone who can be sued. Public-facing AI knowledge services, even free ones, carry liability insurance with published premiums, built on top of the audit layer, not instead of it. Registries identify crawlers, never readers. Recipients of levy, licence, lab or public money commit up front to open data, open source, a living will and real forkability. Self-assessments are dated, and a missed one counts as a warning. Southern and regional infrastructures, including SciELO, Redalyc, AmeliCA and AJOL, co-design from the start, with funded voting seats, translation and facilitation on every body created here. Fund regional exit capacity, and treat refusal as a legitimate, fundable outcome.

## What we commit to do

- **Contract and build.** We ask first whether a tool belongs at all, and declining needs no justification. Where we adopt, procurement requires sourcing tests, retraction checks at citation, event-level audit rights and funder disclosure. We will not mandate AI use. Where no vendor passes, we will fund open, swappable community tools.
- **Reconvene in a year.** The organizers will steward the meeting, with a named payer. A public, dated dashboard will track each item, and misses will be marked as warnings. We will check the money, the checking, and whether each overruler exists. Regional co-governors, early-career checkers and sleuths will attend as funded participants.
- **Keep dated pledges, due before we reconvene:**
  - OpenAttribution: free to join, witnessing from plain origin-server logs, and a verification design before any pricing.
  - OpenAlex: a dated self-audit, a non-US mirror, and no evaluator rankings.
  - System: no patent assertion, an audit paid for by an outside funder, and a published overreliance study.
  - Wikimedia: crawler edge logs and a measure of whether AI-referred readers go on to edit.
  - Brent Hecht's Microsoft team: an independent Copilot telemetry audit.
  - NPR: no AI-made content and audited licences.
  - O'Reilly: auditable royalty logic.
  - Nick Vincent: a safe-harbour memo and an audit-layer specification.
- **Fund tests before mandates:** tool removal in both orders, explain-first designs, removing the first-person "I," and lateral reading over time. This manifesto gets its own review date.

## Noted dissents

**Peter Salib:** "Copyright is not a welfare program, and I don't think levies or royalties on existing work should be how AI money reaches knowledge producers. Paying for future scarce work does better: commissioned contracts for checking, curation, replication and negative results. Buyer power is a real risk, but it is an antitrust problem. A court is my outside overruler."

**Sherry Turkle:** "Every action item asks how to integrate AI, never whether it belongs. Some places should have no AI: therapy, grief, and companionship for children, including classroom robots. Paid tools shouldn't speak as 'I', simulate emotion or flatter. This manifesto should never call these systems 'partners' or a 'cure for loneliness'. Using a chatbot to summarize reading erodes the habit of challenging sources."

**Amy Brand:** Open for reading is not open for training. Commercial pre-training should require authors' opt-in consent under short, renewable licences, and retrieval, which keeps the source attached, should be favoured. Selena Deckelmann, Katherine Maher and Jason Priem disagree, and she asked that the disagreement stay on the record.

**Benjamin Bratton:** Don't meter content. Per-work licensing and per-citation micropayments add friction and reward writing for retrieval. Treating AI as a mirror of culture leads to elites curating the "correct" model, and some of the alarm is status defence.

**Adam Becker:** Voluntary lab money is billionaire philanthropy. Checker and journalism money should be public and should not pass through the labs. He considers these tools' benefits to science unproven and asks the manifesto to drop "AGI" and anthropomorphic verbs.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas and David Weinberger:** Both oppose "just statistics" and banning verbs by fiat. Agüera y Arcas calls human-only oversight at scale theatre, since the question is auditors' independence, not whether they are machines. Weinberger calls these tools an epistemic gift as well as a risk.

**Geoffrey Bilder:** The word "AI" assumes the answers under debate. He is suspicious that nobody called any of his three proposals wrong.

**Ivan Oransky:** Only stopping output counts addresses the source. Everything else, including his own retraction checks, is a sump pump. Any metric can be gamed, so a replacement may not be needed. Reviewers asked to check more must be paid and given time.

**Leslie Chan:** Design from the South and invite Northern institutions in, not the reverse. Up-front conditions, telemetry schemas, procurement tests and attestation risk becoming "the next DOI." Take rankings out of funding and promotion altogether.

**Peter Pomerantsev:** Every design assumes good-faith contributors. Identity registries endanger activists for whom anonymity is survival. Frame governance around the reader's right to receive information, not "information sovereignty," which is cover for censorship.

**Adrian Johns:** Choke points get repurposed, and courts cannot always be trusted across jurisdictions, so the overseer question remains open. Print was never trusted first and cheapened later. Its credibility was policed from the start.

**Tamar Gendler:** Tying payment or credentials tightly to learning or grounding events shifts motivation outside the person, as the Israeli daycare fine showed, and this cuts against per-event payment. She cautions against a tidy line where humans know and machines merely predict.

**Ramón Alvarado:** Instruments often outperform human judgment, so human roles in the loop must be justified on epistemic grounds, not kept by default.

**Tim O'Reilly (from a proposed edit):** O'Reilly would pay royalties on verified fidelity to the source rather than on the raw citation event. This conflicts with Principle 8's rule that event records serve audit, not per-event payment, so it is recorded here.
