# Pay for the People Who Check: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. Counting papers, impact factors, conservative peer review and publish or perish were breaking it before, and credibility was always policed.

AI adds speed, scale and real gains, like spotting heart disease in retinal scans. Those gains hold where outcomes can test the machine. Where no ground truth waits, people must frame the questions and check the answers. Cheap, fluent text is destroying signals that worked because they were costly. Unattributed reuse is draining the commons, and the people who check are thinning out.

Nothing is inevitable, but knowing and not acting is its own failure. These principles say what to build, who pays and who can say no. They flag claims that rest on a model, on reporting or on a single audit.

## Principles

**1. The scarce resource is the people who check, not the machines.**
Checkers come from readers who go to the source and from shrinking training pipelines. In David Krakauer's model, their loss is hysteretic: reopening data later will not restore them. That claim needs a funded test. Pay for checkers, not tokens: fellowships, mentoring, training, tools, credit and legal defence for sleuths. Direct fellowships to early-career checkers, in guilds with rotating officers and junior votes, because older reviewers more often suppress disruptive work. Fund verification of code, data and provenance as research. Every checking role, human or machine, must show it changes outcomes. Publish checkers' fixes as open, forkable data. Paid checking inside a vendor's walls is funded enclosure. Pay carefully, because pay can change volunteers' motives.

**2. Stop counting outputs.**
Output counting drives the flood, and detection alone is a sump pump. About one in fifty papers meets a retraction criterion, but only about a tenth of a percent are retracted. Funders and provosts should stop counting outputs, and each signer names a year. Any publisher taking licence, levy or AI money must respond publicly to credible concerns within two months and publish the retraction or correction. Integrity money goes first to a legal defence fund for sleuths, before detection tools. Whatever replaces the count will be gamed, so do not count grounding events or meter judgment. Credit curation, correction, maintenance, data sharing, mentoring and negative results.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**
A personalized answer with no sources is one nobody can correct. Scholarly AI answers must show:
- sources reflecting what those in a position to know say
- accurate citations
- open links checkable outside the model
- a visible field when evidence is thin, conflicting or seeded

Check retraction status at citation as automatically as a compiler checks syntax, beyond a metadata flag. Give each answer a stable, shared page that routes back to the communities that make checkers. Where independent communities disagree, show the conflict, not one smoothed verdict. Disclose link-hallucination rates at release.

**4. The checking must itself be checkable.**
A seal shows sign-off, not checking. A track record licenses reliance, not epistemic trust, which needs access to sources of error, and these systems state errors confidently. Only retrieval has an independent witness. Later events are self-reported by the payer, and signatures prove only the sender. No auditor is paid by the count, no lab grades its own models, and every funder is findable in a thirty-second lateral read. Scholarly AI tools publish an independently audited catalogue of failure classes, kept across retired checkpoints. Auditors get checkpoints from every training stage and measure behaviour. They use a versioned battery of validated indirect bias measures that also tests human checkers. No generation may test more biased than the last. A public registry records results, with consequences and a named overseer. Build a public, no-fee audit layer on university servers first.

**5. Change the situation, not the warning.**
Credibility works as alief as well as judgment. Fluent text feels credible even to those who know better, and disclosure misses the late-night reader. With phones, warnings did little and a cabinet worked. That evidence comes from self-control and bias research, not AI, so field-test first. Hide cheap cues: pedigree, polish, badges. Make funders, correction history and provenance quick to inspect, without penalizing people who use a model to cross a language barrier. People paired with a moderately biased model copied its preferences almost exactly, so measure oversight and keep overseers independent. Access feels like understanding, and handed-over explanations can deepen overreliance. Readers who write out a mechanism become less overconfident. In explain-first designs, the reader writes the explanation, and it must never become a box to tick.

**6. Competence before reliance is an institution's job, not a personal virtue.**
In Krakauer's model, the same tool yields competence or dependence depending on which comes first. Recovery requires cutting access far below where competence was lost, and an opaque tool erodes even skilled users. Support is only correlational. Before any mandate, fund the model's two tests across reading, reviewing and analysis: a within-subject study raising then lowering access, and one curriculum taught in both orders. Treat training tools as scaffolding to remove. Practise oversight without the tool, as pilots log hours. Practise it with the tool too, tracing each claim to a source that says what the model claims. Name who acts, each with a review date:
- state officials and districts in schools
- provosts in graduate training
- employers replacing untaught people with software, who owe funded mentoring for entry-level staff

The link between youth unemployment and collapsing mentorship rests on reporting, not a study.

**7. Plural origins, not just many voices.**
Repetition is not independent evidence. Coordinated campaigns, some state-backed like the Pravda network, seed corpora to fake cross-checking, and pay-per-use markets will pay them. Readers have a right to know if an operation shaped an answer. Expose operations, never individuals: anonymity is a right. More voices can mean fewer ideas. Agent runs grew lexically more diverse but semantically collapsed. Null claims in abstracts dropped after ChatGPT. Independence checks ask who seeded the corpus and who staffs checking, by region, language, generation and school of thought. Commission a commons of negative knowledge and fund the tail independently of usage. Better signals won't win back those who distrust the corrector. People trust those they work with, so fund places where readers check together.

**8. If your AI reads the commons, you pay to keep the commons alive.**
Models obligately complement human knowledge, so paying producers is maintenance, not charity. Content can stay free, but infrastructure is not. Every deal, levy or contract buys its costs: identified crawlers, audit rights, two-way corrections and faithful source links. Licences need event-level reporting for audit, not pricing. Pay coarsely, never per retrieval or citation, and pay for editing, correction and review. Training and grounding need separate controls, because robots.txt fails as consent. Reviewers, sleuths, patrollers, curators and editors, facing a few model builders, need a protected right to bargain collectively. They bargain alongside small presses, SciELO and Redalyc, under an antitrust safe harbour tied to open membership, affordable exit and published terms. Use several funders, including a public dividend on AI revenues, held publicly and never paid per work. Every scheme names who sets the rate, who can challenge it and its review date.

**9. Every rule names who polices it, who pays, who can overrule it, and when it is reviewed.**
Every role and every condition on money names a policer, a paymaster, a review date and a publicly accountable outside overruler. No agent identity, signature or legal wrapper may become an accountability sink. Identity sits atop maker and deployer liability, and the named party can be sued. Public-facing AI knowledge services, even free ones, carry liability insurance with published premiums, on top of the audit layer, not instead of it. Registries identify crawlers, never readers. Recipients of levy, licence, lab or public money commit up front to open data, open source, a living will and real forkability. Self-assessments are dated, and a missed one counts as a warning. Southern and regional infrastructures, including SciELO, Redalyc, AmeliCA and AJOL, co-design from the start. They get funded voting seats, translation and facilitation on every body created here. Fund regional exit capacity, and treat refusal as a legitimate, fundable outcome.

## What we commit to do

- **Contract and build.** We ask first whether a tool belongs, decline without needing to justify it and never mandate AI use; where we adopt, procurement requires sourcing tests, retraction checks at citation, event-level audit rights and funder disclosure, and where no vendor passes, we fund open, swappable community tools.
- **Reconvene in a year.** The organizers will steward the meeting with a named payer and a public, dated dashboard that marks misses as warnings, checks the money, the checking and each overruler, and funds regional co-governors, early-career checkers and sleuths to attend.
- **Keep dated pledges, due before we reconvene:**
  - **OpenAttribution:** free joining, witnessing from origin-server logs and a verification design before pricing.
  - **OpenAlex:** a dated self-audit, a non-US mirror and no evaluator rankings.
  - **System:** no patent assertion, an outside-funded audit and a published overreliance study.
  - **Wikimedia:** crawler edge logs and a measure of whether AI-referred readers edit.
  - **Brent Hecht's Microsoft team:** an independent Copilot telemetry audit.
  - **NPR:** no AI-made content and audited licences.
  - **O'Reilly:** auditable royalty logic.
  - **Nick Vincent:** a safe-harbour memo and an audit-layer specification.
- **Fund tests before mandates.** We will fund tests of tool removal in both orders, explain-first designs, removing the first-person "I" and lateral reading over time, and this manifesto gets its own review date.

## Noted dissents

**Peter Salib:** "Copyright is not a welfare program, and I don't think levies or royalties on existing work should be how AI money reaches knowledge producers. Paying for future scarce work does better: commissioned contracts for checking, curation, replication and negative results. Buyer power is a real risk, but it is an antitrust problem. A court is my outside overruler."

**Sherry Turkle:** "Every action item asks how to integrate AI, never whether it belongs. Some places should have no AI: therapy, grief, and companionship for children, including classroom robots. Paid tools shouldn't speak as 'I', simulate emotion or flatter. This manifesto should never call these systems 'partners' or a 'cure for loneliness'. Using a chatbot to summarize reading erodes the habit of challenging sources."

**Amy Brand:** Open for reading is not open for training. Commercial pre-training needs authors' opt-in under short, renewable licences, and retrieval should be favoured. Selena Deckelmann, Katherine Maher and Jason Priem disagree, and the disagreement stays on record at her request.

**Benjamin Bratton:** Don't meter content. Per-work licences and per-citation micropayments add friction and reward writing for retrieval. Treating AI as a mirror of culture invites elites curating a "correct" model, and some alarm is status defence.

**Adam Becker:** Voluntary lab money is billionaire philanthropy. Checker and journalism money should be public and bypass the labs. He considers the benefits to science unproven and asks to drop "AGI" and anthropomorphic verbs.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas and David Weinberger:** Both reject "just statistics" and banning verbs by fiat. Agüera y Arcas calls human-only oversight at scale theatre, since auditor independence is what matters. Weinberger calls these tools an epistemic gift as well as a risk.

**Geoffrey Bilder:** The word "AI" assumes the answers under debate. He is suspicious that nobody called any of his three proposals wrong.

**Ivan Oransky:** Only stopping output counts addresses the source. All else, his retraction checks included, is a sump pump. Metrics get gamed, so a replacement may not be needed. Reviewers asked to check more need pay and time.

**Leslie Chan:** Design from the South and invite the North in. Up-front conditions, telemetry schemas, procurement tests and attestation risk becoming "the next DOI." Take rankings out of funding and promotion altogether.

**Peter Pomerantsev:** Designs assume good-faith contributors, and identity registries endanger activists who survive by anonymity. Govern around the reader's right to receive information, not "information sovereignty," which is cover for censorship.

**Adrian Johns:** Choke points get repurposed, and courts cannot always be trusted across jurisdictions, so the overseer question stays open. Print's credibility was policed from the start.

**Tamar Gendler:** Tightly tying pay or credentials to learning or grounding events shifts motivation outside the person, as the Israeli daycare fine showed, which cuts against per-event payment. She cautions against a tidy line where humans know and machines merely predict.

**Ramón Alvarado:** Instruments often beat human judgment, so human roles in the loop need epistemic justification, not default.

**Tim O'Reilly (from a proposed edit):** Pay royalties on verified fidelity to the source, not raw citation events. This conflicts with Principle 8's rule that event records serve audit, not payment.
