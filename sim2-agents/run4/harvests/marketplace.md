# Harvest — What might a well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace look like?

1. Verification is the unfunded piece that every layer depends on. Tim O'Reilly, Alex Springer and Nick Vincent each said that only retrieval has two independent witnesses and that every downstream event is self-reported by the party that owes the money; Mahzarin Banaji called this a conflict of interest that makes the audit layer the basis of the market's legitimacy, and Tui Shaub asked who stewards and pays for canaries and sampled audits beyond a pilot. Adam Becker and Leslie Chan asked that the funding and ownership of every auditor and data supplier be published, and Banaji and Benjamin Bratton said any model used as a checker must come from a different family than the model being checked.

2. The proposed events record that use happened, not whether it was faithful to the source. Ramón Alvarado noted that a stitched or fabricated reference would be logged as "cited" and asked for payment on verified fidelity, with audits sorted by failure class; Banaji added that small errors such as a wrong year or journal must also be caught, Mike Caulfield that an accurate citation can still misrepresent the state of knowledge, and Adam Bly that "extracted" should be an audited event, while Becker went further and argued that fabrication is how these systems normally behave and that they don't belong where being wrong is a problem.

3. Paying by usage will change what gets written. David Krakauer warned that tying income to retrieval produces writing tuned to be retrieved and that "engaged" is not the same as understood, and Shaub, Bly, Kenneth Cukier and Anil Dash added that it pays for the median, positive, retrievable finding and starves nulls, dead ends and the odd paper. Proposed remedies were funding kept separate from usage, such as Vincent's coarse commons fund (Krakauer, Cukier, Bratton), paying to produce missing data on purpose (Bratton, Cukier), funding the training of checkers (Krakauer, Selena Deckelmann), and review dates on pricing rules (Shaub, Cukier).

4. The plumbing has to be open and community-governed, and it has to work without a CDN. Chan cited the DOI becoming a pay-to-be-visible regime and asked for co-design with regional infrastructures like Redalyc, SciELO and AmeliCA from the start, with real exit and refusal, and Dash warned that a meter, ledger and audit inside Big AI's interface would rebuild the app store. Dash, Deckelmann and Cukier agreed it must be permissionless and run on a university server, and Deckelmann asked that crawlers identify themselves and log at the host's own edge, with deals also buying audit rights, two-way corrections and funding for patroller tools.

5. Unresolved: how the market treats past training and consent. Becker argued that paying for outputs quietly forgives training on copyrighted work without permission; O'Reilly answered that it is not an amnesty and that the past belongs to the courts and to required training-data disclosure, and Dash said a market only means something if a no is real and enforceable. Bratton dissented from the theft framing, saying his books' inclusion is worth far more to him than royalties, and preferred a sovereign-wealth-style public dividend to per-citation micropayments, which he said add friction and reward writing for retrieval.

## Minority positions

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Nothing should be paid until a source passes a retraction check against Retraction Watch, CrossRef and PubPeer. He proposed running that check as a funded default MCP service that every agent queries, and credited Ivan's question for the idea.
- Some of the labs' commons funding should go to bounties for sleuths and to a legal defence fund, because the funder cannot also be the only auditor.
- He said per-output royalties are workable today, as O'Reilly Answers shows, and called payment a coordination and mechanism-design problem, not a technical barrier. This is the per-output position Bratton dissented from.

**Alex Springer**
- Robots.txt is not a licensing tool. In his audit, 7.8 percent of citations came from domains blocking the training bot and 2.1 percent from domains blocking the live-search bot, which he read as publishers blocking the wrong bot. He added that private licensed feeds, such as Reddit's to Gemini, make public consent signals meaningless.
- Training controls should be kept separate from grounding controls, because training is effectively permanent and grounding is per-query and revocable.
- AIMS identifies agents and never readers, and the standard carries signals, not prices.
- Any AI licence without an event-level reporting clause is incomplete.

**Nick Vincent**
- A lone researcher gets a price of zero, so guilds, trusts and data joint ventures built from societies and reviewer communities should bargain as counterparties. This would need a published antitrust safe harbour and a Ulysses pact of open membership, affordable exit and published terms. The harvest does not record collective bargaining.
- The commons backstop would be funded by a presumptive commons-rent tax on unexplained capability, which firms can reduce to zero by documenting provenance. The harvest records the fund but not the tax.
- Sellers' leverage is most stable in evaluation data.
- Coarse payouts should sit on top of detailed records, not replace them. This qualifies the harvest's framing of his fund as separate from usage.

**David Krakauer**
- A well-run marketplace could still speed up the erosion of readers' competence. In his view, output accepted uncritically erodes competence, and only output that is reconstructed and verified transfers method.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Responding to Vincent, he said attestation can show who contributed but not where an error came from, since that is buried in the weights. Failure classes can be counted, but no single error can be traced to its source.

**Adam Bly**
- Most flawed papers are never flagged, so "corrected for retractions," including his own 2023 promise, is weaker than it sounds. This cuts against relying on retraction checks as validation.
- Letting users flag errors is not an audit.
- The record should show whether the evidence agrees and where it is missing, not only what was used.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Paying for attribution will produce more misuse of people's names to launder junk. He cited Grammarly putting his name on advice he would never give, and said his complaint was about the misattribution, not about getting a cut.
- He sided with O'Reilly on direction. He asked that the standard require representative sources, accurate citations, and a link to something open that a reader can check outside the model.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- He relayed James's warning that paying the same gatekeepers produces "frame monoculture with a salary attached." This cuts against guilds built from existing societies and associations.
- He proposed a hybrid: meter the ordinary, but fund the tail.

**Leslie Chan**
- Visibility should mean public benefit, not retrievability. He said "don't optimize for legibility, change the regime," which questions the premise of making use countable.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Wikipedia's content stays free, including for commercial training, and Wikipedia will not put up a pay-per-article blocker. What she wants paid for is infrastructure, not content, which sets her apart from per-use content pricing.
- Attribution matters to Wikipedia as a volunteer pipeline, because readers who never visit never become editors, and admin and patroller numbers are shrinking.
- Hosts should publish their own crawler logs instead of relying on anyone's self-reported numbers.
