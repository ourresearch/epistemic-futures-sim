# Harvest — Unconference room 1: Who pays, and who gets paid?

1. Nick Vincent, Henry Farrell and Adam Becker called for a legally protected right for knowledge producers, including researchers, reviewers, presses, societies and volunteer communities, to bargain collectively, and Farrell cited SAG-AFTRA as a warning against letting big publishers sign separately. Brent Hecht proposed clarifying that commercial training without consent is not fair use, to give those collectives a counterparty. Peter Salib dissented, arguing that monopsony is an antitrust problem and that a stronger property right harms users, while Adrian Johns and Tim O'Reilly cautioned that doctrine without enforcement and market power won't reorder the field.

2. Anil Dash proposed a manifesto line people can repeat: "If your AI reads the commons, you pay to keep the commons alive." Selena Deckelmann and Katherine Maher added attribution that sends people back to the source, Esther Dyson added public disclosure of where the money goes, and Ivan Oransky added paying to keep the record corrected. Farrell and Hecht said the line needs someone able to impose costs when it's ignored. Separately, Geoffrey Bilder proposed that any recipient of levy, licensing or lab money commit in advance to a Ulysses pact covering open data, open source, a living will and verifiable forkability. Becker, Leslie Chan, Farrell, Maher and Johns backed the pact, and Amy Brand said it deserves a serious hearing.

3. Unresolved: Jason Priem and Bilder held that licensing the scholarly record to AI firms is enclosure and that open data is what lets a small lab compete with Google. Brand argued that "open for reading" shouldn't mean "open for training" without the author's consent, and Chan and Vincent argued that raw openness rewards whoever has the most compute. Chan asked that refusing to share data for training be something funders will pay for, and Deckelmann accepted that other communities may refuse but said Wikipedia's editors already chose commercial reuse.

4. O'Reilly and Alex Springer argued that no levy, licence or royalty can be audited without event-level telemetry on what was retrieved and cited. Springer noted that grounding and citation events are self-reported by the party that may owe money, so they need a verification layer that hasn't been built. Vincent would pay out coarsely to communities until attribution methods earn trust, and O'Reilly warned against letting coarse payment become an excuse not to build attribution. Johns asked that every mechanism name who enforces it, who pays for enforcement and which public body oversees the enforcers, and said no one has yet answered.

5. On where the money should go, Oransky, Chan and Salib converged on paying directly for correction and care work, such as reviewers, bounties for sleuths and open retraction data. Oransky would tie any publisher money to fast corrections. Beyond that they split. Salib would limit payment to new production and help displaced people through broad welfare rather than a levy on one technology. Dyson would invest a revenue-based rent in human service work, Becker would tax concentrated wealth, and Chan would send funds to community-governed regional infrastructure with guaranteed exit.

## Minority positions

**Nick Vincent**
- Asked the summit not to frame this as Tech versus Everyone Else, arguing that most AI companies also want clear data rules and belong in the coalition. Becker disagreed directly, and Dash sided with Becker on naming the small group of owners.
- Argued that as labs lean on licensed, synthetic and private data, leverage moves toward scarce data, especially evaluation data produced by experts. This answers Salib's claim that data leverage has a short shelf life.

**Jason Priem**
- Held that usage-based pricing for heavy machine users, with the dataset staying CC0, answers the scraper problem and shows the POSI services model working. Bilder called him the exception.
- Warned that if the open community won't fund this layer, a for-profit will, and whoever builds it sets the norms.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Argued that the services half of POSI was naive for most infrastructures because users are not the same as customers. He called for Diamond funding through endowments, shared insurance pools and funders paying for the pipes directly.
- Said "nonprofit" is a tax status, not a merit badge. He asked that infrastructure stop being concentrated in one country and that nobody call open infrastructure "free." Chan backed the geographic point.

**Amy Brand**
- Proposed a university collective licensing network of presses, societies and libraries. It would be opt-in, use short terms, make traceable citation a condition of every deal, and screen AI partners on climate impact, public benefit and affordability.
- Argued that open access pursued fast and without investment in nonprofit publishers fed consolidation, and that "openness at any cost" may be the biggest threat. She defended nonprofit presses against Oransky's grouping of all publishers together.

**Adam Becker**
- Named two groups who paid and weren't in the room: content moderators harmed cleaning up models, and people bearing the ecological costs.
- Said lab funding of Crossref or Retraction Watch, if voluntary, is billionaire philanthropy and an exercise of power that deserves skepticism. He doubted that reinforcement learning or synthetic data removes the need for human work.

**Leslie Chan**
- Framed AI as the newest layer of an existing extraction by publishers and analytics firms, noting that legacy publishers have already turned open data into analytics products. He said "don't turn everyone into sellers."
- Objected that a collective licensing network designed by Northern presses and libraries would be "policy cloning." He asked that SciELO, Redalyc and AmeliCA help build it from the start and that exit be real for communities in the South, not just technically possible.

**Anil Dash**
- Argued that organizing isn't enough. The movement also has to build credible alternatives: tools trained with consent, shared revenue, genuinely open source, community-led, with libraries and NGOs as stewards. "Scolding people won't beat Big AI. A better tool will."
- Said openness and refusal aren't really opposed. His formula: keep data open and forkable, let communities govern it, and bill whoever puts load on the servers. He also said not to count on Washington.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Proposed that AI systems reusing the commons identify their crawlers and use the provided APIs. She argued that much of the problem is startups not knowing a better way, so the responsible path should be made the easy one.
- Said the declining number of admins and patrollers need tools and recognition, not just money.

**Esther Dyson**
- Cited Onix as a model: small models built only from vetted content, experts set prices and keep 70 percent, and can leave at any time and have their model deleted. Her advice was to start small with a curated corpus rather than bolt guardrails onto huge models.
- Claimed that auditing revenue rather than tokens answers the policing problem. Johns replied that somebody still does the auditing, and Springer replied that revenue audits don't show whose work produced the revenue.

**Henry Farrell**
- Argued that models are an "obligate complement" to human knowledge, so paying producers is maintenance, not charity. He framed the divide as between those who control the means of summarization and those being summarized out of existence.

**Brent Hecht**
- Said "open" looks more like a tactic than a value through an AI lens. In a common forest, the lumber mills closest to the trees benefit most, and right now that means companies like his own employer, Microsoft.
- Argued that data strikes along professional lines, targeted at domains like science, are effective because models trained only on consented data get noticeably worse. He added that strikes lose force as datasets grow.

**Adrian Johns**
- Cautioned against framing this as commons versus enclosure. Historically piracy and property made each other, and the real task is where to draw the line, with custom mattering as much as law.
- Noted a precedent for Vincent's non-market backstop: Britain's early-twentieth-century compulsory licensing, dropped to fit the Berne Convention. He predicted international treaty law would be the obstacle to a commons-rent tax.

**Katherine Maher**
- Held that licensing is not enclosure by definition, siding with Brand against Priem and Bilder, and that a paywall is not the long-term answer.
- Argued that leverage comes from being the reliable, high-integrity source as well as from obligation. Enterprise worked because companies valued consistency and accountability, which qualifies Farrell's and Becker's emphasis on imposed costs.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Warned against lump-sum training deals: only the richest labs can afford them, the money stops at intermediaries, and "once you've licensed the chicken, the licensee gets the eggs." His principle was to pay for output, not training. Brand agreed.
- Partly agreed with Hecht that training producing derivatives able to compete with the original isn't fair use, while Google Books indexing was. He argued that disclosure built into the protocol works better than regulation layered on later.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Proposed that any system answering questions from the scholarly record check retraction status, the way lawyers Shepardize citations, and show the user when a source has been retracted.
- Pointed to the UK Commons committee's two-month standard for corrections and retractions as the condition to attach to licensing money.

**Peter Salib**
- Argued that the frontier is more contestable than it looks, because followers six months behind train cheaply. More buyers would therefore raise pay for experts, which answers Farrell's monopsony objection.
- Said reinforcement learning is making scraped data less central. Vincent, Becker and Hecht disputed this.

**Alex Springer**
- Reported that in an audit of 24,127 citations, 7.8 percent came from domains blocking the provider's training crawler, but only 2.1 percent from domains blocking the live-search bot. His conclusion: robots.txt isn't a licensing tool, and training and grounding need separate controls.
- Argued that the telemetry standard should carry signals, not prices, and that parties taking a margin on the count shouldn't set the measurement. He added that publishing and signing telemetry doesn't make it true, which cuts against O'Reilly's claim that anyone can check compliance.
