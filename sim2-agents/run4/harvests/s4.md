# Harvest — Session 4: Epistemic Authority — Credibility in an Age of Infinite Scale

1. Credibility works as alief rather than judgment, so a renewed model of trust has to redesign the situations where claims are met instead of relying on literacy or disclosure (Gendler, Banaji, Sloman, Agüera y Arcas). The room converged on hiding cheap signals like name, pedigree, polish and badges while making expensive ones, such as funders, correction history and the provenance behind any seal, quick to inspect (Banaji, Becker, Caulfield, Bilder, Cukier, Dash, Dyson); Weinberger and Agüera y Arcas added that stripping polish must not penalize people who used a model to get past a language barrier.

2. Most speakers held that a terminal node is a named role a person or organization answers for, with the deployer named in the record, and that a model can work inside that role but cannot be accountable for it (Bilder, Brand, Dyson, Shaub, Deckelmann, Springer, Caulfield). Agüera y Arcas and Bratton dissented that roles should be defined by independence, not substrate, and Salib argued that sanctionability is a legal construct that could extend to registered, bonded agents; Evans, Farrell, Cukier and Alvarado made machine evaluation task-dependent (acceptable for code, data, provenance and ground-truth checks, not for judging novelty), and Turkle warned against redefining judgment as a function.

3. First-order evaluation is labor currently carried by volunteers, and the room called for paying, training and protecting the people who do it: attestation with sampled audits, collective bargaining, early-career fellowships, legal defense for sleuths, and AI answers that route back to the community sources where checkers are made (Vincent, Hecht, Priem, Oransky, Miller, Deckelmann, Dash, O'Reilly, Dyson, Evans, Brand, Maher). Krakauer, Evans, Hecht and O'Reilly agreed that evaluator records should be open but not scored, while Oransky countered that the real risk is too few and uninformative corrections, not staged ones.

4. Independence must cover who staffs the nodes, including region, language, generation and school of thought, not only model family, and those held to any new schema should govern it and be able to refuse it (Shaub, Chan, Evans, Cukier, Bly, Pomerantsev). Bratton argued that open weights and locally fine-tuned models widen access; Chan and Dash countered that diffusion through a few platforms is capture, which Bratton accepted while holding that access should not be slowed.

5. Unresolved: who funds and polices the checkers long-term without capturing them (Hecht, Becker, Dyson, Springer), and whether better nodes will persuade publics that no longer trust the institutions behind them (Maher, Pomerantsev, Turkle, Farrell, Miller). Johns and Shaub proposed that every evaluation role carry a named steward or policer, a named paymaster, a review date and, per Johns, an outside party able to overrule it.

## Minority positions

**Tamar Gendler**
She cautioned against a tidy line where humans know and machines merely predict, because much of human cognition is also prediction. The harvest records the room's consensus that models cannot be terminal nodes without noting that the session lead raised this caution.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
In her work with Bazerman, selecting better people did not help much, so terminal nodes must be defined by independence and audited, not staffed by virtue. Every frontier model tested preferred its own text and machine text over human text, and humans who absorb a model's preferences become relays, not checks.

**Adam Becker**
He treated funding disclosure as the central requirement for any terminal node, which sits uneasily with Gendler's opening claim that trust cannot rest on disclosure. The harvest's first item states both without marking the tension.

**Mike Caulfield**
He conceded he cannot yet show that the click-through habit lasts, and asked that someone fund that test.

**Nick Vincent**
Polish used to work as a negative filter, since sloppy presentation warned reviewers early. That signal is gone, so he proposed crediting plain, checkable artifacts such as data, code and transcripts, and warned that reviewer guilds need rotation and room for outsiders or they become cartels.

**David Weinberger**
The terminal-node picture risks rebuilding the old authority structure "with nicer plumbing." Credentials suit settled knowledge, but at the edges credibility comes from transparency and visible fallibility, so the node is a practice, not a class of people. This cuts against the harvest's definition of a node as a named role.

**Jason Priem**
He retracted his earlier claim that aggregated signals like citations and altmetrics could replace peer review. He also argued that journal peer review was always a proxy and should be decoupled from publishing, so that first-order evaluation becomes its own named, paid service. Attestations can only be audited against an open record.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
The independence rule that bars a model from grading its own family equally bars a lab from grading its own lab's output.

**Amy Brand**
AI needs its own category in the contributor taxonomy, not one folded into validation or review. If AI money flows only to the largest publishers, the unpaid editors, reviewers and copyeditors at small presses and societies who do the evaluation will be lost.

**David Krakauer**
He relocated Gendler's line: expertise is the ability to catch an error, and authority is the right to go unchecked. His main worry is not whether models can evaluate but errors that go uncaught because the faculties needed to catch them have been outsourced.

**Ivan Oransky**
He argued that peer review was never an earned signal. On his view, the line between authority and gatekeeping is whether an institution acts on what its checkers find, and he proposed requiring public publisher responses within two months. Correction notices, he said, should state the cause and name who found the problem.

**Ramón Alvarado**
He argued that what makes something a terminal node is access to the nature and source of its errors, not substrate or sanctionability, which disputes the Bilder–Dyson criterion. He also warned of testimonial injustice from administrators using tools against experts, so trust design must protect human experts against the machine as well as against pedigree.

**Adam Bly**
He qualified "no answers from nowhere": an accurate citation can still misstate what is known, because nulls and failed replications are largely missing from the record. He also said a DOI-backed effect size may be the most persuasive alief of all. The provenance trail needs fields that flag thin or conflicting evidence, and the extraction layer itself must be independently audited.

**Benjamin Bratton**
Responding to Alvarado, he said most human reviewers' errors cannot be traced either, and that this is why adversarial roles exist in the first place.

**Leslie Chan**
He located gatekeeping mainly in infrastructure (indexes, DOIs, Scopus, rankings) rather than pedigree, against Gendler's framing. He warned that an attestation schema designed in Cambridge would exclude Southern reviewers, as DOI fees have pushed some diamond journals to charge authors. This cuts against the harvest's endorsement of attestation.

**Selena Deckelmann**
English Wikipedia's active admins are at a decade low, and most began editing around 2005, so the practice lasts one generation unless the newcomer pipeline is funded. She rejected the panel's assumption that evaluators must be credentialed or selected.

**Esther Dyson**
She proposed mandatory liability insurance for AI knowledge services, on the grounds that insurers lose money when wrong. She added that insurers can also be captured, so premiums should be disclosed.

**James Evans**
Evaluators should be studied against how work turns out years later. He framed this as research on the system rather than a leaderboard, which qualifies the blanket "don't score" position.

**Henry Farrell**
Peer review depends on a legitimacy bargain that someone took care over the judgment. Once people suspect the process is automated, that bargain fails regardless of output quality, which cuts against role-based arguments that focus on output.

**Brent Hecht**
Reviewers already hold a lever: they can require disclosure of which named role did what and which model family touched the work.

**Adrian Johns**
Earned authority and gatekeeping historically came out of the same policing apparatus, so authority cannot be sorted into good and bad by inspection. The only question is who can hold the guards to account.

**Katherine Maher**
She disputed Dash's example: Wikipedia is a relay by design and depends on the journalism and research beneath it, which is being defunded. She added that a personalized AI answer, unlike a Wikipedia article, offers no shared page for anyone to be held accountable for or to correct.

**Tim O'Reilly**
Reference checking against CrossRef, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch should be automatic, like a compiler checking syntax. Any royalties should be paid on verified fidelity, not on citation counts.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
State capture is the main threat: strongmen take over or discredit terminal nodes rather than abolish them. Operations like the Pravda network seed corpora to fake independent cross-checking, so independence checks must ask who seeded the corpus, not only which model family was involved.

**Alex Springer**
He disagreed with Salib's direction. His own agent-identity system names who is answering, but a signed event is "a relay with a name on it" and proves neither truth nor completeness. Real checking needs independent canaries and audits that he has not yet built.
