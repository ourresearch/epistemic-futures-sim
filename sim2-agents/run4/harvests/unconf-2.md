# Harvest — Unconference room 2: The role of knowledge in the age of LLMs

1. Knowledge lives in communities, not individual heads, so these tools should make people do reasoning work, such as explaining how something works or weighing views that pull against each other, rather than hand them verdicts (Pomerantsev, Sloman, Weinberger, Evans, Caulfield). The value of individual literacy stayed contested: Sloman called the evidence for fact-checking and critical-skills teaching thin at best, while Caulfield cited controlled lateral-reading gains but conceded that durability and use outside the classroom are unshown.

2. Pluralism can be staged. Repetition is not independent evidence, a training corpus can be flooded, and more voices can still mean fewer ideas (Pomerantsev, Sloman, and Evans's 200,000 agent conversations). The proposed remedy is to show the independent communities and evidence behind an answer and where they conflict, rather than one smoothed verdict (Sloman, Agüera y Arcas, Cukier, Bly), and to test any such display by how costly it would be to fake (Caulfield).

3. The room backed several forms of transparency:
   - a citizen's right to understand how their information environment is shaped (Pomerantsev)
   - disclosure of training data (Weinberger)
   - claim-level audit trails back to findings, populations, sources and retraction status, governed as public infrastructure (Bly)
   - disclosure of failure modes rather than internals (Alvarado)
   - behavioural audits, because a model's self-report is not enough (Banaji)

   There were two limits. Cukier would open curation algorithms to public authorities and auditors but not to rivals. Agüera y Arcas, Bly and Bratton argued that provenance by type of author (human or machine) will not hold up as writing becomes hybrid.

4. Banaji proposed that no model be the final judge of its own output, or of AI-generated work, without independent audit, and that no new generation be more biased than the last. Evans supported this with data showing that LLM judges reflect shared priors, not shared standards. Which humans should do the judging stayed open. Cukier called for funding a new class of curators, auditors and editors. Evans warned that paying existing gatekeepers adds another narrowing layer, and asked for independent, younger and outside evaluators, diversity audits, and a record of what was ruled out. Bratton warned against a small, curated "correct" model and called for green lines that widen access across languages.

5. Whether LLMs "know" anything was left unresolved:
   - Weinberger held that they don't, while granting it is partly a quarrel over a word.
   - Agüera y Arcas and Bratton argued that statistics add up to understanding.
   - Alvarado said they perform real epistemic operations without intelligence or understanding.
   - Cukier located their success in human frames baked into the training data.

   A related split also stayed open: Bratton sees the spread of once-scarce expertise as the opposite of helplessness, while Pomerantsev argued that an abundance of information can itself produce passivity.

## Minority positions

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- People can usually reach the truth but often don't want it, because belief tracks identity and belonging. He warned that better signals of rigour, verification or supply won't bring back people who have stopped trusting whoever sends the signal.
- He said consensus-producing machines like Google's "Habermas machine" miss the point, because the value lies in people reasoning together themselves. He said propaganda's real aim is helplessness, and LLMs that hand over conclusions could produce it "with a friendlier face."
- The manifesto should not regulate what these systems say. His proposed right explicitly included knowing whether you are dealing with a human or a machine, and whether a model was deliberately seeded. He called lack of transparency itself a form of censorship.

**Steven Sloman**
- He described the knowledge illusion: people can't tell what's in their own heads from what surrounds them. LLMs worsen it because knowledge arrives fluently and in your own words, and the most extreme holders of views tend to know least and feel they know most.
- LLMs are superb models of intuition but nowhere near human deliberation. They don't share intentionality with us, which makes them a source for a community of knowledge and not a member of one.
- He attached a caveat to his own "explain it" finding: the drop in confidence replicates well, but the softening of views is less consistent.

**David Weinberger**
- He called LLMs dialogic partners that answer Socrates' two complaints about writing. He proposed teaching dialogue as a literacy and grading the conversation, not only the essay.
- He proposed dropping "human in the loop" as a slogan in favour of "humans in the flow," working out with the machine what a good decision would look like.
- He flagged the Wattenberg and Viégas finding that models may carry an internal sense of how likely something is to be true while saying false things. He said it should be taken seriously, not explained away.
- He said an answer that won't show its sources should read as arrogance, and called transparency "the new objectivity."

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- He objected directly to Pomerantsev's proposal that knowing "human or machine?" be a basic right. He argued it disadvantages people writing with these tools in a second language or with a disability.
- He placed the danger not in the machine being fake but in the oracle setup of one box answering and a human receiving. His alternative is "ecological AI," societies of human and machine agents deliberating with disagreement built in.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- The only fitting trust is epistemic trust earned with warrants, and a track record isn't enough. This cuts against Cukier's willingness to accept some faith in a system that keeps being revalidated against ground truth.
- He disagreed with Caulfield on how knowable errors are. Classes of "strange error" can be catalogued, but the source of any particular error is spread across billions of weights and can't be recovered.
- Drawing on his work on epistemic loneliness, he said a dialogic partner is not an epistemic partner, meaning a willing peer who shares values.
- He named "but ChatGPT said…" used to overrule a human expert as a testimonial injustice, not neutral efficiency.
- He rejected Bratton's claim that artifactual epistemology concedes "intelligence." He said calling it intelligence misunderstands both computation and intelligence.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Alignment has produced "LLM hypocrisy": models state unbiased answers while biased associations drive their decisions. Against Sloman, she said this explicit layer is a mask, not deliberation.
- Models invent new biases, and across generations biases have often worsened. People working with a moderately biased system adopt its preferences almost exactly, so a human in the loop is not a safeguard in itself.
- She challenged the "show the independent sources" remedy directly by asking who picks the sources. Models prefer LLM-written content, so a machine curator could quietly erode the independence being counted.
- She asked for a public registry of bias. She also asked that auditors get checkpoints from each training stage and that older models be kept rather than deprecated.

**Adam Bly**
- LLMs are not the knowledge but a way to put knowledge in order. A chatbot has no observed or measured model of the world to align with, which cuts against Agüera y Arcas and Bratton.
- Science itself is part of the crisis, with more than ten thousand retractions in 2023, so the grounding layer can be corrupted.
- His answer to Banaji's "who picks the sources?" was to make the picking itself auditable by publishing methods, grounding logic and known biases.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Models have an "epistemic overhang," discovering things human science struggles to take in, so alignment should run both ways, with culture also aligning to AI's disclosures.
- He rejected "Reflectionism," the view that AI is a mere mirror of culture, and said it discloses things we didn't put in.
- He suggested noticing who benefits from telling people that access to expertise amounts to surrendering agency.
- "AI does this" is a finding, and "AI is only this" is a trap.

**Mike Caulfield**
- The internet works less as a brainwashing machine than as a justification machine, and sycophantic AI is a more efficient version. Against Pomerantsev's opening, he said the answer is not to give up on evidence but to make unsupported beliefs cost something.
- Hallucinations cluster in recognisable situations like radar clutter, and calling every error a hallucination blurs a distinction we need.
- Refusing the tools protects no one. The habit to break is asking for answers, not using the tools.
- He said AI systems have worked backwards, writing an answer first and then finding citations. The first test should be whether the sources represent what people in a position to know actually say, before citation accuracy.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Machines lack causality, counterfactuals and constraints, which explains hallucination. Text is a tiny slice of reality, and much of what matters most never gets written down.
- AI delivers the median while excellence is a tail event, so the people who produce excellence should be rewarded.
- Atrophy is real, and human judgment should be kept in practice the way pilots log flight hours.
- Some algorithms, such as spam filters and product recommendations, are legitimate trade secrets. Sometimes performance and explainability must be traded off, and he would accept some faith in a system if it keeps being revalidated.

**James Evans**
- AI helps scientists and hurts science. Users publish about three times as many papers and get nearly five times the citations, while fields narrow toward data-rich questions and scientists engage with each other about 22 percent less.
- Negative knowledge never enters training, and null claims in abstracts dropped sharply after ChatGPT.
- When a model gives a single "balanced" answer, people perform worse but feel better about how they did.
- Systems should be designed to open up the range of voices, not to win trust, and incentives must change, not only models.
