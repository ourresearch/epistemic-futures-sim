# Harvest — Session 1: The Epistemic Landscape

1. Much of what the room diagnosed was breaking before AI. Farrell, Chan, Priem, Oransky and Evans traced drift toward the middle to impact factors, conservative peer review and publish-or-perish, and Johns argued the old filters were never a golden age and always needed policing. Where AI adds to the drift is disputed: Weinberger, Agüera y Arcas and Shaub locate it in the single-answer oracle format, Evans in the model, the format and the incentives together, Banaji in models that amplify bias rather than copy it, and Bratton in the languages and regions missing from training data.

2. Cheap fluent text is destroying signals that worked because they were costly (Farrell, Becker, Caulfield), and Farrell, Vincent and others called detection an arms race. Bilder argued that context signals such as affiliations, registrations and correction histories survive, and Vincent proposed crediting verifiable artifacts over prose. Chan and Bratton countered that fluency was always a privilege signal, and Gendler warned that telling people polish is cheap won't stop it from feeling credible.

3. Unattributed reuse is draining the commons that AI depends on. Deckelmann and Maher pointed to Wikipedia's editor and donor pipeline, Hecht to the collapsed bargain between model builders and content producers, Vincent to a precarious "quasi-enclosure," and O'Reilly and Springer to measurable retrieval and citation choices. Unresolved: Priem sees the core split as open versus enclosed, while Brand, Hecht and Springer argue that openness without consent, a bargain or measurement speeds the drain. Dash warns that locking content away also destroys the record, and Oransky asks whether any licensing money buys a clean literature.

4. Atrophy is in scope, but its scale is contested. Krakauer, Miller, Turkle, Dyson, Cukier and Gendler include individual brains. Farrell emphasizes institutions, and Agüera y Arcas and Sloman treat outsourcing thought as how cognition has always worked. Krakauer and Sloman proposed measuring competence with the tool taken away, especially the oversight skills needed when it fails. Dash, Miller and O'Reilly asked who will train the next generation to catch the tools' errors now that mentorship is collapsing.

5. Several participants said the diagnosis must name power and incentives rather than treat these effects as weather. Becker and Dyson pointed to a few companies and their business models, Pomerantsev to deliberate flooding, including of training data, Salib to unidentifiable agents acting at scale, and Maher to political and financial pressure on public-interest institutions. Shaub argued the room is mostly working from anecdote and that ongoing, funded measurement is itself an intervention, modeled on post-release surveillance in gene editing. Evans, Krakauer, O'Reilly, Springer and Deckelmann offered early measurements, and Johns and Caulfield cautioned against a verdict before the evidence lasts.

## Minority positions

**Henry Farrell**
Disinformation aimed at individuals worries him less than institutions losing judgment work and publics forming malformed shared understandings. The harvest's power section leads with deliberate flooding.

**Leslie Chan**
AI is the latest layer of an extraction built on scholars' unpaid labour and metadata, and openness alone does not protect against it. The remedy is changing who governs the infrastructure, not building better detectors.

**Selena Deckelmann**
She disagreed with Chan and will not enclose Wikipedia content. Her line is that the content is free and the infrastructure isn't, so reciprocity has to be built into the infrastructure. She also reported that crawler traffic is saturating network links and degrading access for human readers.

**Katherine Maher**
Institutions were built for a homogeneous public and were never very accountable to it, so she would diagnose institutions before feeds. She retracted her earlier view that citations suffice, because showing an attribution isn't verification, and asked for correctability instead.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
Better signals and perfect provenance will not win back a public that distrusts whoever issues them, because people believe according to whom they belong with. The deepest harm of cognitive surrender is helplessness and cynicism, and the cure is agency practised together.

**Nick Vincent**
One group's open release can undercut another group's bargaining. Communities should be free to choose the commons but should not negotiate with labs alone.

**David Weinberger**
Since most students won't write essays after they graduate, he would teach dialogue: what you probed and where you trusted too easily.

**Jason Priem**
AI makes reading and auditing cheap, not just writing. Bilder and Alvarado directly disputed this. He also argued the ability to reason over the whole record at scale is what the open movement was waiting for.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
"Lossy summary" gets the mechanism wrong. His Jabberwocky results suggest the models learned patterns that let them deblur the record rather than a blurry copy of it. He also cited reasoning models doing better when they run an internal "society of thought."

**Geoffrey Bilder**
The realistic threat is systems slowly filling with subtly buggy data and code that the same tools can't clean out. He welcomed the possibility that integrity problems become unwinnable, because that might finally separate career evaluation from publication counts.

**Amy Brand**
Hallucination is a feature of these systems, not a bug that better training data will fix, which is why she favours retrieval. Small presses and Global South publishers are already being left out of AI access deals, and she wants that measured before policy is made.

**David Krakauer**
Hysteresis means dependence cannot simply be reversed, so getting the order right now is cheaper than repairing later. He is only partly aligned with Agüera y Arcas: the danger is competence ceasing to exist anywhere in the system.

**Tui Shaub**
She conceded that chance built into a tool does not replace other people. Serendipity has to happen between people, not only inside a product.

**James Evans**
Abstracts with no null claims rose sharply after ChatGPT, and he wants negative knowledge named in the diagnosis and deliberately collected. Adding voices is not the fix, since agent runs grew lexically more diverse while collapsing semantically. Verification needs its own funding.

**Ramón Alvarado**
These are "epistemic technologies" to be audited like instruments, whose particular errors cannot be traced to their source. Disagreeing with Krakauer and the concept note, he argued instruments often outperform human judgment, so human roles in the loop must be justified on epistemic grounds rather than kept by default.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
In her hiring experiments, people paired with a moderately biased model copied its racial preferences almost exactly and fed them into the next model's data. Human judgment was never a gold standard, so the safeguard is structure: strip identity and prestige cues before any evaluation.

**Adam Becker**
He has seen no evidence that these tools accelerate science, and the published positive results look optimistic. This cuts against Priem, Bly and Cukier.

**Adam Bly**
Because models prefer machine-written text, extraction from the literature inherits that tilt and needs its own audit. Oransky contested his reading of the retraction record.

**Benjamin Bratton**
The room's diagnoses contradict each other. Reflectionism leads to elites curating the "correct" model. The collapse of fluency is partly a transfer of expertise to people who never had it, and some of the alarm is status defence.

**Mike Caulfield**
Hallucinations are patterned and predictable, not random, which cuts against Becker's claim that errors and correct answers are indistinguishable. Checking a sourced claim is fast even though rigorous auditing is slow. The underrated new problem is sourcelessness, since most links people see will now come through an LLM.

**Kenneth Cukier**
Answering Bratton, a house of mirrors both reflects and distorts, so both diagnoses can hold. He rejected sorting into utopians and doomers and said nothing about the outcome is inevitable.

**Anil Dash**
The test for real disruption is how much adoption is coerced through mandates and defaults versus chosen, and that can be counted. Against Becker, he said the utility is real for hundreds of millions of users, and scolding them will lose them. The answer is building alternatives.

**Esther Dyson**
Transparency should cover money flows and what systems are designed and prompted to achieve, not weights. Her worry is that people are starting to think like context-poor LLMs, which she calls "information diabetes."

**Tamar Gendler**
The design target is people's situations and habits, not their awareness. That cuts against media literacy and "theory of business mind" as remedies.

**Brent Hecht**
The scholarly community has real bargaining power, especially in peer review and correction work.

**Adrian Johns**
Peer review is salvageable because many independent presses use different referee lists. A single automated reviewer would look more like censorship. Every design should say who polices it and who guards the guards.

**Kara Miller**
On phones in schools, the failure was not panic but knowing the answer for years and not acting. That cuts against waiting for measurement. Collapsed mentorship, not AI, may be the bigger cause of rising youth unemployment.

**Tim O'Reilly**
On atrophy he sides with Miller over Krakauer, arguing via Socrates that we gained more from writing than we lost. Value-return mechanisms need building before the market consolidates.

**Ivan Oransky**
The record is full of junk nobody has flagged: about one in fifty papers meets a retraction criterion, while about a tenth of a percent are retracted. Detection without addressing publish-or-perish demand is "flood insurance." Any new money should require retraction checks in every answer, open retraction data, and journals retracting within two months.

**Peter Salib**
The strong stochastic-parrots claim has been tested and failed. Reasoning models learn goal pursuit and act. He asked sceptics to name a test current models fail, and proposed legible agent identity and a Pigouvian tax on bot output ahead of detection.

**Steven Sloman**
Access to knowledge makes people confident without making them competent. The one hard-to-fake cue of earned authority is willingness to be corrected.

**Alex Springer**
In his audit of about 24,000 AI-answer citations, publishers who thought they had opted out had blocked the wrong bot, so robots.txt fails as consent. Training and per-query grounding need separate controls, and repositories should start logging now.

**Sherry Turkle**
The note's scope is too narrow: AI's effect on reading, not just writing, erodes empathy and imagination. The diagnosis should first ask whether we want these tools in a given setting, since "humans in the loop" assumes the answer.
