# Harvest — Session 2: AI as Epistemic Actor: Risks, Capabilities, and Governance

1. **The business model is a black box, but not the only one.** Esther Dyson argued that the opacity that matters most is money flows and engagement targets. Ramón Alvarado, with David Krakauer, held that a structural opacity would remain even under "unconflicted saints": errors come with the same confidence as correct results and can't be traced in the weights. Others added further black boxes: training data (Amy Brand), how an answer was reached (Adam Bly), publishers' volume model (Ivan Oransky), the scholarly publishing stack (Leslie Chan), state-run flooding of chatbot sources (Peter Pomerantsev), and the supply chain of people whose work trains the models (Brent Hecht).

2. **Accountability stays with people and firms. Whether agents should also carry their own assets is unresolved.** The rule stated by Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Henry Farrell, that agent identity sits on top of maker liability and never replaces it, was accepted by Peter Salib. Salib still defends limited liability by default, plus agent-held assets for harms no human could prevent. Adam Becker and Farrell warned such entities become liability shields or "accountability sinks," and Benjamin Bratton argued that finding culprits doesn't scale to billions of agents at all.

3. **Disclosure doesn't reach the person reading the answer, so change what's easiest to do.** Sherry Turkle, Tamar Gendler, Brent Hecht and Kara Miller argued this, and Mahzarin Banaji and David Weinberger showed that a human in the loop can simply pass the model's bias along, so oversight should be measured, not assumed. Gendler, Steven Sloman and Weinberger named Mike Caulfield's "no answers from nowhere" and Selena Deckelmann's correctable link as the strongest designs. Jason Priem added that the link must point to open content, disagreeing with Amy Brand's opt-in licensing for open-access material.

4. **No proposal works until someone is named to police it and pay for it.** Tui Shaub and Adrian Johns pressed this, and Ivan Oransky, Alex Springer and Geoffrey Bilder warned that retraction flags and signed claims are only a floor. Proposed answers included:
   - labs funding the infrastructure (Tim O'Reilly) and the sleuths (Oransky),
   - a commons-rent tax (Nick Vincent),
   - procurement and funding conditions (Anil Dash, Shaub, Hecht),
   - countervailing power (Farrell).

   Leslie Chan and Bilder required that exit be affordable, including outside the North. Kara Miller warned that none of this works if nobody trains the checkers.

5. **Whether these systems are intelligent is an open question.** Blaise Agüera y Arcas asked that the harvest not settle "just statistics," and Tamar Gendler agreed. Adam Becker called that view pareidolia, Ramón Alvarado proposed "epistemic technology, not actor," and Alex Springer called it a distribution channel. Sherry Turkle's proposal to remove the first person was backed by Becker and Anil Dash, called skin-level by Benjamin Bratton, and met with a reservation from Agüera y Arcas against legislating the question closed.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
Proposed a few simple rules rather than a comprehensive law, chiefly mandatory liability insurance for anyone offering these systems to the public, with disclosed premiums. She also called for a personal habit, "who's paying for this?", rather than a literacy course.

**Ramón Alvarado**
Held that a track record, which is what insurers price, does not license trust in scientific uses, because the trust involved is epistemic. This cuts against Kenneth Cukier's and David Weinberger's reliance on revalidation and reliability records.

**James Evans**
Argued the worst failures are correlated, not strange: shared blind spots and vanishing null results that look normal in any single output and show only at the level of the whole literature. He proposed funding verification as its own science and building a commons of negative knowledge.

**Peter Salib**
Maintained that agents pursue goals that emerge from training, and that holding a human principal liable for harms they could not have prevented is performative rather than a deterrent.

**Sherry Turkle**
Extended the interface proposal beyond the first person to ban simulated emotion and flattering personas, especially for children. She held that with these systems, "the better they get, the worse it gets."

**Geoffrey Bilder**
Said "run by unconflicted people" guarantees nothing, and that a .agent registry would be the most valuable thing to capture. Open data, open source, a living will and forkability must be written in before launch. He also distinguished self-declared from accountably confirmed provenance.

**Amy Brand**
Proposed architecture as a governance lever: favour retrieval, which keeps the source attached, over pre-training. She also warned that licensing deals are going to the largest publishers while small presses and societies are left out.

**David Krakauer**
Proposed sequencing: no opaque pipelines in research until researchers can do the work without them. He also held that only a competent human can catch hallucination. Tui Shaub opposed the first point, and Mahzarin Banaji and Steven Sloman disputed the second.

**Tui Shaub**
Asked that every restriction carry a review date so precaution doesn't harden into permanent hyper-vigilance. Kenneth Cukier agreed.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
Proposed appliance-style standards (no model more biased than its predecessor) and a bias tax scaled to performance and reach. She held that audits must measure what models do rather than what they say ("LLM hypocrisy"), using independent auditors with checkpoint access and with retired models preserved.

**Adam Becker**
Said the real fixes are political: regulate the firms, tax the wealth buying influence over knowledge, and require disclosure of who funds any tool making epistemic claims.

**Adam Bly**
Withdrew part of his own earlier claim, conceding that "corrected for retractions" overstates the guarantee. He said the audit trail must cover LLM extraction, including at his own company, and argued society rather than corporations should pay for auditing by reviving technology assessment. This cuts against Tim O'Reilly's labs-pay model.

**Benjamin Bratton**
Said several proposals in the room are badly designed precautionary regulation. He also held that calling these systems mirrors means auditing only for what we put in, which misses what they reveal. He proposed courtroom-style role templates with adversarial checks.

**Ivan Oransky**
Said nothing replaces reading the paper. He argued the fix is upstream, with funders ceasing to count papers, and that paying for detection without changing incentives is like buying sump pumps. He proposed corrections within two months and public investigation reports.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
Disagreed with Henry Farrell's caution against AI auditing AI. He argued that human-only oversight at scale is theatre, and that the real design question is independence of the auditors, not whether they are machines.

**Mike Caulfield**
Asked that link-hallucination rates be a disclosed number at model release. He argued these tools lower the cost of verification, and asked that the harvest not say "avoid these tools."

**Leslie Chan**
Held that refusal (not collecting, not training) is a legitimate outcome when governance is inadequate. He added that who builds the measurement instruments, and whose languages they count, is itself a governance question. Selena Deckelmann similarly said not using AI can be the responsible choice.

**Kenneth Cukier**
Argued opacity does not rule out trusted use if the system is continuously revalidated. He proposed tiered transparency: trade secrecy for low-stakes uses, and disclosure to authorities but not rivals for high-stakes ones. He held that full public disclosure destroys incentives and protects no one.

**Anil Dash**
Proposed measuring how much adoption is coerced rather than chosen. He doubted US law or courts will act, and said open models about six months behind the frontier are good enough for most scholarly work.

**Selena Deckelmann**
Asked that the affected community, not only the liable principal, sit in the accountability chain. Wikipedia does this through admin-controlled tools, false-positive reporting and published demographic performance.

**Henry Farrell**
Warned that "AI audits AI" as a default fix leads to arms races. He proposed pairing procurement with collective bargaining by knowledge producers, bounded by competition policy.

**Brent Hecht**
Reported that explanations can make overreliance worse. He proposed one policy, that commercial training requires consent, which aligns with Amy Brand against Jason Priem.

**Adrian Johns**
Asked who oversees the police. He said defenders of authenticity tend to become the least accountable party, and that registries tighten control elsewhere, citing an anti-piracy unit reportedly used to track dissidents.

**Katherine Maher**
Disagreed with governance at network scale and said norms must come from the small communities that bear the cost. She argued licensing deals should buy audit rights and two-way corrections, and that provenance does not create trust; trust has to be earned.

**Kara Miller**
Asked for named actors (provosts, library directors, funders) next to every proposal. She partly disagreed with Anil Dash, noting that on phones, states and districts did act, though late.

**Tim O'Reilly**
Revised his earlier view, saying disclosure needs enforcement behind it on the securities model ("a prospectus, not a priesthood"). He proposed DOI validation as automatic as a compiler's syntax check, and argued that AI hasn't consolidated yet, so this is the moment to act.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
Was uneasy about identity registries because anonymity is survival for activists. He proposed targeting coordinated operations instead, and framing governance around a right to receive information. He reported that fact-checking can raise distrust, and warned against "consensus machines."

**Steven Sloman**
Disagreed with David Krakauer that users must reconstruct the tool's working, arguing competence lives in accountable communities. He said machines give off the cues of a community without being one, and that LLMs model intuition but not the deliberation that catches errors.

**Alex Springer**
Said whoever takes a cut of the count should not set the measurement. He noted that the completeness checks needed (canaries, sampled audits, ratio checks) have not yet been built, and asked for an event-level reporting clause in every licence.

**Nick Vincent**
Said researcher guilds need clear antitrust rules to act as counterparties, and that any attested data they sell must carry correction and retraction status.

**David Weinberger**
Asked that the record note an epistemic gift in these tools. With Tim O'Reilly and Kenneth Cukier, he aimed cynicism at the rents rather than the technology.

**Jason Priem**
Cautioned that when OpenAlex first opened curation to outside contributors, two-thirds of the corrections were wrong. This tempers the community-correction designs of Selena Deckelmann and Katherine Maher.
