I've spent most of my career on unglamorous plumbing: identifiers, metadata, the paratext that tells a reader whether to trust something. ### 3. David Krakauer (convener)

I convened this because I study intelligence, and over the years I've come to think its evil twin deserves just as much attention. Stupidity isn't just missing intelligence. It has its own dynamics, and it scales with ingenuity. A civilisation equipped with the most powerful cognitive artifact it has ever built can also be stupid on a scale it has never managed before.

So my framing for today is one question about every tool and workflow we design: what's left when the tool is taken away? A map leaves you knowing how to navigate. A GPS doesn't. That's the difference between complementary and competitive tools, and my model says an opaque tool, handed to billions of people with little prior competence, sits right in the regime where competence collapses. What we should produce are design principles on the right side of that line. That means competence before reliance, tools transparent enough to rebuild, and output that gets reconstructed and verified rather than taken on trust. And a human in the loop isn't enough. An uncritical human in the loop collapses too.

On Blaise's argument: I partly agree with him. Intelligence belongs to the whole system of person plus tool, not to the substrate on its own. But capability isn't intelligence. Calculators beat us at arithmetic a century ago. Intelligence means doing more with less, through understanding and analogy. And Geoffrey's right that the word matters. The worst danger is error nobody can detect, because we've outsourced the very faculties that would catch it.

One last thing: if we agree too quickly today, treat that as a warning sign.
