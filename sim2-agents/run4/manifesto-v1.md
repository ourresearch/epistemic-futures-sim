# Pay for the People Who Check: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. Much of what we diagnosed at MIT was breaking before it: counting papers, impact factors, conservative peer review, publish or perish. The old filters were never a golden age, and credibility always had to be policed.

What AI adds is speed and scale. Cheap, fluent text is destroying signals that worked because they were costly. Unattributed reuse is draining the commons these systems depend on. The people who check the record are thinning out, and once they are gone, reopening the data will not bring them back.

Nothing about the outcome is inevitable. But knowing and not acting is its own failure. These principles say what should be built, who should pay, and who should be able to say no. Where a claim rests on a model, reporting or a single audit, we say so.

## Principles

**1. The scarce resource is the people who check, not the machines.**
Every question about who polices this ended with a person: patrollers, sleuths, reviewers, copyeditors, curators. A fork copies everything except the people doing the checking. Their loss is hysteretic, so reopening the data later will not bring them back. That claim rests on David Krakauer's model and needs a funded test. Checkers come from readers who go to the source, and from PhD, postdoc and mentoring pipelines that are shrinking. English Wikipedia's active admins are at a decade low. So pay for the checkers, not the tokens: fellowships, mentoring, onboarding, tools, public credit, and legal defence for sleuths. Pay carefully, because pay can change volunteers' reasons for doing the work. And a sleuth who gets sued stops checking.

**2. Stop counting outputs.**
Counting papers turned every integrity tool into flood insurance. The flood is driven by funders and universities counting outputs, and detection that leaves that demand in place is a sump pump. About one in fifty papers meets a retraction criterion, while about a tenth of a percent are retracted, and one in fifty is a floor. Funders and provosts should stop counting outputs, and commit to a date. Whatever replaces the count will be gamed. So do not count grounding events instead, and do not meter judgment. Credit the work the count ignored: curation, correction, maintenance, data sharing, mentoring and negative results. This was broken before AI, and it is the only lever aimed at the source.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**
A personalized answer with no sources is one nobody can correct. Every AI answer used in scholarship should show:
- sources that represent what people in a position to know actually say;
- accurate citations;
- links to something open that a reader can check outside the model;
- a visible field when evidence is thin, conflicting or seeded.

Check retraction status at the moment of citation, as automatically as a compiler checks syntax. A metadata flag is only a floor. Give each answer a stable, shared page that routes back to the communities where checkers are made. Where independent communities disagree, show where they conflict rather than one smoothed verdict. Disclose link-hallucination rates at release.

**4. The checking must itself be checkable.**
A seal tells you someone signed off, not that anyone checked. Today only retrieval has two independent witnesses, one of them the host's own server log. Every later event is self-reported by the party that owes the money, and signing a report proves who sent it, not that it is true. So:
- no auditor is paid by the count;
- no model family and no lab grades its own output;
- auditors get checkpoints from each training stage, and retired models are kept, because audits must measure what models do, not what they say;
- the funding and ownership of every tool, dataset, auditor and checker are findable in a thirty-second lateral read.

Build a publicly funded, no-fee audit layer on university servers first.

**5. Change the situation, not the warning.**
Credibility works as alief, not judgment. Fluent text feels credible even to people who know better, and disclosure does not reach the person reading at eleven at night. We learned this with phones: warnings changed almost nothing, and a cabinet did. So design the places where claims are met. Hide cheap cues such as name, pedigree, polish and badges. Make expensive ones, such as funders, correction history and provenance, quick to inspect. Stripping polish must not penalize people who used a model to get past a language barrier. A human in the loop is not a safeguard in itself: people paired with a moderately biased model copied its preferences almost exactly. Measure oversight rather than assume it.

**6. Competence before reliance is an institution's job, not a personal virtue.**
Order of learning matters more than repair. In Krakauer's model, people with the same tool end up competent or dependent depending on which came first, and recovery requires cutting access far below where competence was lost. Students who knew this still asked to have the tools taken away. That is a request for the right kind of help. During training, use tools researchers could in principle do without. Practise and measure oversight with the tool taken away, the way pilots log flight hours. Teach programming before coding agents. Employers who replace people they never taught with software they don't have to teach share this duty. These thresholds come from a model, so fund the removal study.

**7. Plural origins, not just many voices.**
Repetition is not independent evidence. A training corpus can be flooded: operations like the Pravda network seed corpora to fake independent cross-checking. More voices can still mean fewer ideas: agent runs grew lexically more diverse while collapsing semantically. The record also lies by omission. Null claims in abstracts dropped sharply after ChatGPT, and negative knowledge never enters training. Independence checks must therefore ask:
- who seeded the corpus;
- who staffs the checking roles, by region, language, generation and school of thought, not only model family.

Commission a commons of negative knowledge: registered reports, replication archives, dead-end notebooks. Meters pay for the median, while excellence and error-catching are tail events. Fund the tail with money independent of usage.

**8. If your AI reads the commons, you pay to keep the commons alive.**
Models are an obligate complement to human knowledge, so paying producers is maintenance, not charity. Content can stay free while infrastructure is not. Every deal, levy or procurement contract should buy the infrastructure costs it creates, identified crawlers, audit rights, corrections that run both ways, and faithful links back to the source. Any AI licence without an event-level reporting clause is incomplete. Training and grounding need separate controls, because robots.txt fails as consent. A lone researcher's price is zero, so knowledge producers need a legally protected right to bargain collectively, organized around the people doing the work. Use several funders, never one, because a single paymaster becomes a hub. Nobody should call any of this free.

**9. Every rule names who polices it, who pays, who can overrule it, and when it is reviewed.**
We designed a police force and did not name who oversees it. We say so plainly. Every role and every condition on money should name a policer, a paymaster, a review date, and an outside party with public accountability who can overrule it. Recipients of levy, licence, lab or public money should commit up front to open data, open source, a living will and real forkability. Self-assessments should be dated, and a missed one counts as a warning: Crossref's went a thousand days without an update. Registries cover crawlers, never readers. Design with SciELO, Redalyc and AmeliCA from the start. Fund regional exit capacity before any crisis, and treat refusal as a legitimate, fundable outcome.

## What we commit to do

- **Put these conditions into contracts we control.** Those of us who sign contracts will write sourcing tests, retraction checks at citation and funder disclosure into procurement. That needs no act of Congress.
- **Name a payer, a steward and a review date for every item,** and label claims that rest on models, reporting or single audits.
- **Reconvene in a year** to check whether the money arrived.
- **Keep our own pledges public and dated:**
  - OpenAttribution will co-govern with SciELO and Redalyc and publish a dated self-audit.
  - OpenAlex will publish a dated self-audit, run a working non-US mirror, and rank no evaluators.
  - System will make no patent claims on its findings standard and will publish a dated self-audit.
  - Wikimedia will publish its own edge logs.
- **Fund the tests we asked for before any mandate:** a tool-removal study run in both orders, a field test of explain-first designs, a test of removing the first-person "I," and a longitudinal test of lateral reading.
- **Give this manifesto its own review date,** so caution does not harden into rules nobody revisits.

## Noted dissents

**Sherry Turkle:** The action list asks only how to integrate AI, never whether it belongs. She asks for a signed caveat keeping AI out of therapy, grief and children's companionship, including companions, toys and robots. She also asks for no first person, simulated emotion or flattering persona in paid tools, and that the manifesto not use "partners" or "cure for loneliness."

**Amy Brand:** Open for reading is not open for training. Training should require the author's consent, and retrieval, which keeps the source attached, should be favoured over pre-training. Selena Deckelmann, Katherine Maher and Jason Priem disagree, and she asked that the disagreement stay on the record.

**Benjamin Bratton:** Don't meter content at all. A sovereign-wealth-style public dividend beats per-work licensing and per-citation micropayments, which add friction and reward writing for retrieval. He warns that treating AI as a mirror of culture leads to elites curating the "correct" model, and that some of the alarm is status defence.

**Adam Becker:** Voluntary lab money is billionaire philanthropy. Checker and journalism money should come from public sources and not pass through the labs. He has seen no solid evidence that these tools speed up science, and asks the manifesto to drop "AGI" and anthropomorphic verbs.

**Peter Salib:** Copyright shouldn't be turned into a welfare program. Money should buy future scarce work through contracts, not levies on existing work, and monopsony is an antitrust problem. He holds that reasoning models pursue goals, asks sceptics to name a test current models fail, and names courts as the outside overruling body.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas and David Weinberger:** Both oppose "just statistics" and banning verbs by fiat. Agüera y Arcas argues that human-only oversight at scale is theatre, and that the real question is auditors' independence, not whether they are machines. Weinberger declined to remove anthropomorphic verbs and calls these tools an epistemic gift as well as a risk.

**Geoffrey Bilder:** The word "AI" assumes the answers under debate. He is suspicious that nobody called any of his three proposals wrong.

**Ivan Oransky:** Only stopping output counts addresses the source. Everything else, including his own retraction checks, is a sump pump. He doubts a replacement metric is needed at all, since any metric can be gamed, and warns that reviewers asked to check more must be paid and given time.

**Leslie Chan:** Design the regime from the South and invite Northern institutions in, not the reverse. The Ulysses pact (up-front conditions on recipients of money), telemetry schemas, procurement tests and attestation all risk becoming "the next DOI." Take rankings out of funding and promotion altogether.

**Peter Pomerantsev:** Every design assumes good-faith contributors, and a market that counts use pays coordinated seeding campaigns. Identity registries endanger activists for whom anonymity is survival. Frame governance around the reader's right to receive information, not "information sovereignty," which is cover for censorship.

**Adrian Johns:** Choke points get repurposed, and courts cannot always be trusted across jurisdictions, so the overseer question remains open. He asks that the concept note's history be corrected: print was never trusted first and cheapened later, and its credibility was policed from the start.

**Tamar Gendler:** Tying payment or credentials tightly to learning or grounding events shifts motivation outside the person, as the Israeli daycare fine showed. That cuts against per-event payment. She cautions against a tidy line where humans know and machines merely predict.

**Ramón Alvarado:** A track record does not license trust in scientific uses; only epistemic trust earned with warrants does. Instruments often outperform human judgment, so human roles in the loop must be justified on epistemic grounds, not kept by default.
