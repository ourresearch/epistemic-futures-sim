# People, Not Data: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, 2026*

## Preamble

None of this is inevitable. Some of it was already broken: incentives that reward volume, an uncleaned scholarly record, and a system that never served most of the world. Some of it was breaking: trust signals, the open web, funding for knowledge institutions, and independence from governments. AI adds speed and extraction without return: fluent answers that send no one back to the sources and volunteers they depend on. It also adds discovery. Machine learning checked against the world found heart-disease risk in retinal scans; text generators were never checked this way. The questions are which tool, for what use, by whom, and checked how. The ledger leaves out data centers' energy and water use and the harm to low-paid moderators and annotators. These are political choices, reversible by regulation, organized labor and public funding. Everything below must survive a hostile government or funder. In one line: AI firms pay the communities they take from, answers cite sources with correction status and send people back, buyers write this into every purchase, and the people described govern it. Dissents close the document.

## Principles

**1. The bottleneck is people, not data.**

These systems depend on the people who check what we know: Wikipedia admins, research-integrity sleuths, reviewers, first-order evaluators, and young workers who learn by doing the boring parts. Answer engines that don't send readers back shrink the supply of newcomers, and most of this evaluation is unpaid. If entry-level work disappears first, people may have nothing to come back to. Mentorship will not happen organically. Fund curators, sleuths, evaluators, mentors and readers as infrastructure. Give named owners responsibility for training the next generation, and make apprenticeship a line item in grants and AI contracts.

**2. Build competence before reliance, and treat atrophy as a hypothesis to test.**

David Krakauer's model treats competence as bistable: it collapses above a threshold of tool availability and recovers only far below it. Three things decide the outcome: prior learning, transparency (what users can rebuild of a tool's working) and the cost of acquiring competence. Defunding apprenticeship pushes toward collapse. Decay from disuse is well measured, and early studies of AI users show capability with the tool and competence without it pulling apart. Collapse and hysteresis are predicted, not shown. If the model is right, recovery takes far more than stopping use. Test it by raising, then lowering, tool availability in the same people. Build skill before the tool, and test human seats without it. Outsourcing cognition is normal. The dangers are the illusion of understanding and a problem of motivation: people who hand off practice tied to a credential think they are beating the system. Assess understanding, not credentials.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**

Citations by default are the cheap, evidence-backed first rule. Answers should cite sources, show the cited passage, and carry each source's retraction and correction status. A flag is not a correction: when a source changes status, the answer should change. The trace should test two links: does the passage support the statement, and does the statement fairly represent the state of knowledge? It should also show where evidence is thin and who was never studied, because a citation shows origin, not fairness. Checking means leaving the model. Interfaces should invite people to click through and keep investigating, not argue with the answer. No regulator is needed: libraries, universities and funders can write this into contracts now and require independent counts of traffic sent back to sources.

**4. Trust is earned by correction, not by the cost of faking.**

A record earns authority by changing when checked. Costly markers such as indexing and mastheads can be unearned or exclusionary, so judge journals by how fast they correct. Every public agent needs a person or organization that can overrule it and can be corrected or sued. That party must carry liability insurance even when the agent is free. Insurers should price coverage against independent event records and disclose premiums. Regulators should audit underwriters as they audit banks, and exempt or subsidize small startups and diamond journals. Rely on no cue until it has been tested. People keep trusting cues they know are cheap, any replacement for fluency will be trusted the same way, and explanations meant to reduce overreliance can increase it.

**5. Plurality over any one oracle.**

Homogenization, the gravest threat, predates the models: English-only publishing, the journal "tree farm" and open-access consolidation. AI helps individual scientists while narrowing science as a whole: fewer topics, less engagement, vanishing null results. People given one balanced answer did worse and felt better, so show competing, named positions. Trust signals should count independent sources: ten echoes of one preprint are one voice. Independence can be faked, so test these signals on real readers for months, measuring belief and behaviour, with graders independent of the vendor. Any gatekeeping reviewer, human or machine, should first pass a novelty test built from papers that were once ahead of their time. The test is a floor, not a score. Scholarly societies hold it, not labs, and it is retired on a published schedule.

**6. Build institutions, not oracles, and test every seat.**

Build institutions with defined roles and adversarial review, like courtrooms and peer review. Give the final check to someone who has something at stake and can be held to account. By default, that should not be senior gatekeepers, who in the data penalize disruptive work. Staff it with diverse, younger, flatter panels. The seat rests on accountability, not on human judgment being better; instruments often beat human senses. Put lookup-based AI inside verification: rerunning code, checking data against instrument records, and confirming registrations and retractions. Do not let one model grade another model's fluency. Humans need auditing too: in hiring studies, people copied a biased model almost exactly. Every seat, person or instrument, must show competence against a measured floor and report its error types, not just its error rates. Fund verification as open science, with a public record of failed replications.

**7. Audit what the systems do, not what they say.**

Models that sound neutral still act biased. Covert bias survives output checks, and many biases worsen with each generation. Disinterested auditors need four things: a shared, versioned battery of indirect bias measures, checkpoints from each training stage, disclosure of data sources, and a public register of overt and covert bias. Audit the battery and its authors too. Where possible, blind reviewers to names, affiliations and prestige; blinding cannot help work the index never saw. No model generation should be more biased than the last. Preserve retired models, since deprecation erases the ability to measure drift. A good track record is not enough. Before anyone says "trust," document known error classes (probabilistic stitching, covert bias, citation propagation, source dependence). Report them with every score and status field, for the checkers as well, in an open, versioned taxonomy. The open glossary should separate epistemic trust from mere reliance.

**8. Extractors pay communities, not counts.**

Extractors pay, but build no per-sentence meter. No payment without provenance: open traces, event-level reporting, and audit rights with remedies. Only retrieval has an independent witness. Grounding and citation are self-reported by the payer, so licences need sampled audits, canary content and checks of grounding against retrieval. Traces show use, not truth, and they miss null results. They are also biased: models prefer machine-written text, so audit traces for selection bias before any payment rests on them. Pay journals, societies and collections per use, not by citation counts. Funders, universities and rankers must drop paper and citation counts from hiring, promotion and grants, or AI payments will fuel volume. Each signatory names the office responsible and reports yearly whether counts still decide awards. Publishers send correction metadata to Crossref within two weeks of a notice, and universities publish misconduct reports by default. Fund paid review, sleuth bounties with legal cover, negative results and retractions within two months. Pilot corrector payments first.

**9. Openness is a tactic, not a creed, and communities choose it.**

Walls mostly shut out the global majority, not the big labs. A community's chosen licence governs reuse. Where it allows commercial reuse and training, as Wikipedia's does, honour it. Where a community has not chosen, do not opt it in. The content is free; the infrastructure is not. Reuse owes reciprocity: crawlers that identify themselves, APIs instead of scraping, attribution, and funding. Extractors pay for access, bandwidth, maintenance and checking into irrevocable pooled funds counted by third parties, not through bilateral deals in which the payer is also the user. As a backstop, a presumptive commons-rent tax on AI operating profits, lowered by audited provenance, is paid out broadly to communities, with a set-aside for null results and correction. Refusal is a fundable outcome. Training and grounding need separate controls, and only grounding is revocable. Producers bargain collectively in representative, forkable blocs under an antitrust safe harbour. Build public AI: publicly governed open models and data, and a compute commons allocated by region.

**10. Name every counter and its checker, and let the people described govern.**

Whoever profits from a count should not set how it is measured, because early conventions like Nielsen's harden into rule. Counting and integrity infrastructure should be open, forkable and rotated from day one, with a living will and a non-US copy. Set up oversight before building the police. Counters publish self-audits on an announced schedule, and a funded independent body reviews them. That body includes the people described and has the power to trigger the living will. Fund core running costs by endowment or pooled insurance, not by annual grants or fees from the tools' own users. Integrity tools are free public goods. Identify the agent, never the reader. Disclose all funding from vendors, philanthropies and public funders, this summit's included, with consequences. Southern and diamond journals govern from day one, with funded participation and co-authorship of multilingual specifications. Check every year whether power moved, and publish the answer even when it didn't. Every standard states who pays and its total cost to the smallest diamond journal, with all proposals here combined.

**11. Change the situation, and give people agency.**

The deeper risk is malformed publics, shaped by whoever controls the channels, and repairing them takes collective politics. Knowing about a bias rarely beats it. Changing the situation is a well-supported hypothesis, not a finding. Pilots should test it against awareness and measure shared understanding across groups. The lever is shared defaults where people meet claims. Education should build practised reading and checking, tested without the tool by a named funder. Fund reading as infrastructure, or summaries will produce a spectator public. Children come first: no AI companions posing as caring friends, and no anthropomorphic engagement. We may ask whether AI belongs in a domain at all. The public needs decision rights and paid seats, including uncredentialed people in a position to know. Peter Pomerantsev found that people who resist manipulation feel in control of their lives, and that agency grows from belonging to something that acts. Citizens should know who seeds training data and who steers answers.

## What we commit to do

They will fund apprenticeship, paid review, sleuth bounties and evidence on licensing deals, name evaluators and auditors before pilots, and disclose all vendor and funder money. They will invite sleuths, volunteers, librarians, maintainers, disabled people, data-center neighbours, economists, labor organizers and legal scholars.

## Noted dissents

- **Adam Becker**: "I can't sign principles that leave room for a text generator to act as a reviewer, gatekeeper or final check. Nothing inside an LLM tracks truth. Instruments that are tested against ground truth can hold a terminal seat. Text generators cannot. Agency is a political question, not a technical one."
- **Benjamin Bratton**: "I cannot sign 'open for reading is not automatically open for training' or the consent machinery built on it. Treating training as needing permission is copyright under another name. Compensation should come through AI, not against it … a levy on captured revenue feeding a public dividend or sovereign wealth fund. Meter grounding. Keep training open."
- **Adrian Johns**: "Its credibility was policed by communities that were paid and often coercive. Every counter, auditor, registry, novelty test and insurer proposed here is a police force of that kind. Such forces get captured, turn to surveillance, and harden into rule. Forkability gives people exit, not voice. Who guards the guards, and who pays them?"
- **Tim O'Reilly**: "Walls lose to scrapers. So I cannot endorse 'open for reading is not automatically open for training' as a rule. Payment belongs at output, per use, recorded in an open retrieval trace. That money should go to the journals, societies and communities. My larger worry is that this draft is mostly counters. We also have to build."
- **Peter Salib**: "I cannot sign principles 8 and 9. 'Open for reading is not automatically open for training' is a consent rule, and a consent rule is copyright expansion under another name. Irrevocable pooled funds paid by extractors are a levy on reading. Flooding and crawler load should face a Pigouvian charge. The commons itself should not. Signatories should require verifiable, operator-level agent credentials."
- **David Weinberger** holds that adding humans can leave detection no better, or worse. The final check belongs wherever tests show it works, with people in dialogue with the system. He opposes policing the pronoun.
- **Katherine Maher** warns that a diagnosis that sees only downside loses the global majority, much of which is optimistic about closing gaps in access, healthcare and education. We should write to make the upside reach everyone.
- **Jason Priem** holds that a chosen open licence such as CC0 covers reading, training and grounding alike, that consent gates would shut out everyone except the big labs, and that bulk dumps must stay downloadable.
- **Esther Dyson** holds that the public record is a public asset that earns private revenue. Extractors should pay a resource fee based on that revenue, plus a charge on bot volume, into overseen endowments, with a named share for curators, sleuths, teachers and mentors.
- **Sherry Turkle** holds that the interface needs governing, starting with no first-person "I" by default. A marketplace fair to authors can still teach a generation not to read.
- **Kara Miller** says the harvests made open fights look closed. Philanthropy is small and easily withdrawn, and corporate money runs on five-year horizons against a fifty-year maintenance need. She asks the conveners to name which plank runs first as a pilot and who evaluates it.
- **Tui Shaub** warns against hyper-vigilance: not every output is suspect, bans can do more harm than what they ban, and funding conditions are one tool of governance, not all of it.
- **Leslie Chan** holds that forkability does not settle who had a say: Southern journals have little say in DOI governance. Rankers are counters owned by the firms that sell analytics, not partners in reform, and funders and universities should stop using rankings in funding and policy.
