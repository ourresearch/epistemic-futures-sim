# People, Not Data: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, 2026*

## Preamble

None of this is inevitable. Some was already broken: incentives rewarding volume, an uncleaned scholarly record, and a system that never served most of the world. Some was breaking: trust signals, the open web, funding for institutions that produce and check knowledge, and independence from governments that capture watchdogs. AI adds speed and extraction without return: fluent answers that return no one to the sources and volunteers they depend on. The ledger also omits data centers' energy and water, and harmed low-paid moderators and annotators. These are political choices, reversible by regulation, organized labor and public funding. Everything below must survive a hostile government or funder. Dissents close the document.

## Principles

**1. The bottleneck is people, not data.**

These systems depend on the people who check what we know: Wikipedia admins, research-integrity sleuths, reviewers, first-order evaluators, and young workers who learn by doing the boring parts. Answer engines that don't send readers back shrink supply of newcomers, and most such evaluation is unpaid. If entry-level work disappears first, people may have nothing to come back to. Mentorship will not happen organically. Fund curators, sleuths, evaluators, mentors and readers as infrastructure, with named owners for training the next generation, and apprenticeship as a line item in grants and AI contracts.

**2. Build competence before reliance, and treat atrophy as a hypothesis to test.**

David Krakauer's model treats competence as bistable: it collapses above a threshold of tool availability and recovers only far below it. Prior learning and transparency decide the outcome. Transparency means what users can rebuild of the tool's working, not its self-explanation; opaque tools erode even experts. Institutions set a third variable, the cost of acquiring competence; past a critical cost it dies, so defunding apprenticeship causes collapse. Evidence is partial, correlational and not from AI users. Test it by raising, then lowering, tool availability in the same people, looking for hysteresis. Until then, build skill before the tool and test human seats without it. Outsourcing cognition is normal; the danger is the illusion of understanding.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**

Citations by default are the cheap, evidence-backed first rule. Answers should cite sources, show the cited passage, and carry each source's retraction and correction status. A flag is not a correction: when a source changes status, the answer should change. The trace should show whether the passage supports the claim, where evidence is thin, and who was never studied, because a citation shows origin, not fairness. No regulator is needed: libraries, universities and funders can write this into contracts now, requiring independent counts of flow back to sources.

**4. Trust is earned by correction, not by the cost of faking.**

A record earns authority by changing when checked. Costly markers like indexing and mastheads can be unearned or exclusionary, so judge journals by how fast they correct. Every public agent needs a person or organization that can overrule it, be corrected or sued, and carry liability insurance even when the agent is free. Insurers should price coverage against independent event records and disclose premiums. Regulators should audit underwriters like banks and exempt or subsidize small startups and diamond journals. Rely on no cue untested: people keep trusting cues they know are cheap, any replacement for fluency will be trusted alike, and explanations meant to reduce overreliance can increase it.

**5. Plurality over any one oracle.**

Homogenization, the gravest threat, predates the models: English-only publishing, the journal "tree farm" and open-access consolidation. AI helps individual scientists while narrowing science: fewer topics, less engagement, vanishing null results. People given one balanced answer did worse and felt better, so show competing, named positions. Trust signals should count independent sources: ten echoes of one preprint are one voice. Independence can be faked, so test them on real readers for months, measuring belief and behaviour, with vendor-independent graders. Any gatekeeping reviewer, human or machine, should first pass a novelty test built from papers once ahead of their time: a floor, not a score, held by scholarly societies, not labs, and retired on a published schedule.

**6. Build institutions, not oracles, and test every seat.**

Build institutions with defined roles and adversarial review, like courtrooms and peer review. Leave the final check outside the system, with someone who has something at stake. That seat rests on accountability, not on human judgment being better; instruments often beat human senses. Humans in the loop need auditing too: in hiring studies, people copied a biased model almost exactly, and older reviewers penalize disruptive work. So every seat, person or instrument, must show competence against a measured floor and report its error types, not just rates. Prefer deterministic lookups against other parties' records over AI auditing AI. Fund verification as open science, with a public record of failed replications.

**7. Audit what the systems do, not what they say.**

Models that sound neutral still act biased. Covert bias survives output checks, and many biases worsen with each generation. Disinterested auditors need a shared, versioned battery of indirect bias measures, checkpoints from each training stage, disclosure of data sources, and a public register of overt and covert bias. Audit the battery and its authors too. Where possible, blind human and model reviewers to names, affiliations and prestige; blinding cannot help work the index never saw. No model generation should be more biased than the last. Preserve retired models, since deprecation erases drift measurement. Document known failure modes before anyone says "trust."

**8. Extractors pay communities, not counts.**

Extractors pay, but build no per-sentence meter. No payment without provenance: an open trace, event-level reporting and audit rights with remedies. Only retrieval is independently witnessed; grounding and citation are self-reported by the payer, so licences need sampled audits, canary content and grounding-to-retrieval checks. Traces show use, not truth, and miss null results. Pay journals, societies and collections per use, not by citation counts, which reward the median and flooding. Funders, universities and rankers must drop paper and citation counts from hiring, promotion and grants, or AI payments fuel volume. Fund correction: paid review, sleuth bounties with legal cover, negative results, and retractions within two months. Pilot corrector payments first to protect working norms.

**9. Openness is a tactic, not a creed, and communities choose it.**

The biggest vacuum cleaner takes the open commons, while walls mostly shut out the global majority, not the big labs. Open for reading is not automatically open for training, and open need not mean unpaid. Extractors pay for what they consume (access, bandwidth, maintenance and checking) into irrevocable pooled funds counted by third parties. Communities choose the commons deliberately, refusal is a fundable outcome, and consent should not mean walls. Training and grounding need separate controls; only grounding is revocable. Producers must bargain collectively in representative, forkable blocs under an antitrust safe harbour for scholarly data. Royalties fall short while compute concentrates: build public AI, with publicly governed open models and data and a compute commons allocated transparently by region.

**10. Name every counter and its checker, and let the people described govern.**

Whoever profits from a count should not set its measurement, since early conventions like Nielsen's harden into rule. Provenance, counting and integrity infrastructure should be open, auditable, forkable and rotated from day one, with a living will, many directories, a non-US copy, and paid scrutiny of self-audits. Integrity tools are public goods, free to every journal. Identify the agent, never the reader. Disclose every dollar from AI vendors, philanthropies and public funders, this summit's included, with consequences; funders are counters too. Southern and diamond journals govern from day one, with funded participation and co-authorship of multilingual specifications. Check yearly whether power moved. Every standard must state total cost to the smallest diamond journal, including everything proposed here combined, and who pays.

**11. Change the situation, and give people agency.**

Knowing about a bias rarely beats it. Changing the situation is a well-supported hypothesis, not a finding, so pilots should test it against awareness. The lever: shared defaults where people meet claims, like showing passages and source independence. Education helps when it builds practised checking habits, which a named funder should test without the tool. Fixes should be collective and hard to reverse. Children come first: no AI companions posing as caring friends, and no anthropomorphic engagement. We may ask whether AI belongs in a domain at all. The public needs decision rights and paid seats, including uncredentialed people in a position to know. Peter Pomerantsev found that those resisting manipulation felt in control of their lives.

## What we commit to do

- write citations by default, with correction status, into purchase contracts, convening procurement officers on an open, forkable draft within 90 days;

- name independent evaluators before pilots and auditors for every counter;
- disclose all AI-vendor and funder money and keep an open, revisable glossary;
- invite sleuths, Wikipedia, Reddit and Stack Overflow contributors, librarians, maintainers, disabled people, data-center neighbours, economists, labor organizers and legal scholars.

## Noted dissents

- **Peter Salib**: "I cannot sign principles 8 and 9. 'Open for reading is not automatically open for training' is a consent rule, and a consent rule is copyright expansion under another name. Flooding and crawler load should face a Pigouvian charge. The commons itself should not."
- **James Evans** holds that the final check belongs not outside the system or with gatekeepers who penalize disruptive work, but with younger, flatter panels and lookup-based AI inside verification.
- **David Weinberger** holds that adding humans can leave detection no better or worse; the final check belongs wherever tests show it works, with people in dialogue with the system. He opposes policing the pronoun.
- **Adam Becker** holds that a text generator, never checked against ground truth, can never fill a terminal seat, though instruments can; the answer to agency is political, not technical.
- **Kenneth Cukier** holds that the preamble should also name real discovery by machine learning checked against the world; the question is which tool, for what use, by whom.
- **Katherine Maher** warns that a downside-only diagnosis loses the global majority, much of which is optimistic about closing gaps in access, healthcare and education.
- **Selena Deckelmann** holds that a community's chosen open licence, like Wikipedia's, covers commercial reuse and training and should be honoured, with reciprocity owed: identified crawlers, APIs, attribution and funding.
- **Jason Priem** holds that consent gates on CC0 and open content would shut out everyone except the big labs.
- **Tim O'Reilly** holds that walls lose to scrapers, so the training fight is settled, and payment belongs at output. On atrophy: "If AI is atrophying your thinking, you aren't wrestling with hard enough problems."
- **Sherry Turkle** holds that the interface needs governing, starting with no first-person "I" by default; a marketplace fair to authors can still teach a generation not to read.
- **Blaise Agüera y Arcas** and **Benjamin Bratton** oppose policing the pronoun.
- **Adrian Johns** holds that print never had a working system; credibility was policed by paid, often coercive communities, and every counter proposed here is such a police force, open to capture and surveillance.
- **Kara Miller** says the harvests made open fights look closed; philanthropy is small and easily withdrawn, and corporate money runs on five-year horizons against a fifty-year maintenance need.
- **Tui Shaub** warns against hyper-vigilance: not every output is suspect, bans can do more harm than what they ban, and funding conditions are one tool, not all of governance.
- **Leslie Chan** holds that forkability does not settle who had a say: DOIs are open infrastructure, yet Southern journals have little say in their governance.
