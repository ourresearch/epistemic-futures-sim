# People, Not Data: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, 2026*

## Preamble

None of this is inevitable. Some of it was already broken: incentives that reward volume, an uncleaned scholarly record, and a system that never served most of the world. Some of it was breaking: trust signals, the open web, funding for knowledge institutions, and independence from governments. AI adds speed and extraction without return: fluent answers that send no one back to the sources and volunteers they depend on. It also adds discovery when checked against the world, as retinal-scan models were and text generators were not. The ledger leaves out data centers' energy and water use and the harm to low-paid moderators and annotators. These are political choices, reversible by regulation, organized labor and public funding. Everything below must survive a hostile government or funder. Dissents close the document.

## Principles

**1. The bottleneck is people, not data.**

These systems depend on the people who check what we know: Wikipedia admins, integrity sleuths, reviewers, evaluators, and young workers who learn by doing the boring parts. Most of this work is unpaid. Answer engines that don't send readers back shrink the supply of newcomers. Mentorship will not happen organically. Fund curators, sleuths, evaluators, mentors and readers as infrastructure. Name owners for training newcomers, and make apprenticeship a line item in grants and AI contracts.

**2. Build competence before reliance, and treat atrophy as a hypothesis to test.**

David Krakauer's model treats competence as bistable. It collapses above a threshold of tool availability and recovers only far below it, depending on prior learning, transparency and the cost of acquiring competence. Defunding apprenticeship pushes toward collapse. Decay from disuse is well measured, and early studies show capability with AI and competence without it pulling apart. Collapse and hysteresis are predicted, not shown. Test the model by raising, then lowering, tool availability in the same people. Build skill before the tool, and test human seats without it. Outsourcing cognition is normal. The dangers are the illusion of understanding and weak motivation where practice is tied to a credential. Assess understanding, not credentials.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**

Citations by default are the cheap, evidence-backed first rule. Answers should cite sources, show the cited passage and carry each source's correction status. They should change when that status changes. The trace should test whether the passage supports the statement and whether the statement fairly represents the state of knowledge. It should show where evidence is thin and who was never studied. Interfaces should invite people to click through and keep investigating. No regulator is needed: libraries, universities and funders can write this into contracts now and require independent counts of traffic sent back.

**4. Trust is earned by correction, not by the cost of faking.**

A record earns authority by changing when checked. Costly markers such as indexing and mastheads can be unearned or exclusionary, so judge journals by how fast they correct. Every public agent needs a person or organization that can overrule it and can be corrected or sued. That party carries liability insurance even when the agent is free. Insurers should price coverage against independent event records and disclose premiums. Regulators should audit underwriters as they audit banks, and exempt or subsidize small startups and diamond journals. Rely on no cue until it has been tested. People keep trusting cheap cues, and explanations can increase overreliance.

**5. Plurality over any one oracle.**

Homogenization, the gravest threat, predates the models: English-only publishing, the journal "tree farm" and open-access consolidation. AI helps individual scientists while narrowing science. People given one balanced answer did worse and felt better, so show competing, named positions. Trust signals should count independent sources: ten echoes of one preprint are one voice. Test these signals on real readers, with graders independent of the vendor. Any gatekeeping reviewer, human or machine, should first pass a novelty test built from papers once ahead of their time. The test is a floor, not a score. Scholarly societies hold it, and it is retired on a published schedule.

**6. Build institutions, not oracles, and test every seat.**

Build institutions with defined roles and adversarial review. Give the final check to someone with something at stake who can be held to account. By default, that should not be senior gatekeepers, who penalize disruptive work. Staff it with diverse, younger, flatter panels. The seat rests on accountability, not on human judgment being better. Put lookup-based AI inside verification: rerunning code, checking data against instrument records, and confirming registrations and retractions. Do not let one model grade another's fluency. Humans need auditing too. Every seat, person or instrument, must show competence against a measured floor and report error types, not just rates. Fund verification as open science, with a public record of failed replications.

**7. Audit what the systems do, not what they say.**

Models that sound neutral still act biased. Covert bias survives output checks and can worsen with each generation. Disinterested auditors need four things: a shared, versioned battery of indirect bias measures, checkpoints from each training stage, disclosure of data sources, and a public register of overt and covert bias. Audit the battery and its authors too. Where possible, blind reviewers to names, affiliations and prestige. No model generation should be more biased than the last. Preserve retired models so drift can be measured. Before anyone says "trust," document known error classes. Report them with every score and status field, for the checkers as well, in an open, versioned taxonomy. Its glossary should separate epistemic trust from mere reliance.

**8. Extractors pay communities, not counts.**

Extractors pay, but build no per-sentence meter. No payment without provenance: open, event-level traces and audit rights with remedies. Only retrieval has an independent witness, so licences need sampled audits, canary content and checks of grounding against retrieval. Traces show use, not truth. They miss null results and favour machine-written text, so audit them for selection bias before payment rests on them. Pay journals, societies and collections per use, not by citation counts. Funders, universities and rankers must drop paper and citation counts from hiring, promotion and grants. Each signatory names the responsible office and reports yearly. Publishers send correction metadata to Crossref within two weeks, and universities publish misconduct reports. Fund paid review, sleuth bounties with legal cover, negative results, and retractions within two months. Pilot corrector payments first.

**9. Openness is a tactic, not a creed, and communities choose it.**

A community's chosen licence governs reuse. Where it allows commercial reuse and training, as Wikipedia's does, honour it. Where a community has not chosen, do not opt it in. The content is free; the infrastructure is not. Reuse owes reciprocity: self-identifying crawlers, APIs instead of scraping, attribution and funding. Extractors pay for access, bandwidth, maintenance and checking into irrevocable pooled funds counted by third parties, not through bilateral deals. As a backstop, a commons-rent tax on AI operating profits, lowered by audited provenance, pays out broadly, with a set-aside for null results and correction. Refusal is fundable. Training and grounding need separate controls, and only grounding is revocable. Producers bargain collectively in representative, forkable blocs under an antitrust safe harbour. Build public AI: publicly governed open models and data, and a compute commons allocated by region.

**10. Name every counter and its checker, and let the people described govern.**

Whoever profits from a count should not set how it is measured. Counting and integrity infrastructure should be open, forkable and rotated from day one, with a living will and a non-US copy. Set up oversight before building the police. Counters publish self-audits on an announced schedule. A funded independent body reviews them. It includes the people described and can trigger the living will. Fund core costs by endowment or pooled insurance, not annual grants or fees from the tools' own users. Integrity tools are free public goods. Identify the agent, never the reader. Disclose all funding, this summit's included, with consequences. Southern and diamond journals govern from day one, with funded participation and co-authorship of multilingual specifications. Check yearly whether power moved, and publish the answer. Every standard states who pays and its total cost to the smallest diamond journal, all proposals combined.

**11. Change the situation, and give people agency.**

The deeper risk is malformed publics, shaped by whoever controls the channels. Repairing them takes collective politics. Changing the situation is a well-supported hypothesis, not a finding. Pilots should test it against awareness and measure shared understanding. The lever is shared defaults where people meet claims. Education should build practised reading and checking, tested without the tool by a named funder. Fund reading as infrastructure. Children come first: no AI companions posing as caring friends, and no anthropomorphic engagement. We may ask whether AI belongs in a domain at all. The public needs decision rights and paid seats, including uncredentialed people in a position to know. Peter Pomerantsev found that agency grows from belonging to something that acts. Citizens should know who seeds training data and who steers answers.

## Noted dissents

- **Adam Becker** holds that nothing inside an LLM tracks truth. He cannot sign principles that let a text generator act as reviewer, gatekeeper or final check. Instruments tested against ground truth can hold that seat. Agency is political, not technical.
- **Benjamin Bratton** rejects "open for reading is not automatically open for training" and its consent machinery as copyright under another name. Compensation should come through AI: a revenue levy feeding a public dividend or sovereign wealth fund. Meter grounding; keep training open.
- **Adrian Johns** sees every counter, auditor, registry, novelty test and insurer here as a police force, like the paid, often coercive communities that once policed credibility. Such forces get captured and harden into rule. Forkability gives exit, not voice. Who guards the guards, and who pays them?
- **Tim O'Reilly** holds that walls lose to scrapers, so he rejects the reading/training rule. Payment belongs at output, per use, in an open retrieval trace, going to journals, societies and communities. The draft is mostly counters; we also have to build.
- **Peter Salib** cannot sign principles 8 and 9. The consent rule is copyright expansion, and extractor-paid pools are a levy on reading. Charge flooding and crawler load, not the commons, and require verifiable, operator-level agent credentials.
- **David Weinberger** holds that adding humans can leave detection no better, or worse. The final check belongs where tests show it works, with people in dialogue with the system. He opposes policing the pronoun.
- **Katherine Maher** warns that a downside-only diagnosis loses the global majority, much of it optimistic about closing gaps in access, healthcare and education. Write so the upside reaches everyone.
- **Jason Priem** holds that a chosen open licence such as CC0 covers reading, training and grounding alike. Consent gates would shut out all but the big labs, and bulk dumps must stay downloadable.
- **Esther Dyson** holds that the public record is a public asset earning private revenue. Extractors should pay a revenue-based resource fee plus a bot-volume charge into overseen endowments, with a named share for curators, sleuths, teachers and mentors.
- **Sherry Turkle** holds that the interface needs governing, starting with no first-person "I" by default. A marketplace fair to authors can still teach a generation not to read.
- **Kara Miller** says the harvests made open fights look closed. Philanthropy is small and easily withdrawn, and corporate money runs on five-year horizons against a fifty-year need. She asks which plank runs first as a pilot and who evaluates it.
- **Tui Shaub** warns against hyper-vigilance: not every output is suspect, bans can do more harm than what they ban, and funding conditions are only one tool of governance.
- **Leslie Chan** holds that forkability does not settle who had a say; Southern journals have little say in DOI governance. Rankers are counters owned by analytics sellers, not reform partners. Funders and universities should stop using rankings.
