# Harvest — Session 3: Knowledge Infrastructure and Tools: Commons, Enclosure, and Sovereignty

1. **Forkability and open dumps are necessary but not sufficient. The room asked that no body count what it profits from, and that Southern and diamond journals govern from day one.** Johns, Bly, Priem, Banaji, Springer and Vincent called for provenance and counting infrastructure in a neutral, forkable home, audited by parties with no margin on the count. Bratton, Farrell and O'Reilly called for federation or open protocols. Chan, Bilder and Shaub called for decision-rights seats for Southern and diamond journals, with Shaub proposing to enforce this through public funding conditions and Bilder noting the home should not default to the US. Dyson and Bilder warned that seats go unused without funded participation, Gendler that federated registries checking each other still need terminal human checkers, and Evans that an open commons drawing from one well is still a monoculture.

2. **Unresolved: whether training on the open record should stay free.** Bratton, Weinberger, Cukier and O'Reilly argued to keep training open and not meter the record. Becker, Chan, Dash, Hecht, Vincent and Brand countered that unfettered openness mostly benefits whoever owns the compute, and asked for consent or opt-in and for extractors to pay. Deckelmann noted that Wikipedia's editors chose a license that permits training.

3. **There was broad agreement against a per-sentence meter and for extractors paying, but not on the mechanism.** The proposals were:
   * Dyson: a resource fee on AI revenue.
   * Salib, dissenting: pay for depletable services by contract, since reading does not use up the record, plus a Pigouvian charge on crawler load.
   * Vincent: a tax on unexplained capability.
   * O'Reilly, Springer and Agüera y Arcas: meter per-query grounding while paying for training coarsely.

   Farrell, Hecht, Salib, Vincent, Maher and Brand agreed producers will have to bargain collectively, under an antitrust safe harbour.

4. **The bottleneck is people, not data.** Krakauer, Deckelmann, Gendler, Oransky, Turkle and Miller argued that curators, admins, sleuths, first-order evaluators and readers themselves must be funded as infrastructure, with named owners for training the next generation. Gendler and Sloman cautioned that paying volunteer correctors could change their motives and should be piloted and measured first.

5. **The room proposed reader-facing conditions for AI tools and schemas that libraries could write into contracts now.** Caulfield, Dash, Maher and Miller asked that a cited passage be readable. Deckelmann and Springer asked that return traffic be measured. Alvarado, Sloman and Banaji asked for error-type and source-dependence fields, and Oransky for prompt retraction reporting. Sloman and Turkle warned that any displayed cue can be trusted without being earned, and Sloman asked that such displays be tested before they become standard.

## Minority positions

**Adrian Johns**
- There was never a golden age of trusted print, so the concept note's arc of cheap signal, panic, then durable institutions is wrong. Trust in print was imposed by the scientific community and has had to be enforced continuously ever since.
- Private anti-piracy police may have been right and still answered to nobody, and at least one was reportedly turned to tracking dissidents. (The harvest lists Johns as calling for a neutral, forkable home. He posed the three questions and asked that the answer to "who guards the guards" be built in from the start, but did not propose that remedy himself.)

**Adam Bly**
- Funding a public commons from enterprise licenses is a real model, but a self-written annual benefit statement is not adequate oversight. The schema standard itself should be funded by public or philanthropic money, so the police are not paid by the people they watch.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- The line that matters now runs between US, China and EU stacks, not between publishers and labs. National "sovereign AI" and data autarchy lead to capture, so the commons worth defending is open weights and access across those blocs.

**Jason Priem**
- Community corrections should themselves be released as an open dataset. For years universities have done unpaid QA for Scopus and Web of Science, which then sell the results back to libraries.
- His own API usage fees could squeeze the global-majority users he says he serves, so he wants endowments, not just service revenue.

**David Weinberger**
- Openness alone does not produce plurality. Open sources whose metadata does not interoperate are silos in practice.

**David Krakauer**
- His model has two stable population states, and above a critical cost of acquiring competence, the competent state stops existing. Unpaid corrections are how people build judgment, so automating them away removes the people who can check the record.
- A finding-level schema must stay a traceable lookup. If it is collapsed into a single score, it becomes impact metrics again.

**Tui Shaub**
- Philanthropy is not neutral money. Wellcome's investment did not stop the BY-NC drift, and OpenAlex's funding never fixed its board.
- Federation should mean mutual recognition on a thin set of shared principles, not uniform rules.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Human judgment should not be treated as better by default, since sometimes instruments outperform people. Human seats should rest on stakes, accountability and competence that can be shown.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Auditors believe themselves immune to bias they see in others, so trust must rest on structure, not character. No one's corrections, including correctors', annotators' and funders', should become a gold standard without being audited.
- Retired models and training checkpoints should be preserved, with a shared, versioned test battery for independent auditors. Her data shows bias worsening across model generations, and deprecation destroys the ability to measure that.

**Adam Becker**
- Every registry, schema and index should disclose every dollar, including AI-company money. A public dividend should come from taxing those who took the record, since opening the record to machines and taxing wealth are not the same policy.

**Mike Caulfield**
- The schema should include a field for whether the cited passage actually supports the claim, not just that it was cited.

**Leslie Chan**
- The DOI was also called neutral infrastructure, and in practice Southern institutions comply without having a say. A schema will define what counts as a finding, in which languages, and whose idea of authorship.
- He proposed a compute commons with transparent regional allocation and support for small models. Community refusal under CARE or Local Contexts protocols is governance and should be fundable. (The harvest groups Chan with those asking extractors to pay, but his stated remedy was compute access "not only royalties or a dividend.")

**Kenneth Cukier**
- There is a moral duty to use data, not only guard it. Paying per citation rewards the median and misses excellence and null results.
- Transparency should be graduated: full access for independent auditors and public authorities, not necessarily for rivals. A standing right to audit is more enforceable than blanket disclosure mandates.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- He now considers the POSI principle "revenue based on services, not data" naive. He would put lab money or Esther Dyson's fee into endowments and a pooled insurance fund rather than annual grants.
- Research integrity tools are becoming closed, expensive, gatekeeping infrastructure and should be an open public good with equitable access. He has no settled view on pricing.

**Anil Dash**
- "Open weights" is the industry's term. The Open Source Initiative's definition of open source AI requires training-data transparency, and the commons should mean that.
- Opting everyone in by default is not openness.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Crawler traffic has saturated Wikipedia's network links, and human readers are given priority. Wikipedia will not put up a pay-per-article blocker.

**Esther Dyson**
- The transparency that matters is about money flows, not weights, which cuts against Dash's and Banaji's emphasis on training data and models.
- The ICANN .ORG episode shows a "neutral" registry can nearly be sold to private equity. The living will has to hold when someone arrives with money.

**James Evans**
- The record lacks negative knowledge: what was ruled out, what failed to replicate, and dead ends. After ChatGPT, the share of abstracts with no null claims rose sharply. Collecting it through registered reports, replication archives and direct elicitation from experts should be funded as infrastructure.
- He favors collective licensing modeled on collecting societies. The citation trace should be used to map concentration and gaps, not to pay per count.

**Henry Farrell**
- Following Ostrom, commons survive when users set and revise the rules and control relations with outsiders, and downloadable copies do not provide that. A producer bloc run by incumbents is enclosure again.
- Any AI review tool funded through grants should have to show it can recognize novelty rather than filter it out.

**Tamar Gendler**
- The check should be built into the situation rather than left to readers' resolve: show the passage by default and make source independence visible in the interface.

**Brent Hecht**
- "Open" was meant as a tactic and has become a value. He asked for measurement rights, and for economists and labor scholars to be brought in before numbers are set.

**Katherine Maher**
- Proposed a model trained on public broadcasting, built with stations and broadcasters abroad. Nothing AI-generated should go out to NPR's audience.

**Kara Miller**
- Philanthropy is small, and corporate and venture money operate on five-year horizons against a fifty-year maintenance need. She doubts a resource fee will ever be enacted, which cuts against relying on the mechanisms in harvest point 3.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- The open-versus-closed fight over training is effectively settled because walls lose to scrapers. Payment belongs at output, especially output that competes with the original. Lump-sum training deals entrench the biggest labs. (The harvest says he favored paying for training coarsely. He did not say that; he opposed training deals.)
- Labs should fund Crossref, OpenAlex, ORCID and Retraction Watch out of self-interest, and centralization should be avoided early.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Research-integrity sleuths already exist, work unpaid, and are sometimes sued. Lab money should fund bug bounties for them and pay reviewers.
- As long as funders count papers and citations, the infrastructure proposals are "flood insurance." The incentives upstream have to change.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- "Information sovereignty" is Kremlin and Beijing language for a firewall, and should not be adopted. Registries should target covert and deceptive shaping of the record, such as the Pravda network, not foreign sources.
- Any fee should fund broad civic participation, "a thousand civic spaces," not just experts auditing experts. That cuts against the expert-centered framing of harvest point 4.

**Peter Salib**
- Disagreed with Hecht: frontier models are matched in about six months at a fraction of the cost, so this is not a natural monopoly. Worker displacement belongs in general tax policy, not in intellectual property.
- Agents that write into the record should be subject to know-your-customer identity checks on their operators, with no surveillance of readers.

**Steven Sloman**
- Experts contribute because they want to be right and to be acknowledged, so their credit should be made visible.

**Alex Springer**
- His telemetry standard carries signals, not prices, with deliberately no formula. (The harvest lists him under metering grounding.) Retention periods should be mandated and binding, and his own consortium's governance is not yet written.
- In his audit, 7.8% of citations came from sites blocking training bots versus 2.1% from sites blocking search bots, which he called a consent failure that robots.txt cannot resolve.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Every payment scheme discussed still pays out at the moment a summary replaces reading. Reading itself, meaning libraries, classes and time, should be in the manifesto as infrastructure.

**Nick Vincent**
- His capability tax can be reduced to zero by showing licensed, governed or reciprocal sources, so it pushes labs toward contracts rather than replacing them. Auditors should be paid from the fund, rotated, and publish forkable methods.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Training is untraceable recombination, like human originality, so per-token royalties claim a precision nobody has. The underlying threat is inequality among humans, which calls for plurality in the models themselves, not only in indexes.

**Amy Brand**
- Open access showed that a well-meant open policy rolled out fast can open public knowledge to the private sector and concentrate power in the largest publishers. In her survey of about 6,000 authors, only 3% wanted AI use left unregulated.
- She prefers short deals renegotiated every two years, with grounding at query time rather than pre-training because attribution survives retrieval. She warned that AI deals are already concentrating among the biggest publishers.
