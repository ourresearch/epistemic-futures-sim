# Harvest — Session 1: The Epistemic Landscape

1. **Homogenization is the most serious threat, and it predates the models.** Farrell ranked it first, and Evans reported evidence from 41 million papers that AI helps individual scientists while narrowing science's topics and suppressing null results. Chan, Priem, Brand, and Banaji traced the pull to earlier systems that models automate and amplify: English-only publishing, the journal "tree farm," and consolidation driven by open-access policy. Chan, Deckelmann, and Maher added that the system "was never working for most of the world." Weinberger dissented, calling homogenization a design and deployment failure rather than the technology's essence. Agüera y Arcas and Bratton located the risk in concentration among a few firms and argued for plurality and broader inclusion, while Krakauer countered that many opaque models remain opaque to the people judging them.

2. **What is new is extraction without return.** Answers that don't send people back to sources are cutting the traffic, volunteers, and donors that produce the vetted human knowledge models depend on (Deckelmann, Maher, Dash, Hecht). Vincent, Hecht, and Springer noted that extraction predates generative AI but that the value chain is now unreadable, consent mechanisms are broken, and contributions are shifting into private pools. O'Reilly's citation data showed disintermediation is a design choice. Who pays remains unresolved: Dyson proposed a levy on commons-derived revenue, Salib opposed it in favor of general tax policy and direct contracts, and Priem and Brand split on whether data open for reading should be open for training.

3. **Trust signals were already collapsing, and fluency cheapened them further, but the room did not agree that better signals are the fix.** Farrell, Becker, Caulfield, Pomerantsev, and Salib described this collapse through the dying linked web, flooding, and fake consensus. Bilder's open provenance graph drew Chan's warning, "be traceable or be invisible," and Johns's question of who maintains and polices it. Priem replied that the graph must be made comprehensive. Oransky, Bly, and Sloman added that it must record retractions, work at the level of findings, and count independent sources rather than echoes. Pomerantsev argued that belonging outweighs signals, while Caulfield replied that the realistic aim is to make bad evidence harder to use.

4. **Cognitive atrophy stays in scope as a hypothesis to test, not a finding.** Krakauer, Gendler, and Miller all took this position. Krakauer, Deckelmann, Miller, and Johns framed the concrete risk as a collapsing competence pipeline: shrinking admin pools, and young workers nobody teaches. Krakauer proposed a raise-then-lower tool-availability test for hysteresis. Sloman and Agüera y Arcas noted that outsourcing cognition is normal, and Turkle and Dyson extended the concern to reading and to the interface itself. Caulfield, Dash, Hecht, and O'Reilly asked that the manifesto include no "avoid LLMs" plank. Becker held that usefulness is not reliability and that claims of AI accelerating science are unproven. Shaub, Miller, and Oransky asked who will fund apprenticeship, verification, and integrity work.

5. **Unresolved: whether the question of what these systems are matters, and what follows for "humans in the loop."** Farrell and Dash set the ontology question aside. Agüera y Arcas and Salib argued the models are more than mirrors, and Becker asked what observation would falsify that. Alvarado proposed "epistemic technologies" and identified error opacity as what is new, and Bratton and Salib used Banaji's amplification findings against the concept note's "reflecting our intentions back." Cukier held that both views are true. Banaji, Evans, and Alvarado argued that the humans in the loop must themselves be audited and each human role justified, and Weinberger proposed designing for humans in the dialogue instead.

## Minority positions

**Henry Farrell**
- Warned against answering collapsing signals with "more robots," such as detectors or AI reviewing AI, because that road leads to an arms race. This cuts against Caulfield's call for AI tools that make refutation cheaper and Evans's call for verification "with AI inside it."
- Said organizing knowledge producers helps only if the bloc can be forked and stays accountable to its members. Otherwise it becomes enclosure.
- Said he is relatively relaxed about misinformation aimed at individuals. Dyson and Miller disagreed outright.

**Leslie Chan**
- Disputed Farrell's claim that "nobody talks about" homogenization. She said the Global South has discussed it for twenty years as the "monoculture of the mind."
- Argued the first question should be who owns the machinery that extracts value and who decides what counts as knowledge, not how to detect bad text.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Said Wikipedia's imminent death has been predicted for twenty years without happening. She asked that the diagnosis rest on trackable numbers: pageviews, new editors, retention, and donors.

**Katherine Maher**
- Cautioned against techno-doom. She cited surveys showing optimism in much of the global majority about closing gaps in access, healthcare, and education, and warned that a downside-only diagnosis loses standing with those people.
- Put political and financial attacks on knowledge institutions into the landscape: a third of US local newspapers lost, and federal funding for public media gone.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Argued the cracks were widened deliberately. Authoritarian "censorship through noise" aims to exhaust people into surrendering their judgment, not to make them believe lies.
- Named deliberate seeding of training data with propaganda as a new target, and called opacity about who shapes the summary a form of censorship. He framed the deepest cognitive surrender as civic: people become spectators to reasoning done for them.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Called the premise that a composite of billions outweighs any individual the "central dogma" of generative AI and a new behaviorism. She argued it is anti-democratic because democracies collect opinions rather than average them.

**Nick Vincent**
- Called the current state precarious rather than lost. The subsidized "club goods" labs sell back can be cut or withdrawn at the owner's discretion.

**David Weinberger**
- Proposed grading students' dialogues with AI, including where they probed and where they trusted too easily. He cited studies where adding humans made the loop less accurate.

**Jason Priem**
- Argued the question is not whether AI homogenizes a healthy system but whether it finally lets us replace an unhealthy one, the journal system. He said the real risk is enclosure, not intelligence.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Argued that content-based AI detectors impose an invisible legibility tax on translated and non-native-English work. A gap in an open graph, by contrast, can be seen and fixed.
- Warned that closed, expensive research-integrity tools are becoming a product that brands non-users' research as suspect.

**Amy Brand**
- Held that hallucination is a feature of these systems, not a bug, and that better training data won't cure it. She favored retrieval with attribution as the way to keep provenance intact.

**David Krakauer**
- Said dialogue with a model counts only if the student reconstructs and verifies what comes back. Otherwise the student becomes the tool's instrument. This qualifies Weinberger.
- Argued that if competence follows a hysteresis loop, abstaining won't restore it, and apprenticeship must be funded as infrastructure.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Disputed Pomerantsev's "identity wins." He described the web as a justification machine and said partisan identity has become easy to read while expertise has become hard to read.
- Rejected "it's all hallucination" as a conversation-ending slogan. He argued errors cluster predictably, like radar clutter, and proposed the manifesto rule "no answers from nowhere."

**Tui Shaub**
- Argued that where flattening follows old patterns it is stereotype, not lost serendipity, so friction or randomness won't fix it. She called funding conditions a governance tool and said philanthropy has funded symptoms.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Argued the manifesto should require documented failure modes before anyone says "trust," since a good track record doesn't license trust where feedback is slow.
- Named "testimonial diminishing" ("But ChatGPT said...") as a new harm that falls hardest on people who already had little credibility. He dissented from resting the human final check on human judgment being better.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Reported that many biases are getting worse with each model generation, while humans have become less biased over centuries. In hiring studies, people copied a moderately biased model almost exactly.
- Asked that older models be kept so drift can be measured.

**Adam Becker**
- Called "it's inevitable" a rhetorical move, not a finding. He said usefulness doesn't settle theft, energy costs, or the treatment of content moderators.
- Argued that when hallucinations are mitigated, the credit belongs to external checking (retrieval, fact-checkers, people), because nothing inside the model checks output against the world.

**Adam Bly**
- Proposed replacing the lossy-summary framing with grounded versus ungrounded systems. He noted that he builds a grounded system and asked the room to discount his view accordingly.
- Said provenance should show where evidence is thin or missing. He added that a citation shows origin, not fairness.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Argued the room contradicts itself by treating AI as both "just us" and "not us." The departure from us is the real phenomenon.
- Said part of the room's alarm is institutions defending their own scarcity, and that cheap access to expertise is a gain for the Global South. He opposed a small council curating a "correct" model.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Called Becker's "benefits are unproven" too broad, citing retina-scan discovery. Becker then narrowed his claim to LLMs accelerating science.
- Argued the room isn't contradicting itself: models reflect our mental models, and text is a distorting mirror.

**Anil Dash**
- Argued that much of AI's impact is coercion: mandates, forced upgrades, and assistants inserted into every tool. He said the tool must be separated from the companies deploying it.
- Named the absence of community-governed alternatives as part of the diagnosis.

**Esther Dyson**
- Argued for transparency on money flows and design goals before explainability of weights. She called sycophancy a design choice paid for by retention.
- Introduced "information diabetes," the worry that people come to think like LLMs: fluent, easy, and without understanding.

**James Evans**
- Cautioned that one shared open database makes results look more reproducible than they prove in the wild. He said "open, yes, but not a monoculture," which cuts against Priem and Bilder.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Argued that knowing doesn't change behavior. Fluency triggers trust as an alief that survives belief, so remedies must change situations through shared rules rather than rely on literacy or individual resolve.
- Warned against a sharp line between statistics and knowing minds, since much human cognition would fall on the wrong side.

**Brent Hecht**
- Disclosed that he works on Copilot at Microsoft. He argued that ChatGPT ended an implicit bargain between producers and builders, that models now compete with their own supply chain, and that data strikes seem nearly unavoidable.
- Reported that overreliance performs worse than either human or AI alone, and proposed tools designed to challenge the user.

**Adrian Johns**
- Challenged the "already breaking" framing itself: print never had a working system to crack, and credibility was built and constantly policed by communities.
- Noted recurring literacy crises since about 1920, and warned that technical authenticity fixes have served surveillance, citing Microsoft's anti-piracy work in Russia.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Took the optimist's position on atrophy: "If AI is atrophying your thinking, you aren't wrestling with hard enough problems."
- Argued labs should fund Crossref, OpenAlex, and Retraction Watch out of self-interest, and that the moment to decentralize is now.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Ranked a fourth threat above homogenization: models trained on a record never properly cleaned. He estimated retractions should run near one in fifty, not one or two in a thousand.
- Said he is less optimistic than Bilder that AI forces decoupling careers from publication counts. He wants AI money directed to unpaid, lawsuit-threatened volunteer sleuths.

**Peter Salib**
- Named fake consensus as the worst new harm and proposed proof of personhood and a Pigouvian tax on bot-generated content.
- Warned that a diagnosis assuming mere mirrors will be outrun by the systems' capabilities.

**Steven Sloman**
- Named the knowledge illusion (people feel they understand more with access they can't use) and machines sending confident cues with no shared intentionality behind them. He asked that every machine contribution answer who stands behind it.

**Alex Springer**
- Reported that publishers block the wrong bot and that licensing deals quietly override robots.txt.
- Cautioned that whoever takes a cut of the count shouldn't set how it is measured.

Attribution note on harvest item 3: the point that the graph must record retractions and corrections was made by Bilder and O'Reilly, not Oransky. Oransky's point was that models are trained on an uncleaned record.
