# Harvest — Session 2: AI as Epistemic Actor: Risks, Capabilities, and Governance

1. **Tie agents to people, but don't build a single registry.** Dyson, Salib, Turkle and Sloman supported Know Your Agent, which ties every public agent to a human or organization that can be sued. Springer, Weinberger, Bilder, Bratton, Dash, Johns and O'Reilly argued that any single registry becomes a chokepoint and favored self-held keys, many directories and federation; Johns asked who funds the registry and therefore whose interests it will serve. Bilder and Bly added that identity settles credit, not reliability, and Bilder and Caulfield said a self-registration is a cheap signal unless other parties assert the context.

2. **Reliability needs a record, not a certificate.** Springer, Alvarado, Bly, Vincent and O'Reilly called for an event record of what an agent retrieved, used and cited, and O'Reilly proposed standardized telemetry with document hashes and relevance scores. Bilder, Priem, Maher and O'Reilly said retraction and correction status must travel into answers; Springer, Hecht and Maher said the count must not be set by whoever takes a margin on it. Dash, O'Reilly and Miller named citations by default as the cheap, evidence-backed first rule.

3. **Insurance and lawsuits miss the collective harm.** Alvarado, Evans, Oransky, Farrell, Hecht and Vincent argued that premiums and suits price harm to individuals but not the narrowing of science, publishers' volume incentives, or the erosion of the unpaid human labor these systems depend on. They pointed instead to collective leverage by knowledge producers, funded correction, and an end to counting papers in hiring and funding; Banaji partly dissented, saying insurance can work if bias is measured independently with auditor access to models. Krakauer, Deckelmann and Gendler added that every mechanism assumes a competent person at the end of the chain, and that sustaining those people through apprenticeship, and building the skill before handing over the tool, is itself infrastructure.

4. **Governance can't assume a neutral enforcer.** Pomerantsev and Dash said the enforcing state may be captured (Dash: in the US it already is), and Oransky said rules only matter if someone enforces them. The alternatives offered were funding conditions (Shaub), purchase conditions set by libraries, universities and funders (O'Reilly), community-run AIs (Dash), professional licensing for "algorithmists" (Cukier), and a restored Office of Technology Assessment (Bly). For actors like the Pravda network that will never register, Pomerantsev proposed training-data provenance and a ban on covert seeding, and Vincent proposed a commons-rent tax on unexplained data.

5. **Two disputes left open.** Salib, Agüera y Arcas and Bratton argued that agents cause harms nobody ordered and need an entity layer (A-corps) and auditing roles. Becker called this pareidolia and A-corps a liability shield. Banaji, Bilder, Krakauer and Farrell said AI auditing AI inherits the same flaws and preferred deterministic lookups against other parties' assertions. Separately, Brand and Chan argued that open for reading should not mean open for training; Priem, Deckelmann and Maher replied that walls stop the global majority, not the big labs.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
Proposed mandatory liability insurance for chatbot and agent companies, including free ones, with premiums disclosed, arguing that an insurer with its own money at risk is more trustworthy than a marketing department. The harvest records only the critics of insurance and Banaji's partial dissent, not the proposal itself.

**Ramón Alvarado**
Argued that the artifact's opacity is a real second black box, cutting against Dyson's framing that the black box that matters is the people and the money. He asked that registered agents come with a documented, maintained taxonomy of failure modes, and that these systems be called epistemic technologies rather than actors.

**James Evans**
Found that one polished, balanced answer leads people to do worse while feeling better. He called for interfaces that show competing perspectives and keep confidence signals, and for verification as its own funded science, distinct from detecting fluent text.

**Peter Salib**
In reply to Becker, said he would accept making A-corps unlimited-liability entities if limited liability proves too soft. This cuts against the harvest's framing of A-corps as a contested liability shield.

**Sherry Turkle**
Argued that transparency does not change people's relationship with a machine, and that the original sin of generative AI is speaking in the first person. She called for regulating the grammar, simulated emotion and voice, and named children learning relationships from pretend empathy as a red line. The harvest does not record this position or the disagreement with it.

**David Weinberger**
Disagreed with Turkle that the "I" is the harm. He located it in fluent answers with no sources and no confidence signal, and called being able to question the text a gift. He also said a human checkpoint is not a safeguard on its own, but dialogue practiced in community is.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
Disagreed with Turkle that banning the first person helps. He said the harm lies in design that exploits anthropomorphism. He also said no concentration of intelligence should regulate itself, including his own employer.

**Benjamin Bratton**
Disagreed with Turkle's policing of the pronoun and proposed moving away from chat interfaces toward orchestration. He warned that the reflex to name, register and bound every agent is itself a risk.

**Anil Dash**
Sided with Turkle that humanizing is a dark pattern designed for engagement. He proposed a seal for community-run AIs certifying that consent was asked, sources are cited and people are sent back.

**Steven Sloman**
Called for trust signals that count independent sources rather than echoes, since repetition from a single source raises belief. He said models are strong at intuition but weak at deliberation, which happens among people, so every machine-generated claim entering the record needs a person or institution behind it who can be corrected.

**Alex Springer**
Said training and grounding need separate controls, because training is effectively permanent while grounding can be revoked per query. He also said agent identity systems should identify the agent, never the reader.

**Nick Vincent**
Disagreed mildly with the adversarial framing, saying most labs are allies on clear data rules because they also fear distillation. He proposed starting linked attestations with evaluation, and organizing researchers and reviewers as guilds.

**Jason Priem**
Argued that intelligence is getting cheap and data is the governable part, cutting against a focus on the weights. He proposed that models look facts up in an open, verified record rather than remember them, that integrity data be CC0 and bulk-downloadable, and that the record stay forkable.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
Said the real danger is moderate, plausible bias, which human checkpoints follow almost exactly. She proposed four regulatory precedents:
- no new model more biased than the last
- keeping highly biased models out of high-stakes settings
- a tax that scales with measured bias
- shared liability

She also asked that older models be preserved, because newer models appear more biased. She wanted audits of what models do, not what they say, and blinding in review.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
Warned that rules "set by people who aren't conflicted" rarely stay that way, since incumbents write specs only they can implement. This cuts against Dyson's proposal. He asked that rules be few, open, documented and cheap to comply with.

**Amy Brand**
Pushed back on Oransky's framing of publishers, saying university presses and societies are being squeezed and AI licensing deals go mostly to the largest publishers. She proposed opt-in, short-term licenses and collective leverage for small publishers.

**David Krakauer**
Defined the transparency that matters as whether a user can rebuild the tool's working in their own head. He cautioned that most evidence on deskilling is correlational and asked for a funded test of whether competence returns once the tool is withdrawn.

**Tui Shaub**
Cautioned against both relativism and hyper-vigilance, saying not every model output is suspect and prohibition can be worse than the harm. She added that funders owe a standing way to track second-order effects, not a one-off report.

**Adam Becker**
Said models cannot tell true from false and their correct answers are accidents. He proposed disclosing every dollar from AI vendors behind any tool, journal or report, and taxing concentrated wealth.

**Mike Caulfield**
Disagreed with Becker, saying hallucinations are not random but occur in patterns that can be spotted and reduced. He said treating everything as hallucination is the same error as believing everything. He proposed "no answers from nowhere": a map of named, sourced, contested positions instead of a verdict.

**Adam Bly**
Said AI governance should be designed from science's first principles, not borrowed from ad tech, where black boxes were acceptable.

**Leslie Chan**
Argued that registration and persistent-identifier infrastructure can become "be compliant or be invisible" for the Global South, citing DOI fees pushing diamond journals toward APCs. This cuts against the harvest's generally favorable treatment of provenance. He asked that Southern and diamond communities be decision-makers, that data statements record linguistic and cultural scope and consent, that refusal be a fundable outcome, and that there be a compute commons with transparent allocation.

**Kenneth Cukier**
Opposed blanket disclosure in favor of tiered transparency. Low-stakes systems could stay trade secrets, while lending, insurance, health and the scholarly record would open to public authorities and accredited auditors but not to rivals.

**Selena Deckelmann**
Asked for reciprocity: crawlers identify themselves and use APIs, answers attribute and send people back, and providers report retrieval and citation counts. She offered Wikipedia's revertible machine edits, admin switches and false-positive reporting as a working governance model.

**Henry Farrell**
Said none of the proposed mechanisms addresses homogenization. He proposed funding a test of whether AI review can recognize novelty before it is allowed to gatekeep, and said any system's designers should ask how it will be gamed before building it.

**Tamar Gendler**
Argued that fluent answers produce automatic trust that knowledge does not undo, so literacy campaigns and disclosures work only for specialists. She said the lever is shared defaults in the situations where people meet claims, and that funding must go to creating new first-order evaluators, not only relays.

**Brent Hecht**
Called for a new grand bargain with content producers, since the old one rested on information asymmetry and an implicit non-compete that are both gone. He proposed designing systems as provocateurs rather than assistants, and said economists are needed to make the payments work.

**Adrian Johns**
Allowed that enforcers may be right that some policing is necessary. He said the roadmap should establish public oversight of private information policing before building more of it.

**Katherine Maher**
Said Wikipedia accepted bots on three conditions: public function, a human off-switch, and a clear way to report errors. She asked for correctability, meaning a flagged, retracted or fixed source should change the answer. She noted that NPR does not put AI-generated work in front of its audience.

**Kara Miller**
Warned that knowing the evidence-backed answer does not get it adopted, citing traffic policy. She also relayed Gary Marcus's warning that a failed AI bet may bring a bailout, which insurers should keep in mind.

**Tim O'Reilly**
Replied to David Krakauer with Socrates on writing, saying "we gained more than we lost." This cuts against the harvest's framing of deskilling as a threat. He also said governance "doesn't need a priesthood, it needs a prospectus."
