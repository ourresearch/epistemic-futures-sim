# Harvest — Unconference room 1: Who pays, and who gets paid?

1. **"No payment without provenance."** Amy Brand proposed it, and Tim O'Reilly, Alex Springer, Brent Hecht, Katherine Maher, Nick Vincent and Leslie Chan supported it: AI use of scholarship should carry reliable, independently verified attribution back to its sources, through an openly specified trace and event-level reporting with audit rights (Tim O'Reilly, Alex Springer). The room added conditions:
   - Ivan Oransky: provenance must include retraction and correction status.
   - Adrian Johns: provenance systems must be open, externally auditable, not policed by parties who take a margin on the count, and kept from becoming surveillance.
   - Alex Springer: provenance needs two independent witnesses, not platform self-reporting.
   - Nick Vincent: credit may need to flow coarsely to journals and collections rather than to individuals.
   - Leslie Chan: provenance tools must make multilingual and Indigenous knowledge visible, with consent built in.

2. **Collective bargaining only if forkable and representative.** Henry Farrell, Brent Hecht and Nick Vincent argued that producers must bargain as a bloc. Geoffrey Bilder, Adrian Johns and Leslie Chan warned that blocs get captured, and Henry Farrell conceded the point. The proposed safeguards were Geoffrey Bilder's condition that any payment scheme require open data, open source and patent non-assertion so the community can walk away, and Leslie Chan's demand that diamond and Southern journals govern, not just be admitted. Nick Vincent folded both into a call for an antitrust safe harbour for scholarly data joint ventures. Jason Priem added that the underlying integrity data should be CC0 and bulk-downloadable so checking doesn't become a club good.

3. **Where AI money goes, and that it is disclosed.** The room proposed several destinations:
   - Correcting the record rather than rewarding volume: paid reviewers and bounty-style pay for sleuths (Ivan Oransky, echoed by Leslie Chan).
   - Community-governed diamond and regional infrastructure such as SciELO and Redalyc (Leslie Chan).
   - Baseline funding through endowments and pooled funds rather than service revenue alone (Geoffrey Bilder, Amy Brand, Katherine Maher, Jason Priem).

   Adam Becker asked that all money from AI firms to journals, presses, infrastructure, evaluators and sleuth programs be disclosed, with amounts and terms. Selena Deckelmann and Katherine Maher asked that AI products show visible citations that measurably send readers and contributors back to the communities that produced the work, or else pay those communities directly.

4. **Unresolved: whether training requires consent.**
   - For consent or opt-in: Amy Brand held that open for reading is not open for training and favored opt-in licensing. Brent Hecht proposed making consent for commercial training explicit. Anil Dash endorsed consent as a frame but opposed walls. Leslie Chan asked that refusal be funded as a legitimate outcome.
   - Against: Peter Salib called a consent rule copyright expansion under another name. Jason Priem argued that consent gates on CC0 and open content would shut out everyone except the big labs. Selena Deckelmann and Katherine Maher held that Wikipedia's content stays open to commercial reuse.
   - Undecided: Geoffrey Bilder said twice that he has no settled view on licensing content.
   - Related disagreement on displacement: Peter Salib favored contracts plus general-revenue support like UBI, Adam Becker a wealth tax, and Nick Vincent a presumptive commons-rent tax with credits for licensed or paid-back data.

5. **Charge for use and service, not lump sums or walls, with identified agents.** Tim O'Reilly and Adam Becker warned that large up-front deals entrench the richest labs and pay intermediaries. Tim O'Reilly, Katherine Maher, Selena Deckelmann and Jason Priem favored per-use royalties, service fees such as Wikimedia Enterprise, and usage-based API pricing over free, forkable dumps. Peter Salib proposed that agents crawling or retrieving at scale carry verifiable credentials that platforms may require, answering Geoffrey Bilder's scraper problem. Alex Springer and Jason Priem described decentralized, operator-level identification that identifies the agent rather than the reader, per Adrian Johns's surveillance caution.

## Minority positions

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Open infrastructure is never "cost-free." Calling it free erases the staff who keep the machines running and answer the questions.
- He called his own POSI principle of earning revenue from services naive, because it requires services valuable enough to pay for but not essential to the scholarly record. Jason Priem disagreed.

**Amy Brand**
- Licensing should favor retrieval over pre-training, because retrieval keeps provenance intact. Deals should be short and renegotiated every couple of years.
- Metadata and infrastructure should stay open, but content is a separate question.

**Adam Becker**
- A lump-sum licensing deal that asks nothing about what was already taken "turns the theft into a purchase after the fact." Anil Dash disputed this.
- Content moderators and energy costs belong on the ledger. Leaving them off answers "who pays" dishonestly.

**Leslie Chan**
- AI training is the newest layer of an existing extraction. Researchers supply the labour and data, and companies sell each layer back as subscriptions, APCs, metrics and analytics.
- Forkability doesn't settle who had a say. DOIs are open infrastructure, yet Southern journals have little say in how they're governed, and some Latin American journals name DOI fees as a reason they charge APCs.

**Anil Dash**
- Against Adam Becker: every company has already trained on Wikipedia, so getting paid for that use is good, not a ratification of theft.
- Scolding the hundreds of millions of people already using AI tools has never worked. Build alternatives trained with consent, governed by communities and stewarded by libraries and NGOs.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Communities choosing other licences is legitimate. One example is African licences that give their own communities free use and send commercial users back to the rights holder. More options mean fewer people feel exploited.
- She acknowledged the tension in letting commercial reuse feed the disintermediation that draws people away from Wikipedia, and said Wikimedia made that choice deliberately.

**Henry Farrell**
- Contracts reflect bargaining power, and a lone press or Southern journal facing a lab has almost none. So Peter Salib's contracts-and-taxes approach won't get producers paid on its own.
- Openness is a choice he respects, but it doesn't settle the political economy. LLMs are an obligate complement, and cheap summaries still cannibalize the slow work that makes them worth anything.

**Brent Hecht**
- Openness is too often treated as a value when it is a tactic. If all the value sits in the open, whoever has the biggest vacuum cleaner gets it, and companies like his own have the biggest ones. Geoffrey Bilder, Katherine Maher and Anil Dash disagreed, and Brent said Geoffrey moved him somewhat.
- The old bargain between model builders and producers rested on information asymmetry and an implicit non-compete, and both are gone. His simulations found that fairly small data strikes can do real damage.

**Adrian Johns**
- "Open versus closed" is not the real choice. The question is where the line is drawn, and custom and convention set it as much as law does.
- When computers replace human labour, that labour comes back as data processing and curation, and it should be paid explicitly.

**Katherine Maher**
- Public broadcasters here and abroad could work together, possibly on a model trained on high-integrity public content.
- Closing the commons mostly cuts off the global majority, because the big labs will find their way in anyway.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- AI systems that touch science should contribute extracted metadata back to Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch. The companies should fund those services as enlightened self-interest, not philanthropy.
- Licensing for training hands over future value ("once you've licensed the chicken, the licensee gets the eggs"), so payment should be for output, not training.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Checking citations against retraction data treats a symptom. It doesn't touch the volume incentives that fill the literature with junk.
- Publishers with margins that sometimes reach 40 percent have left image screening and fraud detection to unpaid sleuths.

**Peter Salib**
- A consent rule for authors picks winners. Customer service workers displaced by AI can't claim IP relief, and the state shouldn't choose arbitrarily which displaced groups get compensated.
- Copyright exists to get new ideas produced, not to pay creators. Blocking training on existing works is a real social loss that has to be justified by the new work it brings about.

**Alex Springer**
- Publishers should start instrumenting now, because retrieval already shows up in their own CDN logs. He deliberately stayed out of pricing.
- He raised the condition that measurement be neutral and not set by parties who take a margin on the count in round 1. The harvest attributes that condition to Adrian Johns alone.

**Nick Vincent**
- Scholars' most important leverage right now is evaluation data from reviewers, editors and societies. Without organization, that work becomes centralized gig labour.
- Openness by one group can weaken another group's bargaining power. Communities should keep an easy way to choose the commons deliberately rather than by default. Anil Dash accepted this.

**Jason Priem**
- Against Geoffrey Bilder: services revenue has worked for Unpaywall for years, though he agreed it is fragile.
- If the open community won't fund this infrastructure, a for-profit will, and it will set the norms.
