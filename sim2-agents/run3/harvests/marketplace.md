# Harvest — What might a well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace look like?

1. Attribution is feasible now and should run on open, shared telemetry: document hashes (a DOI works) and relevance scores, a two-witness record of retrieval, and the source's retraction status carried in the trace, at zero cost to the smallest journals and with disclosure made a condition of purchase (Tim O'Reilly, Alex Springer, Nick Vincent; Kara Miller urged libraries and universities to demand traces now). Ivan Oransky and Katherine Maher backed the status field, but Oransky warned it is only as good as retraction records that are currently leaky and kept up largely by unpaid sleuths.

2. Pay communities, not counts: pay per use at the journal or society level (O'Reilly), through guilds and collective bodies that give scholars leverage individuals lack (Vincent, James Evans, Brent Hecht) and that must be representative and forkable (Vincent, Henry Farrell). Tamar Gendler warned, citing the Israeli daycare fines, that pricing contributions could crowd out norm-driven work like refereeing and sharing null results. Maher (Wikimedia Enterprise) and Hecht (the Reddit moderator study) offered evidence that paying the institution avoids this, while Gendler said it should be piloted and watched, not assumed.

3. Several argued citation counts are the wrong unit to pay on. They reward what already draws attention and miss null results (Evans, Farrell), are already gamed by citation rings and paper mills (Oransky), reward whoever floods the corpus (Peter Pomerantsev), and count repetitions of one source as independent evidence (Steven Sloman); Maher countered that the same graph can also expose gaps. Evans and Oransky proposed spending part of Vincent's commons-rent tax on negative results, sleuth bounties and faster retractions, while Peter Salib rejected the tax itself as an IP-style entitlement and preferred direct contracts for missing data, general-revenue support for displaced workers, and a Pigouvian tax on flooding.

4. Unresolved: who counts, who checks the counter, and what makes forking possible in year ten (Kara Miller). Springer held that whoever takes a margin on the count shouldn't set it (endorsed by Farrell and Vincent). Adrian Johns and Miller warned that early counting conventions harden into rule, as with the Stationers' Company and Nielsen, and Johns said oversight of the private auditing industry Vincent's scheme would create must be decided now.

5. Sloman and Sherry Turkle raised the reader, whom the three designs don't name. Readers take citation strings as cues without checking them, so the trace should show readers how independent the sources are (Sloman), and the interface, not only the ledger, needs governing (Turkle). Turkle added that a marketplace fully fair to authors can still teach a generation not to read, and Pomerantsev asked that commons money also fund public participation, not only expert checks.

## Minority positions

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Opposed lump-sum training licences because they entrench the richest labs and rarely reach creators. He argued for paying for outputs, not training.
- Said labs should check citations against Crossref, OpenAlex, ORCID and Retraction Watch by default, contribute metadata back, and pay for that infrastructure.
- Argued for open, federated, inspectable protocols rather than platforms, so no single registry becomes either the monoculture or the policeman.

**Alex Springer**
- Said robots.txt can't tell training from grounding (7.8% of citations came from domains blocking the training bot, against 2.1% for the live-search bot). Because training is permanent and grounding is revocable, the two need separate controls.
- Noted that only retrieval has an independent publisher-side witness. Grounding and citation rely on provider self-reports, so every licence needs an event-level reporting clause.
- Said telemetry should identify the agent, never the reader, and data holders should publish retention policies. He conceded that an agent's identity manifest means nothing until other parties attest to it.
- Declined to offer any pricing formula. He said ownership, price and compensation belong to the communities, and that OpenAttribution's governance should be one member, one vote.

**Nick Vincent**
- Wanted attestation across the whole supply chain, not just retrieval: training data, expert participation in evaluations, model outputs, and AI-assisted papers.
- Called evaluation data scholars' strongest current leverage, with centralized gig labor as the default alternative. He said refusing a deal has to be an outcome someone will fund.
- Proposed specific governance: public tax authorities set the rules, accredited auditors funded from the tax review evidence, and a public technical board maintains the schemas.

**James Evans**
- Argued the pricing objection is weaker than people think, because labs already weight their data mixtures and scaling laws tell them what extra data is worth. This sits in tension with Hecht's statement that he still needs economists to set prices.

**Henry Farrell**
- Said funders should test whether AI review can recognize novelty before it is allowed to screen papers. His bet is that it can't.

**Brent Hecht**
- Said models trained only on explicitly consented data lose a lot of performance, so withholding organized by professional domain, science included, would actually bite.
- Presented the Reddit moderator measurement ($3.4 million a year) as evidence for collective bargaining, not as evidence that paying institutions avoids crowding out. He said he still needs economists for pricing.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Said payment should go to the terminal nodes who do first-order evaluation, not the links that pass their verdicts along, and that the framing of payment matters as much as the amount.

**Adrian Johns**
- Pointed to the Stationers' Company's rotating wardens as the historical answer to "who guards the guards." He said forkability is a better answer but has to be designed in from the start, and that counting conventions matter more than the pricing formula.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Said funders must stop counting papers and citations. Otherwise AI payments will speed up the volume play.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Said telemetry should show who is seeding training data, not only who gets cited.
- Argued for measuring trust and constructive engagement rather than citation counts, which he called click counts in academic dress.
- Backed a levy precedent, Zuckerman's tax on data-hungry advertising paid into a public-broadcasting-style fund. This cuts against Salib's rejection of the commons-rent tax.

**Peter Salib**
- Supported O'Reilly and Springer because their proposals are contract, not property, with grounding as a revocable service. He said the fairness complaint about training is factually true but IP-style entitlements are the wrong tool to address it.

**Steven Sloman**
- Said credit that's visible in the trace may matter more to experts than a per-use fee, because they contribute to be right and to be acknowledged. This cuts against the room's focus on payment.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Said a citation string under a fluent first-person answer is transparency in the Macintosh sense, not the open-the-hood sense, and that transparency doesn't launder the relationship. She added that a royalty doesn't give back what summarizing in place of reading costs the reader.
