# Harvest — Session 4: Epistemic Authority — Credibility in an Age of Infinite Scale

1. Credibility cues are alieved: people keep responding to them automatically even after learning they are cheap to produce. Any replacement for fluency will be alieved too, so cues must be tested before they are relied on, and the fix has to come from shared defaults rather than individual resolve (Gendler, Banaji, Sloman; Bilder on CrossMark, Maher on "confidence transfer," and Bly on watermarks each described building or seeing such cues). Hecht added that designs meant to reduce overreliance, such as explanations, can increase it, and Turkle proposed governing the interface itself, starting with no first-person "I" by default.

2. Unresolved: can a machine fill a terminal-node seat? Becker held that a text generator never can, because nothing in its loop is checked against ground truth, while measuring instruments are fine (Caulfield, Sloman and Cukier broadly agreed). Alvarado, Bratton, Salib, Deckelmann, Priem and Agüera y Arcas held that the question is whether a seat's occupant carries its own warrant and passes independent checks. The room largely converged on testing every seat, human or machine, against a measured floor (Evans) and a novelty test built from papers now known to have been ahead of their time, since agreement can be won by "gatekeeping by banality" (Farrell with Evans; backed by Weinberger, Brand, Krakauer, Vincent, Agüera y Arcas). Krakauer asked that human seats also be tested with the tool taken away.

3. Earned authority was located most often in corrigibility, meaning whether the record changes when checked, rather than in how hard a signal is to fake (Sloman, Miller, Oransky, Cukier, Weinberger, Maher, Bilder). Chan, Miller and Oransky showed that expensive, unfakeable markers such as long histories, indexing and mastheads can be unearned or exclusionary, and Oransky proposed judging journals by how fast they correct. Dyson, Cukier and Salib required that an accountable party be able to overrule the system and bear the cost of being wrong. Dyson and Springer added that disclosure, which Becker and Shaub extended to all AI-vendor and funder money, counts only when it is tied to consequences and witnessed by parties who don't share an interest.

4. Gendler's question of who makes new terminal nodes was treated as a labor and pipeline problem. Answer engines that don't send readers back are shrinking the supply of newcomers (Dash, Deckelmann, O'Reilly), and first-order evaluation is mostly unpaid (Hecht). Proposals converged on paying the community, not the count, to avoid Gendler's daycare trap: endowed funding, onboarding and mentoring, reviewer guilds with Southern seats, CRediT roles for AI contributions and for the humans who verify them, and funded apprenticeship (Hecht, Shaub, Deckelmann, Vincent, Chan, Brand, Krakauer). Miller asked who will count whether any of it works.

5. On Gendler's open question about the public, the room largely rejected literacy campaigns in favor of participation. Proposals included seats with decision rights and paid people to fill them (Shaub, Chan, Dyson, Maher), people "in a position to know" who hold no credentials (Caulfield, Deckelmann), and trust built through agency and shared action, which Pomerantsev saw protect people against manipulation. Johns cast the public as trained readers and Turkle as readers before checkers, and Turkle asked that the manifesto be allowed to ask whether, not only how.

## Minority positions

**Mahzarin Banaji**
Experts err in systematic ways, so earned authority belongs to procedures rather than to people. She proposed blinding: information evaluators, human or model, should ask not to have, enforced by a curtain rather than a declared intention, while conceding it cannot undo disadvantages inherited earlier. Chan countered that blinding does nothing for a journal the index never saw.

**Adam Becker**
Named treating wealth as expertise as a separate gatekeeping failure. From the Inference case, he warned that placing junk beside real work under one masthead launders it. When called, he argued that once a leash or trace is added, the terminal node is the human operator or checker, and the text generator only routes to them.
