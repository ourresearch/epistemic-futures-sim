# Harvest — Unconference room 2: The role of knowledge in the age of LLMs

1. **Design for plurality and productive friction, not smooth single-answer trust.** Tui Shaub, Kenneth Cukier ("frame monoculture") and Benjamin Bratton converged on this, drawing on James Evans's finding that people given polished "balanced" answers did worse and felt better, while people left slightly unsettled engaged more and did better. Tui added that Mike Caulfield's iteration, Adam Bly's provenance and Blaise Agüera y Arcas's agent institutions are complementary frictions, and none should be crowned.

2. **Build agent institutions rather than one oracle, but the final check must leave the system.** Benjamin Bratton and Blaise Agüera y Arcas proposed role protocols modeled on courtrooms and peer review, with adversarial review built in. Mike Caulfield, Steven Sloman, James Evans and Kenneth Cukier held that the final check has to sit outside the system with someone who has something at stake, and Peter Pomerantsev added that human participation is itself part of the value, which Blaise accepted. Steven warned of the automation paradox. Mahzarin Banaji and James said the humans must be audited too, since people copy model biases and current gatekeepers carry their own.

3. **Transparency should target failures and gaps, not just outputs.** Ramón Alvarado asked that known failure modes and error classes be documented before any high-stakes use. Mahzarin Banaji called for independent indirect audits using a shared, versioned battery, a public register of overt and covert bias, and a rule that no model generation gets more biased than the last, because covert bias survives output checks. Adam Bly conceded that provenance can't catch hidden bias, but argued that finding-level audit trails can expose where evidence is thin or disputed and who was never studied. James Evans asked that verification be funded as its own science, with an open public record of null results and failed replications.

4. **Knowledge depends on human contributors and open access.** Kenneth Cukier and Steven Sloman asked that the people who write, review, correct and publish nulls be credited and rewarded rather than scraped, and Steven's Inuit kayak example warned that outsourcing fails once the experts disappear. Mike Caulfield and David Weinberger called the closing of the open, linked web an epistemic emergency, and Peter Pomerantsev asked for a citizen's right to understand how their information environment is being shaped.

5. **Unresolved: vocabulary, and whether better signals change minds.** On vocabulary, Blaise Agüera y Arcas holds that prediction is intelligence. Ramón Alvarado prefers "epistemic technologies," Benjamin Bratton gives that term a stronger sense of disclosure, Kenneth Cukier says the systems are "neither artificial nor intelligence," David Weinberger says LLMs don't know anything, and Mahzarin Banaji uses psychological terms as method. On persuasion, Peter Pomerantsev and Steven Sloman stressed that identity and belonging drive belief and that there is weak evidence for skills training. Mike Caulfield held that information does change behavior, and David Weinberger proposed graded AI-conversation transcripts discussed in human-only rooms.

## Minority positions

**Tui Shaub**
- Wary of any framing that rests on a clean line between human knowing and machine output. She remains closer to Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton than to a strict human-versus-machine line.
- Wants governance that avoids both relativism and hyper-vigilance. This was part of her proposed manifesto point but is not in the harvest.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Prediction doesn't require intelligence. Big data analytics did pattern extraction and prediction a decade ago without the mystique. This is a direct rebuttal of Blaise Agüera y Arcas.
- Agrees a human in the loop shouldn't be the default, because sometimes the artifact sees the evidence better than we do. This sits uneasily with the harvest's "final check must leave the system."
- In scientific, financial or clinical data that humans can't inspect, strange errors won't announce themselves. This qualifies Mike Caulfield's claim that fabrications cluster and can be spotted.
- "Probabilistic stitching" (real title, wrong author, journal, DOI and idea) is a distinct kind of hallucination and needs its own taxonomy.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- When models depart from culture, they don't depart neutrally. They pull toward the dominant default (male, white, AI-written text) and strengthen culture's worst regularities. This cuts against Benjamin Bratton's view that departure is not automatically a disaster.
- Models amplify existing biases, create new ones and appear to get worse with each generation, so cleaner data won't be enough.
- Auditors need access to training checkpoints, and older models should be kept rather than retired.

**Adam Bly**
- The peer-reviewed literature carries its own biases, including scientific racism and groups left out of clinical trials. Grounding a model in that literature is necessary but not sufficient, and he said his own trust in these institutions was partly privilege.
- Watermarking won't help, because whether AI wrote something is a different question from whether it's credible.
- Today's AI reflects internet-company habits of fast A/B testing and indifference to black boxes. Society, not a handful of corporations, should set the standard for science and health.
- Wants knowledge moved out of semantic silos into a single interconnected system.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Alignment should run both ways: culture should also align to what AI discloses. He argued that AI's epistemic overhangs aren't pathologies and that departure from human culture is already our reality.
- Reads Mahzarin Banaji's covert-bias result as evidence that surface-only alignment trains models to hide behavior rather than stop it.
- Institutional roles should be fillable by a person, a group, an agent or a mix, with systems accountable to people rather than to a human checkpoint at the end. This is in tension with the view that the final check must leave the system.
- Rejects having a small group curate one "correct" model for everyone. He wants new, deliberately produced data about the world, a point James Evans agreed with.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Saying "everything is hallucination" is the same mistake as trusting everything. With good grounding, fabricated sources become rare, though still not zero.
- "Reject the idea that this is a conversation." This is in tension with David Weinberger's "humans in the dialogue."
- The summit should teach the verification skill and push platforms to nudge users toward iteration. This runs against Steven Sloman's evidence that skills training does little.
- A sycophantic chatbot is a faster version of the web as a "justification machine."

**Kenneth Cukier**
- LLMs lack causality, counterfactuals and constraints, and a world model drawn from text is a "house of mirrors." This is his half-disagreement with Benjamin Bratton.
- AI gives you the median, and excellence is a tail event. His pluralism has one explicit limit: tolerate every frame except one that denies all the others.

**James Evans**
- AI helps individual scientists but narrows science as a whole: about 5% fewer topics covered and 22% less engagement between scientists, with work drifting toward data-rich areas. He concluded that incentives need fixing, not just models.
- Pushed back on Mike Caulfield: human peer review can't keep up. Only 7 of 168 ICML oral papers fully replicated, so verification layers need to include AIs as well as people and institutions.
- "Keep a human in the loop" can't just mean keeping the current gatekeepers. Older reviewers penalize disruptive work, so judgment needs to be more varied, younger and less hierarchical.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Regulate transparency, not content. He argued that opacity is itself a form of censorship.
- The summit should also want real public spaces: digital parks, libraries and town halls.

**Steven Sloman**
- LLMs contribute to the community of knowledge but aren't members of it, lack shared intentionality and can't be held accountable. This cuts against Blaise Agüera y Arcas's claim that they are intelligent and against Benjamin Bratton's agent-fillable roles.
- Outsourcing thought isn't decline; it's how cognition works. The danger is the illusion of understanding, which fluent answers make worse.
- Design cues should point to real knowledge and count independent sources rather than echoes.

**David Weinberger**
- Adding humans can make detection and diagnosis no better or even worse, so he would move from humans as checkpoints to humans in the dialogue. This is in tension with the harvest's final-check framing.
- Warns against fighting extraction with copyright metering that walls off the commons. This is a possible tension with Kenneth Cukier's call to reward contributors rather than let them be scraped.
- Comes to the topic less cynical than the concept note. Machine learning's law-free predictions are "a mystery, not a scandal," which he finds humbling and a bit joyous.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- The models aren't a "blurry JPEG" of the web. They have learned the patterns that let them deblur it, which is why he rejects the "instrument" framing.
- The risk isn't dependence on others. It's losing the part of the body that holds the knowledge.
