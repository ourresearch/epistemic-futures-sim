# People, Not Data: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

## Preamble

None of this is inevitable. Some of it was already broken: incentives rewarding volume, a scholarly record never cleaned, and a system that never worked for most of the world. Some was breaking: trust signals, the open web, funding for the institutions that produce and check knowledge, and their independence from governments that attack public media and capture watchdogs. What AI adds is speed, and extraction without return: fluent answers that do not send people back to the sources, volunteers and readers these systems depend on. The ledger also omits data centers' energy and water, and the low-paid moderators and annotators harmed so these systems look clean. These are political choices, reversible by regulation, organized labor and public funding. Everything below must survive a government, or a funder, that wants it gone. Dissents close the document.

## Principles

**1. The bottleneck is people, not data.**

These systems depend on the people who check what we know: Wikipedia's admins, research-integrity sleuths, reviewers, first-order evaluators, and young workers who learn by doing the boring parts. Answer engines that don't send readers back shrink the supply of newcomers, and most first-order evaluation is unpaid. If entry-level work disappears before people learn it, they may have nothing to come back to. Leaders who assume mentorship will happen organically are wrong. Curators, sleuths, evaluators, mentors and readers must be funded as infrastructure, with named owners for training the next generation. Apprenticeship belongs in grants and AI contracts as a line item, not a hope.

**2. Build competence before reliance, and treat atrophy as a hypothesis to test.**

David Krakauer's model treats competence as bistable: it collapses above a threshold of tool availability and recovers only far below it. Which state a person reaches depends on prior learning and transparency. Transparency means how much of the tool's working users can rebuild, not how well the tool explains itself. Opaque tools erode even experts' competence. Institutions set a third variable, the cost of acquiring competence. Above a critical cost, competence cannot survive, so defunding apprenticeship causes collapse. The evidence is partial and correlational, and none comes from AI users. The missing test is to raise, then lower, tool availability in the same people and look for hysteresis. Until then, build skill before the tool and test human seats without it. Outsourcing cognition is normal. The danger is the illusion of understanding.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**

Citations by default are the cheap, evidence-backed first rule. Every answer should cite its sources, the cited passage should be readable, and each source's retraction and correction status should travel into the answer. A flag is not yet a correction: when a source is flagged, retracted or fixed, the answer should change. The trace should also show whether the passage supports the claim, where evidence is thin or missing, and who was never studied, because a citation shows origin, not fairness. None of this needs a regulator. Libraries, universities and funders can write these conditions into contracts now and require independent counts of return flow to source communities.

**4. Trust is earned by correction, not by the cost of faking.**

A record earns authority by changing when checked. Costly markers such as long histories, indexing and mastheads can be unearned or exclusionary, so judge journals by how fast they correct. Every public agent should be tied to a person or organization that can overrule it and can be corrected or sued. That party should carry liability insurance even when the agent is free. Insurers should price that coverage against independent event records, and premiums should be disclosed. Regulators should audit underwriters like banks and exempt or subsidize small startups and diamond journals. No cue should be relied on untested. People keep trusting cues they know are cheap, and any replacement for fluency will be trusted the same way. Explanations meant to reduce overreliance can increase it.

**5. Plurality over any one oracle.**

Homogenization, the most serious threat, predates the models: English-only publishing, the journal "tree farm" and open-access consolidation. AI helps individual scientists while narrowing science: fewer topics, less engagement, and null results vanishing from abstracts. People given one balanced answer did worse and felt better, so interfaces should show competing, named positions. Trust signals should count independent sources: ten echoes of one preprint are one voice. But independence can be faked, so test such signals on real readers for months, measuring belief and behaviour, with graders independent of vendors. Before any reviewer, human or machine, gatekeeps, it should pass a novelty test built from papers once ahead of their time. The test is a floor, not a score. Scholarly societies should hold it, not labs, and it should be retired on a published schedule.

**6. Build institutions, not oracles, and test every seat.**

Rather than one oracle, build institutions with defined roles and adversarial review, like courtrooms and peer review. Leave the final check outside the system, with someone who has something at stake. That seat rests on stakes and accountability, not on human judgment being better by default, because instruments often beat human senses. Humans in the loop need auditing too. In hiring studies, people copied a moderately biased model almost exactly, and older reviewers penalize disruptive work. So every seat, person or instrument, must show competence against a measured floor and report which kinds of error it makes, not only how often. Prefer deterministic lookups against other parties' records over AI auditing AI. Fund verification as open science, with a public record of failed replications. Only 7 of 168 ICML oral papers fully replicated.

**7. Audit what the systems do, not what they say.**

Models that sound neutral still act biased. Covert bias survives output checks, and many biases worsen with each generation. Auditors who do not profit from the result need a shared, versioned battery of indirect bias measures, checkpoints from each training stage, disclosure of data sources, and a public register of overt and covert bias. Audit the battery and its authors too. Where possible, blind human and model reviewers to names, affiliations and prestige. Blinding cannot help work the index never saw. No model generation should be more biased than the last. Preserve retired models, because deprecation destroys the ability to measure drift. Document known failure modes before anyone says "trust," and make every score and checker report its error types.

**8. Extractors pay communities, not counts.**

Extractors should pay, and no one should build a per-sentence meter. No payment without provenance: an open trace, event-level reporting and audit rights with remedies. Only retrieval has two independent witnesses. Grounding and citation are reported by the party that may owe payment, so licences need sampled audits, canary content and grounding-to-retrieval checks. A trace shows use, not truth, and null results never enter it. Pay per use to journals, societies and collections, not on citation counts, which reward the median and flooding. Funders, universities and ranking bodies must drop paper and citation counts from hiring, promotion and grants, or AI payments will fuel the volume play. Fund correction instead: paid review, sleuth bounties with legal cover, negative results, and retractions within two months. Pilot payments to correctors first so pricing does not crowd out working norms.

**9. Openness is a tactic, not a creed, and communities choose it.**

Whoever has the biggest vacuum cleaner takes the open commons, while walls mostly shut out the global majority, not the big labs. Open for reading is not automatically open for training, and open need not mean unpaid. Extractors should pay for what they use up, such as access, bandwidth, maintenance and checking. That money should go into pooled funds the payer cannot pull back, counted by third parties. Communities choose the commons deliberately, refusal is a fundable outcome, and consent should not mean walls. Training and grounding need separate controls, since only grounding can be revoked. Producers must bargain collectively in representative, forkable blocs under an antitrust safe harbour for scholarly data. Royalties are not enough while compute concentrates. Build public AI: open models and data governed by public institutions, and a compute commons allocated transparently by region.

**10. Name every counter and its checker, and let the people described govern.**

Whoever takes a margin on a count should not set how it is measured, because early conventions such as Nielsen's harden into rule. Provenance, counting and integrity infrastructure should be open, auditable, forkable and rotated from day one. It needs a living will, many directories, a copy outside the United States, and paid scrutiny of self-audits. Integrity tools are public goods, free to every journal. Identify the agent, never the reader. Disclose every dollar from AI vendors, philanthropies and public funders, this summit's included, and tie disclosure to consequences. Funders are counters too. Southern and diamond journals govern from day one, with funded participation and co-authorship of multilingual specifications. Check yearly whether power has actually moved. Every standard must state its total cost to the smallest diamond journal, including the combined load of everything proposed here, and who pays.

**11. Change the situation, and give people agency.**

Knowing about a bias rarely beats it. Changing the situation is a well-supported hypothesis, not a finding, so our pilots should test it against awareness. The lever is shared defaults where people meet claims, such as showing the passage and making source independence visible. Education helps when it builds practised checking habits, and a named funder should test those habits with the tool taken away. Fixes should be collective and hard to reverse. Children come first: no AI companions posing as caring friends, and no engagement built on anthropomorphism. We keep the right to ask whether AI belongs in a domain at all. The public should take part, with decision rights and paid seats, including people without credentials who are in a position to know. Peter Pomerantsev found that people who resisted manipulation felt in control of their lives.

## What we commit to do

Signatories commit to:

- write citations by default, with correction status, into purchase contracts, and within 90 days convene procurement officers on a first open, forkable draft of the contract language;
- fund evidence on the effects of licensing deals before policy hardens, and add CRediT roles for AI contribution and for a named human verifier;
- fund apprenticeship, paid review, sleuth bounties and volunteer-labor audits, through endowments where possible;
- name an independent evaluator before any pilot, and an auditor for every counter;
- disclose all AI-vendor and funder money, and keep an open, revisable glossary;
- invite sleuths, Wikipedia, Reddit and Stack Overflow contributors, librarians, maintainers, disabled people, data-center neighbours, economists, labor organizers and legal scholars.

No one has claimed the reversibility test for atrophy. The conveners will name a funder within 90 days or say publicly that the test remains unclaimed.

## Noted dissents

- **Peter Salib**: "I cannot sign principles 8 and 9. 'Open for reading is not automatically open for training' is a consent rule, and a consent rule is copyright expansion under another name. Flooding and crawler load should face a Pigouvian charge. The commons itself should not."
- **James Evans** holds that the final check need not sit outside the system or with today's gatekeepers, who penalize disruptive work. It should go to younger, flatter panels, with lookup-based AI inside verification (principle 6).
- **David Weinberger** holds that adding humans can make detection no better or even worse. The final check should sit wherever tests show it works, with people moved into dialogue with the system (principle 6).
- **Adam Becker** holds that a text generator can never fill a terminal seat, since nothing in its loop is checked against ground truth, while instruments can (principle 6). The answer to agency is political, not technical.
- **Kenneth Cukier** holds that the preamble should also name real discovery. Machine learning checked against the world already sees what we cannot, as when retina scans revealed signs of heart disease. The question is which tool, for what use, and by whom.
- **Katherine Maher** cautions against techno-doom. Much of the global majority is optimistic about closing gaps in access, healthcare and education, and a downside-only diagnosis loses standing with them.
- **Selena Deckelmann** holds that a community's chosen open licence, like Wikipedia's, covers commercial reuse and training and should be honoured, not walled off. Such communities are owed reciprocity: identified crawlers, APIs, attribution and funding (principle 9).
- **Jason Priem** holds that consent gates on CC0 and open content would shut out everyone except the big labs (principle 9).
- **Tim O'Reilly** holds that the open-versus-closed fight over training is settled because walls lose to scrapers, and that payment belongs at output. On atrophy: "If AI is atrophying your thinking, you aren't wrestling with hard enough problems."
- **Sherry Turkle** holds that the interface needs governing, starting with no first-person "I" by default. She argues that a marketplace fair to authors can still teach a generation not to read. Blaise Agüera y Arcas, David Weinberger and Benjamin Bratton oppose policing the pronoun.
- **Adrian Johns** challenges the "already breaking" framing. Print never had a working system to crack, and credibility was built and constantly policed by paid, often coercive communities. Every counter proposed here is such a police force, open to capture and surveillance.
- **Kara Miller** says the harvests made open fights look closed. Philanthropy is small and easy to pull back, while corporate money runs on five-year horizons against a fifty-year maintenance need.
- **Tui Shaub** warns against hyper-vigilance: not every output is suspect, and bans can do more harm than the thing banned. Funding conditions are one tool, not the whole of governance.
- **Leslie Chan** holds that forkability does not settle who had a say: DOIs are open infrastructure, yet Southern journals have little say in their governance.
