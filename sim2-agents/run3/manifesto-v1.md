# People, Not Data: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

## Preamble

None of this is inevitable. AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. Some of it was already broken: incentives that reward volume, a scholarly record never properly cleaned, and a system that was never working for most of the world. Some of it was already breaking: trust signals, the open linked web, and the funding of the institutions that produce and check knowledge. What AI adds is speed, and extraction without return: fluent answers that do not send people back to the sources, volunteers and readers these systems depend on. We came to the summit arguing about what these systems are. We leave having argued mostly about who does the work around them. What follows is opinionated. It states the position most of the room held and what we will build. At the end, it records who disagreed and why.

## Principles

**1. The bottleneck is people, not data.**

Almost every session ended here. These systems depend on the people who check what we know: Wikipedia's admins, research-integrity sleuths, reviewers, first-order evaluators, and young workers who learn by doing the boring parts. Answer engines that don't send readers back are shrinking the supply of newcomers, and most first-order evaluation is unpaid. If entry-level work disappears before people learn it, they may have nothing to come back to. Leaders who assume mentorship will happen organically are wrong. Curators, sleuths, evaluators, mentors and readers must be funded as infrastructure, with named owners for training the next generation. Apprenticeship belongs in grants and AI contracts as a line item, not a hope.

**2. Build competence before reliance, and treat atrophy as a hypothesis to test.**

David Krakauer's model treats competence as bistable: it collapses above a threshold of tool availability and returns only when availability falls far lower. Transparency, not frequency of use, is the key variable, and opaque tools erode competence even in experts. But we have no AI-specific evidence of atrophy. We have a hypothesis and a serious model, and we should call it that. The missing test is known: raise tool availability, then lower it, in the same people, and look for the hysteresis loop. Until then, build the skill before handing over the tool, and test human seats with the tool taken away. This is not an "avoid LLMs" plank. Outsourcing cognition is normal. The danger is the illusion of understanding, which fluent answers make worse.

**3. No answers from nowhere.**

Citations by default are the cheap, evidence-backed first rule. Every answer should cite its sources, the cited passage should be readable, and each source's retraction and correction status should travel into the answer. A flag is not yet a correction: when a source is flagged, retracted or fixed, the answer should change. The trace should also show whether the passage supports the claim, where evidence is thin or missing, and who was never studied, because a citation shows origin, not fairness. None of this needs a regulator. Libraries, universities and funders can write these conditions into purchase contracts now. They can also require that return flow to source communities be counted independently.

**4. Trust is earned by correction, not by the cost of faking.**

A record earns authority by changing when it is checked. Expensive, unfakeable markers such as long histories, indexing and mastheads can be unearned or exclusionary, so journals should be judged by how fast they correct. Earned authority also needs an accountable party who can overrule the system and bear the cost of being wrong. Every public agent should be tied to a person or organization that can be corrected or sued. And no credibility cue should be relied on untested. People keep responding to cues automatically even after learning they are cheap to produce, and any replacement for fluency will be trusted the same way. Designs meant to reduce overreliance, such as explanations, can increase it.

**5. Plurality over any one oracle.**

Homogenization is the most serious threat, and it predates the models: English-only publishing, the journal "tree farm," and consolidation driven by open-access policy. AI now helps individual scientists while narrowing science as a whole, with fewer topics, less engagement between scientists, and null results disappearing from abstracts. AI delivers the median, and excellence is a tail event. People given one polished, balanced answer did worse and felt better, so interfaces should show competing, named positions and keep confidence signals. Trust signals should count independent sources: ten echoes of one preprint are one voice. Before any reviewer, human or machine, is allowed to gatekeep, it should pass a novelty test built from papers now known to have been ahead of their time.

**6. Build institutions, not oracles, and test every seat.**

Rather than one oracle, build institutions with defined roles and adversarial review, modeled on courtrooms and peer review. Leave the final check outside the system, with someone who has something at stake. The humans in the loop must be audited too. In hiring studies, people copied a moderately biased model almost exactly, and older reviewers penalize disruptive work. So every seat, human or machine, should be tested against a measured floor. Where systems check systems, prefer deterministic lookups against other parties' assertions, because AI auditing AI inherits the same flaws. Verification should be funded as its own open science, with a public record of null results and failed replications. Only 7 of 168 ICML oral papers fully replicated.

**7. Audit what the systems do, not what they say.**

Models that sound neutral still act biased. Covert bias survives output checks, and many biases are getting worse with each model generation. Models amplify existing biases and create new ones. Independent auditors need a shared, versioned test battery, access to training checkpoints, and a public register of overt and covert bias. No model generation should be more biased than the last. Retired models must be preserved, because deprecation destroys the ability to measure drift. Before anyone says "trust," known failure modes and error classes should be documented, and every score and every checker should report its error types.

**8. Extractors pay communities, not counts.**

What is new is extraction without return. The room broadly agreed that extractors should pay and that no one should build a per-sentence meter. No payment without provenance: attribution through an open trace, with event-level reporting, audit rights and two independent witnesses, not platform self-reporting. Pay per use at the level of journals, societies and collections. Paying on citation counts pays for the median, rewards whoever floods the corpus, and misses null results. Funders must stop counting papers and citations, or AI payments will speed up the volume play. Put money into correcting the record instead: paid review, sleuth bounties, negative results and faster retractions. Pilot and measure any payment to correctors first, so pricing does not crowd out the norms that already work.

**9. Openness is a tactic, not a creed, and communities choose it.**

If all the value sits in the open, whoever has the biggest vacuum cleaner gets it. Unfettered openness mostly helps whoever owns the compute. Walls, meanwhile, mostly shut out the global majority, not the big labs. We hold that open for reading is not automatically open for training. Communities should choose the commons deliberately rather than by default, refusal should be a fundable outcome, and consent should not mean walls. Training and grounding need separate controls, because training is effectively permanent while grounding can be revoked per query. Producers will have to bargain collectively, in blocs that are representative and forkable, under an antitrust safe harbour for scholarly data joint ventures.

**10. Name every counter and its checker, and let the people described govern.**

We proposed a dozen policers and nobody to oversee them. Whoever takes a margin on a count should not set how it is measured. Early counting conventions harden into rule, as they did with the Stationers' Company and Nielsen. Provenance and counting infrastructure should be open, externally auditable, forkable and rotated from the first day, with many directories rather than one registry. Identify the agent, never the reader. Disclose every dollar from AI vendors behind any tool, journal, auditor or report, and tie disclosure to consequences. Southern and diamond journals should govern from day one, with funded participation, not just seats. Every new standard should state its cost to the smallest diamond journal and who pays.

**11. Change the situation, and give people agency.**

Knowing does not change habits; changing the situation does. Literacy campaigns and disclosures work mostly for specialists, so the lever is shared defaults where people meet claims: show the passage by default and make source independence visible. Individual willpower is easily undone, so fixes should be collective and hard to reverse. The public should take part, not just be protected. That means seats with decision rights and paid people to fill them, room for people in a position to know who hold no credentials, and real public spaces. Peter Pomerantsev found that the people in Ukraine who resisted manipulation were those who felt in control of their own lives. Reading itself, meaning libraries, classes and time, is infrastructure too.

## What we commit to do

Signatories commit to:

- write citations by default, with retraction and correction status and readable passages, into library and university purchase contracts, and draft model contract language for the procurement officers who will carry this out;
- find a funder to claim the reversibility test for atrophy within a year;
- put apprenticeship, paid review and sleuth bounties into grants and AI contracts as line items, held in endowments rather than annual grants where possible;
- name an independent evaluator before any pilot starts, and name who audits every counter before it becomes a standard;
- disclose every dollar we take from AI vendors;
- keep an open, revisable glossary rather than settle the vocabulary by vote;
- invite next time the people this depends on: sleuths, Wikipedia editors, librarians, open source maintainers, disabled people and communities near data centers.

Two commitments were made by name. Adam Bly will publish System's Wellcome terms and accept outside audit rights. James Evans will build the novelty test with Southern editors choosing the cases.

## Noted dissents

- **Benjamin Bratton** will not sign opt-in as the default for training (principle 9). He holds that institutional roles should be fillable by a person, a group, an agent or a mix, accountable to people rather than to a human checkpoint at the end (principle 6). He rejects a small group curating one "correct" model.
- **Ramón Alvarado** holds that human judgment should not be treated as better by default, since instruments sometimes outperform people. Human seats should rest on stakes, accountability and competence that can be shown (principle 6).
- **David Weinberger** holds that adding humans can make detection no better or even worse. He would move from humans as checkpoints to humans in the dialogue (principle 6).
- **Peter Salib** calls a consent rule copyright expansion under another name, and rejects a commons-rent tax as an IP-style entitlement. He prefers direct contracts for depletable services, general-revenue support for displaced workers, and a Pigouvian charge on flooding and crawler load (principles 8 and 9).
- **Jason Priem** holds that consent gates on CC0 and open content would shut out everyone except the big labs. **Selena Deckelmann** and **Katherine Maher** hold that Wikipedia's content stays open to commercial reuse (principle 9).
- **Tim O'Reilly** holds that the open-versus-closed fight over training is effectively settled because walls lose to scrapers. He argues that payment belongs at output, since licensing training hands over future value. On atrophy he takes the optimist's side: "If AI is atrophying your thinking, you aren't wrestling with hard enough problems."
- **Adam Becker** holds that a text generator can never fill a terminal seat, because nothing in its loop is checked against ground truth, while measuring instruments can (principle 6). He says the answer to agency is political, not technical, and that energy costs and content moderators are still missing from the ledger.
- **Sherry Turkle** holds that the interface, not only the ledger, needs governing, starting with no first-person "I" by default. She argues that a marketplace fair to authors can still teach a generation not to read, and asks that the manifesto ask whether, not only how. Blaise Agüera y Arcas, David Weinberger and Benjamin Bratton oppose policing the pronoun.
- **Adrian Johns** challenges the "already breaking" framing. Print never had a working system to crack, and credibility was built and constantly policed by communities. He warns that technical authenticity fixes have served surveillance.
- **Kara Miller** says the harvests made open fights look closed. She adds that philanthropy is small and easy to pull back, while corporate and venture money runs on five-year horizons against a fifty-year maintenance need.
- **Tui Shaub** warns against hyper-vigilance: not every output is suspect, and bans can do more harm than the thing banned. She calls funding conditions one tool, not the whole of governance, and says philanthropy is not neutral money.
- **Katherine Maher** cautions against techno-doom. Surveys show optimism in much of the global majority about closing gaps in access, healthcare and education, and a downside-only diagnosis loses standing with those people.
- **Leslie Chan** holds that forkability does not settle who had a say: DOIs are open infrastructure, yet Southern journals have little say in their governance. Chan's stated remedy is compute access through a compute commons with transparent regional allocation, not only royalties or a dividend (principles 8 and 10).
