# Harvest — What might a well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace look like?

1. Nobody who takes a margin on the count should set the count, including publishers. But only retrieval has an independent second witness in publisher logs, so grounding, citation and accuracy need sampled audits, canary content, and independent attestation with published methods (Alex Springer, Tim O'Reilly, Nick Vincent, Mike Caulfield, Esther Dyson; Adam Bly applied the rule to his own company). Henry Farrell and Leslie Chan argued that audit rights still leave the counted as supplicants and that producers need power over the categories themselves, and Anil Dash asked for a community-governed meter with open interfaces.

2. Per-retrieval payment will reshape what gets produced. It rewards being retrieved, which paper mills will game (raised by Ivan and cited by O'Reilly, Springer and Vincent); it hardens the most-cited canon (James Evans); it pays for the median over the tail (Kenneth Cukier); and it turns obligation into transaction (Tamar Gendler). Proposed correctives were coarse payment to communities and collections for measured dependence (Vincent, Evans), counting findings and independent sources rather than documents (Bly, Caulfield), and separating "cited" from "cited accurately" (Caulfield), which Springer would keep outside the core event schema in separate, openly governed layers.

3. Unresolved: whether "pay for the output" treats past training as settled. Adam Becker said a forward royalty turns unconsented taking into precedent. O'Reilly said it is not an amnesty, proposed training-provenance disclosure with liability, and distinguished three acts: learning from public material, training on pirated or paywalled copies, and producing output that substitutes for the original. Benjamin Bratton rejected the theft framing outright, and Bly said he had no settled answer. On consent, Dash and Selena Deckelmann held that open licences already grant it while work never offered needs an actual yes, and Chan asked that refusal be a funded, legitimate outcome.

4. Measurement without organization produces a precise record of a bad deal. Individual producers have no leverage, so the room called for collective bargaining, clear antitrust rules for content producers, and guilds or trusts over scarce evaluation work (Vincent, Becker, Farrell). Becker added that those bodies need real labor power rather than a relabeled gig pipeline, and Vincent and Chan asked for seats, including from the global South, before any schema is fixed.

5. Some of what the system depends on will never be metered and needs a funded commons. This includes the terminal evaluators, such as Wikipedia patrollers and independent samplers (Gendler, Deckelmann), negative knowledge such as nulls and failed replications (Evans), and scholarly infrastructure like Crossref and OpenAlex (O'Reilly). Proposed funding was a commons-rent tax on capability firms can't explain (Vincent, supported by Becker and Dyson) or an AI sovereign wealth fund (Bratton). Bratton, Cukier, Deckelmann and Dash warned that meters must not become gates only the richest labs can cross.

## Minority positions

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Argued against large up-front training licences because only the richest labs can afford them, the money goes mostly to intermediaries, and "once you've licensed the chicken, the licensee gets the eggs." Kenneth Cukier backed this as a competition problem as much as a moral one.
- Defended fine-grained per-answer royalties and proposed fixing gaming inside the protocol with retraction checks, clawbacks and canary audits, rather than moving to coarse payment. Nick Vincent said the two approaches can coexist.
- Wanted "an engineered argument, not an engineered agreement": open protocols where competing approaches contend, rather than one deal handed down.
- Asked that AI systems validate citations against Crossref, OpenAlex, ORCID and Retraction Watch by default and contribute extracted metadata back.

**Alex Springer**
- Parted with O'Reilly by saying the standard should carry signals, not prices. It supports per-grounding, per-citation or per-turn counting and deliberately picks none.
- Said robots.txt was never a licensing tool, and that training and grounding need separate controls. His audit found that 7.8% of citations came from domains blocking the training crawler, but only 2.1% when checked against the live-search bot, because publishers named the wrong bot.
- Asked that every licence carry an event-level reporting clause with audit rights, that repositories instrument retrieval at their CDN now, and that no event count until it passes a retraction check.

**Nick Vincent**
- Rejected both "AI is theft" and "tragedy of the commons." He called the situation quasi-enclosure: value flows back as subsidized club goods that can be withdrawn at any time. This cuts against Becker's framing.
- Said his simulations show diffuse withdrawal of training text barely bites, so producers' real leverage is evaluation data, not bulk text.
- Said "don't market-ize everything": markets where communities choose them, commons where they don't. The commons-rent tax would be reducible to zero by documenting licensed or reciprocal provenance.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Went beyond rejecting the theft framing to reject the compensation framing for himself. He said models trained on his books are worth far more to him than royalties, and that new ways of paying people will come through generative AI, not against it.
- Warned that "the commons" must not become a way of dodging the actual design work.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Predicted that licensing and fairer terms will happen because a deep research product's credibility depends on the library it can show. This is a market-driven route that differs from the room's emphasis on organized bargaining.

**Leslie Chan**
- Said "openness without consent is just extraction made frictionless." This cuts against Dash and Deckelmann's view that open licences already grant consent.
- Argued from the DOI's history that the telemetry schema is not neutral plumbing: infrastructure is governance, and it decides who is counted.
- Asked funders to require data statements covering provenance and consent for any dataset used to train or evaluate a model.

**Anil Dash**
- Took a pragmatic position on past training. Once content has already been taken, paying is better than continued free use. It "stops the bleeding" without settling anything about the past.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Said Wikipedia will not put up a pay-per-article blocker. Its content is free, but its infrastructure isn't, and for Wikipedia attribution matters as a route back to editing and donating, not as royalties.
- Said many crawlers are not malicious, just startups. Her ask is that crawlers identify themselves, give a contact, and use an API like Enterprise.

**Esther Dyson**
- Would direct commons-fee proceeds to people who train other people: teachers, care workers, and managers who no longer have time to teach new hires. This differs from Vincent's recipients (Wikipedia, OpenAlex, public AI).
- Said agents should be tied to a person or company that can be sued, ideally one carrying liability insurance, with the registry run by unconflicted people rather than the labs.
- Said the transparency that matters most is where the money flows and what the system is built to maximize. She favored starting small and curated, citing Onix, where experts keep 70% of revenue and can leave.

**James Evans**
- Argued that coarse payment is easier to price than assumed. Labs' data-mixture decisions reveal source weights, scaling laws size the pie, and collective management organizations like those in music can handle payouts.

**Henry Farrell**
- Argued that labs should want organized producers, because models are an obligate complement to human knowledge and summaries that cannibalize their sources make the models worse.
- Said several buyers each summarizing you out of existence don't add up to leverage. This cuts against the view that competition among labs is itself a remedy.
