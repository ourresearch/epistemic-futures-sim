# Harvest — Session 3: Knowledge Infrastructure and Tools: Commons, Enclosure, and Sovereignty

1. Credibility is maintained infrastructure, and its scarcest element is the human "terminal nodes" who do first-order checking: patrollers, retraction sleuths and people drawing out null results. These people are unpaid and declining in number (Johns, Gendler, Deckelmann, Oransky, Miller). The room broadly agreed they should be funded as a body rather than per item, with funders barred from governing (Shaub, Gendler, Hecht, Maher, O'Reilly, Brand), and that paid apprenticeship is needed because lost competence may not return (Miller, Turkle, Krakauer); Farrell, Hecht and Vincent added that checkers need collective bargaining and binding power over categories, not just seats.

2. Adam Bly's proposed finding-level provenance standard drew amendments the room largely accepted:
   - keying to any maintained, resolving identifier, not only DOIs (Chan, Bilder; conceded by Bly)
   - mandatory retraction and null-result fields
   - method, model version and known error classes for machine-extracted findings (Alvarado)
   - preserved model checkpoints and auditing by a different model family and by humans (Banaji)
   - funder disclosure (Becker, Dyson)
   - sources readable at the point of verification (Caulfield)
   - confidence based on independent lines of evidence rather than volume (Evans, Sloman, Agüera y Arcas)

   Sloman and Turkle warned that the layer itself may become a cue people trust instead of checking.

3. Who pays remained unresolved. Proposals included:
   - a revenue levy or public dividend (Dyson, Pomerantsev, Bratton), or Vincent's commons-rent tax with credits for licensed or reciprocal sources
   - paying directly for future work plus a Pigouvian charge on junk entering the record (Salib)
   - charging for access and service rather than content (Deckelmann, Maher)
   - a community-run meter (Dash, opposed by Deckelmann)
   - diamond endowments, with Bilder retracting "free data, paid service"

   Points of near-consensus: no per-retrieval payment (Weinberger, Evans, O'Reilly, Vincent), and counts not set by whoever takes a margin (Springer, Agüera y Arcas). Miller warned that a stable US public funder can no longer be assumed, and Bilder called for redundancy outside the US.

4. Non-commercial licensing was judged unforkable in practice (Priem, Bratton, Weinberger, Cukier), and Bly committed to take CC0 on data plus patent non-assertion to his board and report back publicly. Forking was also called exit without voice or funded curation (Shaub, Farrell), and CC0 was said to favour whoever sits closest to the forest (Hecht, Vincent), while Bilder defended it as insurance against the steward: "We need both."

5. Consent and sovereignty stayed contested. Becker called training on the record "fruit of the poisoned tree." Brand reported that authors want consent, attribution and compensation, and Chan asked that refusal be a legitimate, fundable outcome under CARE. Bratton held that open weights are the only form a Southern lab can fine-tune and that no transformative technology arrived by consent (disputed by Brand), against Alvarado and Chan's view that imported openness is not sovereignty. Agüera y Arcas proposed federated learning as a third route, while Dash and Cukier argued the forward question is fair return, not past legitimacy.

## Minority positions

**Adrian Johns**
- Rejected the session's commons-versus-enclosure frame. Openness and property came out of the same fight, so the question is where the line goes, who draws it and who keeps enforcing it, not whether to enclose.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Placed the main enclosure in bloc-level sovereign stacks that redefine sovereignty as the exclusive right to produce data, not in publisher paywalls. He cited proposals in Congress to bar Americans from using Chinese open models.
- Warned that things declared "nobody's property" become nobody's problem. He also said walling off regional archives produces "Galapagos archives," which cuts against Chan.
- Asked that documenting provenance stay cheap for small labs and labs outside the North Atlantic, so compliance doesn't become a moat.

**Jason Priem**
- Said CC0 settles who may use the data, not who decides what counts as a work or an affiliation, and that OpenAlex is still working out that governance.

**David Weinberger**
- Argued that open sources whose metadata doesn't interoperate are still silos. He repeated his warning that software enforcing attribution tends to be captured by rights-holders.

**Tui Shaub**
- Asked that grants set conditions on how the work is done rather than impose bans, and that they fund organised evaluators rather than gig platforms. She also asked that participants be paid before the schema's categories are fixed.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Called it hermeneutical injustice written into a schema when categories are fixed without the people they describe.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Warned against "trust us, we're professionals," citing the SEC's failure on auditor independence. She asked that checkers not see author names or journal prestige and that the checkers themselves be audited. Gendler backed this and added a fixed order of review.
- Noted that citation errors from AI tools already feed back into indexes like Google Scholar, polluting the commons without any enclosure.

**Adam Becker**
- Extended Johns's hidden-labour claim to the poorly paid content moderators and annotators, harmed by the work, who make models usable. This is part of why he rejects the enterprise.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Argued that licensing is coming anyway and that refusing to engage hands its terms to whoever shows up. This is directly against Becker's position.

**Leslie Chan**
- Asked that any pooled fund have transparent regional allocations and build regional infrastructure such as SciELO and Redalyc, rather than pay for compliance with Northern standards. He also asked for data statements covering consent and cultural scope, and for Local Contexts labels.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Proposed tiered disclosure: error classes and correction logs public, checkpoints to independent auditors but not to rivals. He also proposed designing a process for problems no one can anticipate rather than a body for every foreseeable one: "gardening, not architecture."

**Anil Dash**
- Proposed a manifesto line: "open record, swappable models, paid checkers, community-run meter."

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Stated that Wikipedia's content stays free, including for commercial use and training, which cuts against Brand's and Becker's consent demands. She asked instead that crawlers identify themselves and who runs them, use APIs like Wikimedia Enterprise, and that AI products attribute. Crawler identification was also backed by Pomerantsev, Salib and Springer and is not in the harvest.

**Esther Dyson**
- Disclosed that she has invested in Adam Bly's company.
- Put disclosure of money flows (who pays, who takes a margin, what the system maximises) ahead of disclosure of algorithms.

**James Evans**
- Reported that the share of abstracts with no null claims rose sharply after ChatGPT. He proposed distributing any fee collectively, weighted by data-mixture shares.

**Henry Farrell**
- Argued that the standard, the meter and the fee are all lossy summarisation technologies. On that view, deciding what counts as a "finding" or a "work" is where power sits, not neutral metadata.

**Brent Hecht**
- Called "open" a tactic rather than a value.
- Asked that unpaid checking labour be measured first so the fund has a number to argue from. He cited Reddit moderators at a minimum of 466 hours a day, about $3.5 million a year.

**Katherine Maher**
- Proposed separating whoever runs the infrastructure from whoever sets editorial policy, on the Wikimedia model: whoever runs the pipes shouldn't decide what counts as a finding. Bly noted that Maher sits on his board.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Proposed that any AI system generating or citing scholarly text automatically check every reference against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch. He also proposed that these systems contribute extracted metadata back, noting that over 60% of OpenAlex records lack full affiliations.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Said a retraction field only reports retractions that exist: about 0.2% of papers, against a real rate he puts near 2%. He proposed conflict-free bug bounties for sleuths, funded by publishers.
- Argued the root lever is upstream: funders and hiring that still count papers.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Argued that many actors gaming the record aren't after money, such as the Pravda network, so meters and clawbacks won't touch them. He asked that covert, coordinated, inauthentic behaviour aimed at the record be made illegal, with individual anonymity kept as a right.
- Asked that provenance serve citizens, not only auditors: people should be able to see why an answer showed one source and not another, and who paid for it.

**Peter Salib**
- Said the crawler registrar can't be a lab and that secondary use of the registry needs hard legal limits.
