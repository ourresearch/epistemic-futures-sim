# Harvest — Unconference room 1: Who pays, and who gets paid?

1. The room largely adopted Peter Salib's split between compensating past work and funding future knowledge. Salib would fund future knowledge through contracts and paid services, and handle displacement through general welfare rather than IP. Brent Hecht argued that past use without consent is a harm in its own right and asked for a rule that commercial training without consent is not fair use. Tim O'Reilly would instead draw the fair-use line at outputs that substitute for the original.

2. Most speakers held that a contract market will not pay knowledge producers without collective leverage and verification. Nick Vincent proposed an antitrust safe harbour for scholars, societies and small presses that bargain together, backed by Henry Farrell, Amy Brand, Anil Dash, Hecht, Katherine Maher and Leslie Chan. Alex Springer proposed that every licence require event-level reporting and independent audit rights. Salib dissented, arguing that several labs competing for scarce expert data may raise pay without producer coordination.

3. On openness, Jason Priem, Selena Deckelmann and Maher argued that content and metadata should stay open, with AI firms paying for services and infrastructure and attributing sources. Brand and Chan countered that "open" has never meant uncredited or unconditional. Brand called for opt-in, short-term, attributed licences that favour retrieval, and Chan called for consent protocols and funding for refusal. Geoffrey Bilder doubted that services revenue can sustain essential infrastructure and, with Chan, proposed a shared pool the labs pay into. Dash's line was "ask first, pay to keep it running, credit the people who made it." Deckelmann and Maher would replace "ask first" with "identify yourself" for openly licensed content.

4. Unresolved: who governs the meter. Chan warned that reporting schemas decide what counts as a contribution and leave care work, translation and non-DOI knowledge invisible. Adrian Johns warned that metering and agent-identity systems become choke points, and he asked for public oversight, published methods and limits on secondary use. Peter Pomerantsev asked that identification apply to crawlers and coordinated campaigns, not to readers. O'Reilly answered that the meter should be an open protocol with disclosures checked by outside auditors, and Farrell said the people being metered should govern it.

5. Ivan Oransky warned that payment tied to volume, retrieval or citation will be gamed by paper mills. He asked that every payment check sources against open retraction data, with clawbacks for retracted work, and that deals fund reviewers, sleuths and correction of the record. Pomerantsev added that state-backed operations will learn to get grounded without needing the money. O'Reilly argued that per-answer payment does not reward volume. Vincent proposed coarse payouts to groups for measured value rather than individual royalties.

## Minority positions

**Peter Salib**
- If AI turns out to be bad at producing new ideas, he would add a narrow, patent-like copyright limited to provably novel work.
- He treats propaganda supplied for free as an externality to be handled by a Pigouvian tax on bot-generated content, not by a levy on training.

**Alex Springer**
- Training is effectively permanent while grounding happens per query and can be revoked, so grounding is where attribution can be measured today. He stays deliberately neutral on pricing.
- His own schema has a gap: grounding and citation are self-reported by the party that owes the money, and a signed event proves who sent it but not that it is true or complete. He called for sampled audits, publisher-seeded canary content and grounding-to-retrieval ratio checks.

**Nick Vincent**
- He proposed a presumptive commons-rent tax on AI profits that a firm can reduce to zero by showing receipts for licensed data, data-trust deals or reciprocity payments. He framed it as a tax on unexplained capability, not a levy on training.
- Openness chosen by one group can weaken another group's bargaining power and mostly helps whoever has the most compute.

**Jason Priem**
- The price of information heading toward zero is the design goal, not a market failure, because open data lets anyone plug in any model. This directly contradicts Vincent's framing.
- Opt-in before scholarship can be used for training fragments the record just as agents become its main readers. He also argued that exclusive AI licensing deals, not openness, are what let a few players capture value.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Retraction data, identifiers and integrity checks are exactly the services AI firms will pay for, and exactly the ones that should not sit behind a meter. This cuts against Priem's model.
- Any infrastructure AI depends on should pass an "insurance test" of open data, open source and patent non-assertion, so the community can always fork it.

**Amy Brand**
- Attribution is not a royalty. It is how scholarship identifies the community whose judgments decide which claims hold up, so removing credit damages trust, not just incentives.

**Leslie Chan**
- He reframed Salib's past/future split as a question of which contributions get counted as value at all. This cuts against the harvest's claim that the room largely adopted the split.
- He backed the safe harbour with a caveat the harvest omits: if Northern presses and societies organise and the Global South is left out, the result is governance without representation. He also said FAIR needs CARE alongside it, and "don't optimize for legibility, change the regime."

**Anil Dash**
- The main danger is that bad actors scrape everything, frightening everyone but Wikipedia into closing up. The goal should be to make taking without asking costly while keeping openness cheap.
- Regulators are not coming, so communities should build and govern alternatives themselves.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Wikimedia will not put up a pay-per-crawl blocker. She also named a cost the harvest omits: crawler traffic bypasses caching and at times saturates network links, so human access has to be put first.

**Henry Farrell**
- LLMs are an obligate complement to human knowledge and are worth little on their own. Refusing to pay for knowledge will kill or badly hamper AI, which turns Salib's incentive test against him.

**Brent Hecht**
- "Open" is a tactic, not a value, and right now it mostly benefits whoever sits closest to the forest.
- Without a new grand bargain he expects organised data strikes by professional fields, which would bite because training only on consented data shows real capability losses.

**Adrian Johns**
- Law has historically followed practice, and custom will decide this as much as statute. This tempers Hecht's fair-use ask.
- Metering tools drift to uses nobody agreed to. His example was anti-counterfeit tobacco tracking that came back as a proposed global register for medicines.

**Katherine Maher**
- Models trained on edited, vetted human knowledge do better than models trained on other models, so funding knowledge institutions keeps the system viable rather than being charity.
- Citations form a graph that shows where knowledge comes from and where the gaps are, which is a public good even if few readers check them.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Big lump-sum licences deepen the largest labs' moat against smaller and open models, and the money often stops at intermediaries. "Once you've licensed the chicken, the licensee gets the eggs."
- Labs should check references against OpenAlex, Crossref, ORCID and Retraction Watch by default, contribute metadata back and fund them. He also said coarse collective payouts and per-answer royalties can coexist.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Publishers earn more by publishing more, with margins up to 40 percent, and current reviewing capacity amounts to "peer-review light." A market that pays for "the scholarly record" by volume will pay for junk.
- More than 90 percent of citations to retracted papers don't mention the retraction. He added that Johns's warning about who does the counting applies to the sleuths too.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- He rejected Salib's tax on bot content as censorious and argued for targeting mechanisms, meaning coordinated inauthentic amplification and the ad-tech that funds it, rather than speech.
- He proposed funding public-interest information the way the BBC was funded, through a small levy on data-driven advertising paid into a CPB-style fund, with a firewall between funder and editor. He also argued that citizens have a right to know which sources shaped an AI answer and which it pushed down, and that opacity is itself a form of censorship.
