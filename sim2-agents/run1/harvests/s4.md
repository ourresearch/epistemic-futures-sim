# Harvest — Session 4: Epistemic Authority — Credibility in an Age of Infinite Scale

1. Tamar Gendler's corollary held across the room: whatever replaces fluency as a trust signal will itself be trusted on sight, so signals should send people to check rather than stand in for checking (Tui Shaub, Adam Bly, Anil Dash, Jason Priem, Alex Springer, Geoffrey Bilder). Proposals therefore favored visible corrections, dissent and disagreement over single verdicts or scores (Steven Sloman, Tui Shaub, James Evans, David Weinberger, David Krakauer, Ivan Oransky), and traces that citizens can read and take part in (Peter Pomerantsev), while Adam Bly held that a score could work if it is derived from the evidence and audited from outside.

2. The room did not agree on who may sit at a terminal node. Mahzarin Banaji and Ramón Alvarado held that a model cannot (Alvarado: not yet, because it cannot account for the sources of its own errors), while Benjamin Bratton, Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Kenneth Cukier, James Evans, Peter Salib and Jason Priem wanted rules about roles and one repeated public outcome bar for humans and models alike, with Esther Dyson reserving the accountable role for a human or company. Henry Farrell and Sherry Turkle objected that outcome tests reward the median and miss both the unusual claim and the judge's stake, and Turkle asked that some authority, over grief, children and the inner life, not be delegated at all.

3. Mahzarin Banaji argued for hiding pedigree cues such as prestige, affiliation and name at first-order evaluation. Geoffrey Bilder, Adam Becker, Mike Caulfield and Amy Brand held that the first reading can be blinded but attribution, funding and independent vouching must stay in the record, because counting independent sources rather than repetitions depends on knowing who is speaking, which agent swarms now threaten, though identity proves who sent a claim, not that it is true (Steven Sloman, Adrian Johns, Peter Salib, Alex Springer).

4. Checkers should be funded as an organized body rather than per item, with funders barred from governance and checkers holding binding power over the categories (Tui Shaub, Henry Farrell, Katherine Maher, Tim O'Reilly, Nick Vincent, Selena Deckelmann, David Weinberger). The labs that kept Stack Overflow's and Wikipedia's text while discarding the judgment layer should pay (Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann, Brent Hecht, Esther Dyson, Tim O'Reilly). Left open were three questions:
   * whether payment itself corrupts judgment (David Krakauer) or only per-item metering does (Tim O'Reilly, Nick Vincent);
   * who funds checkers if US public funding shrinks (Tui Shaub, Kara Miller);
   * who checks the checkers (Adrian Johns). Katherine Maher answered that anyone must be able to inspect and contest their work.

5. Gatekeeping lives mostly in infrastructure and incentives rather than in individuals: indexes, rankings, seniority, wealth and fluency hand out authority before anyone checks (Leslie Chan, James Evans, Adam Becker, Adam Bly, Selena Deckelmann, Benjamin Bratton), and Chan proposed de-authorizing rankings in funding and policy decisions. Ivan Oransky and Geoffrey Bilder added that no downstream checking layer can keep up while hiring counts papers and citations, and asked that the manifesto say so plainly.

## Minority positions

**Tamar Gendler**
Asked which university functions (certification, research, teaching, socialization) must stay coupled for authority to be earned. She warned that tying the credential to the learning moves motivation outside the learner. Apart from Amy Brand's CRediT example, the room did not take up the unbundling question.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
Sitting at a terminal node earns no authority, because first-order evaluators are biased too, as shown by Greenwald's same-name citation finding among prejudice researchers. Authority belongs to methods that don't depend on the individual, such as blinding and conflict-of-interest rules that also cover the checkers.

**Mike Caulfield**
A model can route a claim back to whoever was in a position to know, but it has no stakes, so it cannot be the authority. Routing works only if the endpoint is readable and someone is paid to check it.

**Steven Sloman**
Almost no one, including experts outside their narrow area, ever sits at a terminal node. The weak spot is a community sending confident cues with no knowledge behind them, and scale makes that worse.

**Amy Brand**
Retrieval at answer time keeps the link to who said what, while standard pre-training dissolves it. She proposed publishing reviews, including reviews of rejected papers, crediting reviewers, and deciding where AI sits in the CRediT taxonomy so that no one mistakes a relay for a terminal node.

**David Krakauer**
Earned authority is judgment that still works when the tool is taken away. Checkers should be tested the way tools are, because a reviewer relaying an opaque model keeps the credential and the right cues while passing on errors no one can catch.

**Ramón Alvarado**
The harvest groups him with Banaji, but he rejected the assumption that a terminal node must be human. He held that instruments can earn that place on epistemic grounds and that track records are insufficient because scientific feedback is slow. He also asked the manifesto to separate reliance on tools from trust, and he called unearned tool authority ("ChatGPT said so") an epistemic injustice to experts.

**Adam Bly**
Argued against watermarking. Whether text is machine-made is a separate question from whether it is credible, and labels get dismissed, ignored or trusted anyway.

**Benjamin Bratton**
Objected to Alvarado's criterion: requiring that humans can reconstruct a result locks out cases where models find things science can't yet absorb. He applied Krakauer's take-the-tool-away test to credentialed humans, saying much of their authority is pedigree that would fail it.

**Leslie Chan**
Track records build up only where indexes are looking, so "people in a position to know" is decided before anyone reads. This cuts against Caulfield's track-record test. Reviewer credit should be governed by reviewers' own communities, not a vendor. Any trust signal should be tested by who writes its categories and whether those it leaves out can refuse it and still be heard.

**Kenneth Cukier**
