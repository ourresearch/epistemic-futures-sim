# Harvest — Unconference room 3: Cognitive atrophy: real phenomenon or not?

1. Only David Krakauer took a firm position that AI-driven cognitive atrophy is real. His model has competence collapsing to a low dependent floor once tool availability passes a tipping point, and that point is set by how much was learned beforehand and how transparent the tool is. Tamar Gendler and Kara Miller said they had no data on AI and offered a habit-formation framework and a phone analogy instead.

2. The room converged on the view that recovery depends on what was laid down first. Krakauer argued that collapse is hysteretic, distinguishing storage from retrieval strength. Sherry Turkle, prompted by Krakauer and Gendler, narrowed her claim that capacities "come back" to capacities that were depressed, not ones that were never built.

3. Gendler, Miller, Turkle and Esther Dyson agreed that changing settings works better than warning individuals. Gendler proposed that institutions and tool builders carry that responsibility, and Miller added that every recommendation needs a named implementer and should be acted on early and studied as it goes, not held until proof arrives. Turkle cautioned against writing off individual agency, and Dyson called for a cultural movement as well as institutions.

4. Miller identified first jobs as a cracking pipeline for building competence before the tool. Krakauer called this the core mechanism, not a side issue, and Turkle noted that mentorship is conversation and the first thing cut. Dyson proposed paying front-line trainers with a fee on AI companies for training on shared public knowledge.

5. The room did not settle how to judge AI tools. Krakauer proposed judging a tool by what competence remains when it is removed, and funding studies that follow the same learners through acquisition, reliance and removal. Dyson argued that effort-minimization is a business choice, not a law of nature, and that tools should be judged by whether they free human time for teaching and conversation. Turkle asked whether some places should have no AI at all: the seminar, the family table, the first job.

## Minority positions

**Tamar Gendler**
- Cast the problem as partly motivational (the "G. I. Joe Fallacy" and the 2012 problem-set example). Outsourcing looks like beating the system when a course is framed around credit, so assessment should be designed around understanding.
- Disagreed with Dyson that "we are starting to think like LLMs." The brain has always done the most likely thing given what it did before, and what's new is only how cheap the easy path has become.

**Kara Miller**
- Framed phones, and by extension AI, as a collective trap that no one can opt out of alone, which is why the setting has to do the work.
- Cautioned against leaning on institutions as Gendler proposed. U.S. school policy moves district by district and slowly, and tool designers understand brain science better than the teachers expected to counter them.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Widened the question from cognitive atrophy to atrophy of the self. She cited a 40 percent decline in empathy markers among college students and argued that AI threatens the capacity for attachment by teaching people that "pretend empathy" is enough.
- Asked the summit to address reading, not only writing. Students who get a ChatGPT summary of Proust can report what happened but lose the experience, and with it imagination, empathy and the joining of thought and feeling.

**David Krakauer**
- Disagreed with Gendler that habit explains most of it. Map readers and satellite-navigation users diverge in spatial memory even with equal exposure, so the tool's opacity matters independently of use.
- Warned of a role reversal that is very hard to undo: when goals are uncertain and the tool dominates, the tool starts setting goals and the human becomes its instrument.
- Said an opaque tool reaching billions of people with little prior competence sits "right in the collapse regime." He also called for expecting people to reconstruct and check AI output, not just accept it.

**Esther Dyson**
- Framed the problem as "information diabetes" and argued that humans are converging on LLM-like thinking: fluent but without understanding. Meanwhile the shared context built by working through problems with colleagues is shrinking.
- Argued that business models paying for dependence mean "the cabinet is fighting upstream." She called sycophantic companions parasites and said these tools should be treated as services with accountable owners, not as speech.
- Said she doesn't want the room to sound anti-AI. She split friction into "stupid friction" (paperwork, scheduling) that AI should remove and "good friction" (teaching, conversation) it should make time for, which sits in tension with Turkle's call for some AI-free places.
