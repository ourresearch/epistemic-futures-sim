# Harvest — Session 2: AI as Epistemic Actor: Risks, Capabilities, and Governance

1. Esther Dyson argued that accountability must land on people who can be sued, fired or embarrassed, and Ramón Alvarado, Peter Salib and Blaise Agüera y Arcas added that a registry should record the whole chain (trainer, fine-tuner, deployer, prompter) rather than name one scapegoat. The room did not resolve whether agents should also hold seizable assets: Salib, Benjamin Bratton and, provisionally, Agüera y Arcas supported it, while Sherry Turkle, Adam Becker, Kenneth Cukier, Anil Dash, Henry Farrell, Steven Sloman and Katherine Maher rejected it as an illusion or a place for builders to hide.

2. Alvarado, Adam Bly, Alex Springer and Adrian Johns agreed that registering an agent identifies who acted, not whether a claim is true. Bly, Jason Priem, Agüera y Arcas, Geoffrey Bilder and Amy Brand proposed a separate layer that resolves each claim to open, retraction-checked records. The room split on fabrication: David Krakauer, Becker and Brand called it intrinsic, while Mike Caulfield, Dash, Priem and Bilder called it a measurable defect and asked for per-release error rates to be published.

3. Becker, Mahzarin Banaji, James Evans, Bratton, Agüera y Arcas, Springer, Nick Vincent, Ivan Oransky and Brand converged on a conflict rule: whoever profits from, pays for, or is subject to an audit does not control it, and no model family grades its own work. Leslie Chan, Johns, Farrell, Bilder and David Weinberger warned that any registry or "vetted literature" list becomes a choke point, and they asked who writes the schema. Their answers were:
   * endowment funding and forkable data (Bilder)
   * excluded communities seated before the schema is written (Chan, Bilder)
   * collective power over the categories (Farrell)
   * registration of agents acting on the commons, never readers (Selena Deckelmann, Maher, Springer, Peter Pomerantsev)

4. Tamar Gendler, Turkle, Kara Miller and Brent Hecht argued that disclosure alone rarely changes behavior, so governance should target design defaults. The proposals included:
   * friction and visible disagreement (Tui Shaub, Evans)
   * measuring how people perform once the tool is removed (Krakauer)
   * testing whether mitigations actually improve decisions (Hecht)
   * accountability understood as seeing, undoing and contesting a system while it runs (Deckelmann, Maher, Weinberger)

   Tim O'Reilly would aim disclosure at auditors through machine-readable telemetry instead. On persona, Turkle wants no first person and recognizably mechanical systems, Bratton called that another mask, and Shaub would target smoothness instead.

5. Hecht, Vincent, Miller, Caulfield, Oransky, Deckelmann and Maher said every proposed mechanism runs on human checking labor that is unpaid and shrinking. Hecht, Vincent and Farrell added that evaluators gain leverage only if they are organized. Who pays remained open:
   * funders and philanthropy (Shaub, Caulfield)
   * the labs and platforms (O'Reilly, Dash, Deckelmann)

   Oransky and Becker held that the payer must not control the checking, and Oransky and Evans said funders and universities must stop rewarding paper counts upstream. Shaub, Miller and Johns asked that every proposal name who runs it, who pays for it, and who checks the checkers.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
- Proposed mandatory liability insurance with disclosed premiums, saying she trusts an insurer with money at risk over a marketing department. The harvest does not mention insurance.
- Named the core danger "information diabetes": people coming to think like LLMs, fluent and easy but without understanding.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Said insurance can price how often errors happen and how large they are, but not their nature or source. Premiums address harm, not warrant.
- Asked that registration also record an agent's known failure modes and error classes, and that outputs be traceable for error analysis.

**James Evans**
- Reported that off-the-shelf LLM judges of scientific hypotheses sometimes did worse than chance. He set the bar for any evaluator at the roughly 61% agreement between two human reviewers.

**Peter Salib**
- Said insurance fails for catastrophic losses that no premium can cover, and that those require targeted rules up front.
- In his reply, said nothing in his proposal rests on AI having minds. An A-corp is owned by humans, can be sued in its own name, and has its veil pierced if it is undercapitalized or used to direct wrongdoing. He framed the real choice as anonymous swarms of copies versus registered names with seizable assets. The harvest does not carry this rebuttal.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Argued that before writing integration rules, the room should ask where these systems belong at all. Therapy, children's companionship and grief may be places they should not be.

**David Krakauer**
- Said an insurer cannot price errors that no claimant can detect.
- Called for competence before reliance in research training. He said being in the loop protects no one; only reconstructing and checking the output does.

**Tui Shaub**
- Accepted bounded restrictions but said prohibition should not be the default frame for knowledge, pushing back on Turkle.
- Proposed that funders make audits, failure records and the take-the-tool-away test conditions of grants now, without waiting for a statute.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Backed insurance, with the caveat that an insurer can only price what someone has measured.
- Said alignment can hide biased associations that still drive decisions ("LLM hypocrisy"). Registration should therefore record behavior on a shared, versioned battery of indirect tests, not self-report.
- Asked that checkpoints from each training stage be available to auditors and that old models be kept rather than retired.
- Proposed that no new model generation may be more biased than the last. She also proposed that developers share liability with deployers, and carry more of it when a known bias went undisclosed.

**Adam Becker**
- Called for disclosure of funding and vendor relationships on every tool, journal and report that claims to say what is true, citing Inference magazine.
- Said model output should not carry authority anywhere being wrong matters, and that nobody has shown these systems speed up science. Bratton and Caulfield disagreed.

**Adam Bly**
- Called on Congress to rebuild the Office of Technology Assessment.
- Said confidence should be calculated from independent studies, not repeated from the model's own view of itself.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Rejected Becker's "never carry authority" rule. He said authority should come from how roles are arranged, as in a courtroom, with roles that a person, an agent or both could fill.
- Said to keep "human in the loop" but not let it turn into "human at the centre."

**Mike Caulfield**
- Proposed "no answers from nowhere": the model as a conduit, not an arbiter, giving a table of named, linked, conflicting sources, with checking done outside the model.
