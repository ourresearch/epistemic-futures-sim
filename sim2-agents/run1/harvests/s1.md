# Harvest — Session 1: The Epistemic Landscape

1. **Homogenization is the headline risk, but the room split on whether it is inherited or new.** Farrell put homogenization ahead of noise, and Evans backed it with data: AI-using scientists publish and are cited more, but topic range shrinks, engagement falls 22 percent, and the decade-long rise in null results reversed after ChatGPT. Chan, Deckelmann, Priem and Evans held that the narrowing predates the models, while Banaji, joined by Bratton and Salib, argued the models are not mirrors: they exaggerate human biases and form new ones, such as a preference for machine-written text, that cannot come from the data.

2. **Fluency no longer signals care, but credibility never rested on signals alone.** Farrell, Becker and Vincent agreed polish is dead as a signal, while Johns, Caulfield and Oransky argued it was always cheap and credibility came from policing that is now strained and unfunded. Johns and Springer asked that any registry or context layer name who polices it and who pays, and Springer held that nobody who takes a margin on a count should set how it is counted. Bilder called for open context with exit, Chan warned of "comply, or be invisible," and Gendler and Turkle held that knowing fluency is cheap does not neutralize it, so institutions must change the situations where claims are met.

3. **Value has stopped flowing back to the producers the models depend on, and who acts is unresolved.** Farrell (obligate complement), Hecht (the broken grand bargain), O'Reilly, Deckelmann and Springer (roughly 38,000 pages crawled per referral) described the break, and Vincent called the present state quasi-enclosure: precarious, not lost. On remedies, Hecht expects data strikes, Vincent found leverage only in collective action over scarce expert evaluation data, and Chan asked whose power and whose seat. Brand warned that "openness at any cost" speeds enclosure, Priem dissented that gating training recreates it, and Oransky placed the root cause upstream in publish-or-perish incentives, with funders as the lever.

4. **Atrophy is in scope, framed as judgment-bearing roles and the pipeline that fills them.** Farrell and Agüera focused on hollowed roles, and Krakauer proposed judging a tool by what is left when it is taken away. Sloman argued competence was never individual and located the harm in the knowledge illusion and the automation paradox, and Banaji measured conformity: people copied a biased model's preferences. Miller, Dash and O'Reilly identified the thinning entry-level pipeline, and Turkle added reading. Weinberger, O'Reilly and Caulfield cautioned against measuring loss against an imagined individual competence, and Miller disputed Shaub's rejection of prohibition.

5. **The ontology stayed open, and a minority objected to a loss-only diagnosis.** Positions ranged from cultural technology or lossy reflection (Farrell, Cukier), to intelligence (Agüera, Bratton), text generators (Becker), epistemic technologies (Alvarado), and goal-pursuing agents (Salib). Agüera and Salib argued the answer changes the remedy, while Springer argued effort belongs elsewhere. Cukier and Weinberger held that a clear-eyed diagnosis must also record discovery.

## Minority positions

**Henry Farrell**
- Detectors are not the answer to cheap fluent text. "Your AI denying my AI" is an arms race, not a fix.

**Leslie Chan**
- What was already broken includes epistemic injustice. English as the default, and promotion tied to elite Anglo journals, meant the system never worked for much of the Global South.
- AI training is the newest rent layer on an existing publisher stack (Elsevier's publishing, Scopus, SciVal and rankings data), not a new crisis.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Crawlers that bypass caching have saturated Wikipedia's network links: the content stays free, but the servers don't. Pundits have wrongly predicted Wikipedia's death for twenty years, so she asked the room to stick to numbers.

**Katherine Maher**
- Wikipedia's bias is visible and fixable, which she held makes it better than a model whose weights nobody can inspect.
- Public media's loss of more than a billion dollars in federal funding belongs in the diagnosis as part of how the ecosystem is being drawn down.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- He warned against a supply-side-only diagnosis. People believe what fits their group, so better provenance won't win back publics who distrust whoever issues it.
- Training data is now a propaganda target (the Pravda network aims at models, not readers). The surrender in scope is civic, and opacity is itself censorship, because the right to receive information includes seeing how an answer was shaped.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- If the problem is intelligence concentrated in a few systems, the remedy is plurality: many models, many training traditions, and structured disagreement among agents and people. He also called for auditors who don't grade their own homework.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Most crawlers are probably buggy bots built with coding assistants, not malicious ones. The realistic threat is systems slowly filling with subtly wrong data and code, which will take a decade to clean out without help from the same tools.

**Amy Brand**
- In her survey of MIT Press authors, most wanted consent, attribution and compensation, and only 3 percent supported unregulated use, so the unease is a structural grievance, not panic. "Open for reading" should not mean "open for training without credit."
- Hallucination is a feature of these systems, not a bug, and better training data won't remove it.

**David Krakauer**
- He rejected the roles-versus-brains distinction Farrell and Agüera drew, arguing they are the same dynamics at two scales and a society can lock into collective dependence. He also resisted leaving atrophy as "jury out," since skill decay is among the best-measured effects in the literature.

**Tui Shaub**
- Friction is how failure becomes visible, and smooth surfaces hide dissent, null results and dead ends. Design should surface disagreement and error on purpose, keep public records of failures, and build chance into discovery.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Errors spread across billions of weights come out with the same confidence as correct results. We can measure how often a system is wrong but not the source or nature of its errors, and a context layer tells you where a result came from, not why it failed.
- Administrators deferring to metrics was already an epistemic injustice. Neither machines nor humans should get default deference.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Humans have become less biased over the centuries, while models are getting more biased from one generation to the next. People who copy a model's bias produce the next model's training data. She also warned against romanticizing human judgment, arguing that behavior is what harms people and what can be audited.

**Adam Becker**
- Hallucination is how these systems normally work, "intelligence" is marketing, and the claim that AI speeds up science is the companies' story, unsupported by evidence. The diagnosis must name the handful of companies responsible and ask who pays the auditors.

**Adam Bly**
- What is new is that AI grew up in unregulated, A/B-testable consumer settings, the opposite of science and medicine on every count.
- Whether something was machine-written is separate from whether it is credible. Detectors and watermarks answer only the first question, and he called for an audit trail back to data, methods and prompts that carries retraction status and counts independent studies.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- He accepted Chan's diagnosis but drew the opposite conclusion. Cheap access to artificially scarce expertise is the biggest gain the Global South could get, and the fix is more languages and archives in the models, not small curated ones.
- Some of the morning's alarm is old gatekeepers defending their authority to narrate, and he held the diagnosis should say so.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Against Becker, he argued fabrications are not random. They occur in learnable situations and can be mitigated.
- The old web of free, linked sources is mostly gone, and verification depends on reaching the source. He named scaffolding as a third option besides offloading and doing nothing, and asked that tools be used to make refutation cheaper, with funded tests of whether they do.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- These tools produce a "frame monoculture": they give the median, while excellence is a tail event. He cited four-fifths of ChatGPT users being unable to quote essays they had written minutes earlier, and asked that firms be named case by case, not as one villain.

**Anil Dash**
- He proposed a coercion test. Much adoption came through forced upgrades, defaults and AI-first mandates, and a technology that has to be forced on people is suspect. Against "it's all marketing," he pointed to roughly 900 million weekly users who mostly aren't forced.
- Defensive reactions are causing collateral damage to research: walled content, blocked Internet Archive access, and closed APIs. After Twitter closed its API, over 60 percent of the research projects that used it stalled or were abandoned.

**Esther Dyson**
- The black box that matters is the people and business models, not the weights, so transparency should target money flows and what systems are optimized for (engagement, retention, dependence). She called the result "information diabetes."
- Companies training on a public asset should pay for it like water rights, with the money going to teachers, curators and managers who mentor new hires.

**James Evans**
- He pushed back on the panel's comfort with human gatekeeping. Older reviewers, who get the most say, are most likely to resist disruptive work, so the question is which humans are in the loop and from which distributions. The flood is too big to keep AI out of verification altogether.

**Tamar Gendler**
- She rejected Becker's dividing line, arguing that human brains also do the most likely thing given what came before. The question is what any pattern-follower does when nothing in the situation pushes back.

**Brent Hecht**
- His review found that overreliance produces worse results than either the human or the AI working alone. He framed this as a design choice that can be designed against.

**Adrian Johns**
- Tag-and-register systems worked only where a policing culture already existed. On their own they became strong but brittle and drifted to other uses, and the tobacco industry championed them.
- Automation moves human labour into data curation rather than removing it, and that labour has to be paid for.
