# Harvest — Session 6: Closing Synthesis

1. **Stopping paper counts comes first, but a declaration won't do it.** Kenneth Cukier, Kara Miller, Tim O'Reilly and Peter Salib agreed that ending the counting of papers and citations comes first. Ivan Oransky, pointing to DORA, argued it needs enforcement: misconduct reports public by default, retraction notices that say whether the institution investigated, and audit power for ORI or its successor. He added that correction speed is itself a metric Goodhart applies to.

2. **Unresolved: who is the payer of last resort?** Kara Miller asked who pays if the labs balk while federal science funding is cut, and the room did not answer it. Esther Dyson pledged her own money and proposed mandatory liability insurance with disclosed premiums. Henry Farrell, Brent Hecht and Tim O'Reilly said labs will pay only once checkers organize and can withhold their work. Peter Pomerantsev proposed an advertising levy and Ivan Oransky an endowment. Katherine Maher said public media cannot be that payer, and Adrian Johns and Steven Sloman said they had no answer.

3. **"Checkers hold the categories" needs to be spelled out in the charter.**
   * James Evans asked for evaluators drawn deliberately from younger, outsider and other-field pools, and for active collection of unwritten negative results.
   * Henry Farrell asked that checkers ratify and can veto definitions, that anyone proposing a revision justify it in public, and that the charter include rotating wardens and fixed-term review.
   * Adrian Johns said the charter must state whose law its limits run under, since paper mills, state operations and crawlers are not guild members.
   * Katherine Maher asked that local public-interest journalism be named as checking the fund pays for.

4. **Most people read signals as cues, not as prompts to check.** Steven Sloman proposed testing every signal on someone who checks nothing, to see whether it moves them toward truth or only toward trust. Peter Pomerantsev argued that belief tracks belonging and asked that citizens be able to see and contest how answers are shaped. Kara Miller and Tamar Gendler held that changing the setting (defaults, grant conditions, hiring) works better than labels and disclosures.

5. **Open design questions.**
   * Tamar Gendler asked whether paying for checking crowds out the sense of obligation that motivates volunteers. Brent Hecht agreed this applies to his proposal to count checking labor, which he would design with the volunteer communities.
   * Gendler also asked who owns the question of which university functions must stay coupled.
   * James Evans and Henry Farrell asked that someone fund a test of whether reviewers, human or model, recognize work later found to matter.
   * Peter Salib warned that the plan assumes today's capabilities and should be built for goal-pursuing agents. He proposed registered, capitalized agents with human owners as a partial answer to Johns's jurisdiction problem.

**Kara Miller**

**Esther Dyson**

**James Evans**

- He endorsed Henry Farrell's point that agreement-based evaluation rewards the typical case, which cuts against Brent's bar as stated. He said the tail test may fail and should be published either way.

**Henry Farrell**
- He said real power often means setting the agenda so that nobody sees a fight, and that a meter or a schema does exactly that.
- He acknowledged Mahzarin's warning that a guild with a veto can become a closed shop that strains out novelty. That risk sits inside his own proposal.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Her unbundling point cuts against item 1. She said "stop counting papers" treats the credential as the problem, when tying any credential to learning shifts motivation outside the learner.

**Brent Hecht**

- He said Microsoft should pay into the fund but not govern it.

**Adrian Johns**
- He warned that historically the jurisdiction gap was filled by private policing that turned into public, transnational policing with no public oversight at all.

**Katherine Maher**
- She committed Wikimedia Enterprise to charging for reliable access and never for the integrity signal.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- He argued that timing decides the outcome. Protocols spread slowly and become almost impossible to dislodge (SMTP, 1982), so the first defaults decide the argument.
- He proposed default reference checks against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch, with metadata contributed back and retrieval and citation logs that two parties can verify. He called this hygiene rather than verification and said "telemetry doesn't pay anyone."

**Ivan Oransky**
- He cautioned that retraction checks catch only the roughly 0.2 percent of papers that have been retracted, when the real problem is probably ten times larger. This cuts against relying on the default checks O'Reilly proposed.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- He said the synthesis never names the politics. The deepest gamers, such as the Pravda network, want to be grounded in model outputs rather than paid, and states are capturing or defunding the institutions assumed to do the funding.
- He noted that the action list does not use the finding, which Kara Miller also raised, that fact-checking pieces raised hostility while plain reporting built trust.

**Peter Salib**
- He proposed that labs commission null results under contract, with checkers setting the terms, since no meter will pay for them. This is a different route from Evans's active collection.
- He argued for paying for future work instead of arguing over the past, which sides with fair terms over remedy.
- He said he still owes Henry Farrell a proper answer on leverage, so that exchange is unresolved.

**Steven Sloman**
- He said the action list is written for people who check, when almost nobody checks, experts included outside their own field. This cuts against the synthesis's premise that signals should send people to check.
- He cited research that people prefer explanations padded with empty jargon, that repetition raises belief even when it traces back to one source, and that confidence follows consensus more than accuracy.
- He added from his COVID work that vaccination depended more on leaders and close contacts than on individual understanding.
