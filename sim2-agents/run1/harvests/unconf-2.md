# Harvest — Unconference room 2: The role of knowledge in the age of LLMs

1. A confidence score the model reports about itself is a self-report and settles nothing. Ramón Alvarado, Mahzarin Banaji and Adam Becker made this point against David Weinberger's proposal to show "how confident the system actually is." Uncertainty should instead be computed from traceable evidence (Adam Bly, Mike Caulfield) and should count independent lines of evidence, not repetitions (Steven Sloman), with a claim-level audit trail required for consequential decisions such as diagnoses, hires and policy (Adam Bly).

2. A human in the loop is not a safeguard by itself. People copy a moderately biased model's preferences (Mahzarin Banaji), oversight atrophies as systems grow more reliable (Steven Sloman), and which humans do the judging matters (James Evans). The human's role has to be active verification outside the model, not approval (Mike Caulfield, Ramón Alvarado), and Caulfield and Evans held that refusing AI tools for that verification would be unilateral disarmament.

3. The room converged on independent, adversarial institutions over dyadic fixes like RLHF or one-on-one dialogue (Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton, Mahzarin Banaji). These would include audits on a shared, versioned battery, no model grading itself or its maker's outputs, and no generation more biased than the last (Banaji). Who audits stayed unresolved: Adam Becker wants publicly funded auditors with all funding disclosed and no firm money, Bratton argues human auditors will need public AI systems to work at scale, and Ramón Alvarado cautioned that audits measure error without explaining it.

4. Smoothness is the hazard. AI-mediated answers should surface disagreement, null results and dead ends, and let people run into what they didn't ask for (Tui Shaub, David Weinberger, James Evans, Kenneth Cukier). Evans called for a public commons of negative results, and Cukier called for frame pluralism over consensus.

5. Unresolved: can a track record justify trust in systems whose errors can't be traced to a source? Ramón Alvarado says not in science or hiring, and Kenneth Cukier says a constantly revalidated one can. The ontological split also stood, with Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton holding that prediction is intelligence against Adam Becker and David Weinberger holding that the models don't know as we do. Alvarado offered his "epistemic technology" framing as a way to set that question aside.

## Minority positions

**David Weinberger**
- Machine learning shows the limits of generalizations and the power of particulars. He finds that humbling and "joyous," and asked that AI's effect on knowledge not be treated as only corrosive.
- LLMs answer two of Socrates' three complaints about writing (fixed text, can't answer questions). He sees them as a new synthesis of oral and literate culture.
- He kept dialogue as a way of training people even after conceding it can't replace audits. The harvest's "convergence on institutions over dialogue" omits this.
- The harvest groups him with Becker, but in round 2 he granted that model statements "can count as knowledge in some sense." His emphasis is that they don't *think* like us, and he finds that the interesting part.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Next-word prediction is AI-complete. Humans hallucinate too, so the useful question is how well hallucination gets corrected, and by what.
- If "intelligence" is dropped for precision, "knows nothing" has to go too. Ramón Alvarado accepted this.
- Machine learning can launder human prejudice so that it looks objective.

**Tui Shaub**
- Both dialogue and collective correction assume the question is already formed. She wants training for hybrid people who invent concepts for realities not yet stabilized.
- She cautioned against both relativism and governing mainly by prohibition: audits and public failure records, not blanket bans.
- Artists and designers are missing from the room.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- In round 2 he moved beyond setting the ontology aside. He called the models "epistemic agents in a thin, operational sense," which the harvest doesn't record.
- Humans and instruments each have to earn their epistemic role. Being human doesn't confer a warrant.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- She answered Alvarado's "audits don't explain": decisions are behavior and behavior is what hurts people, and bias never had to be located in a neuron before it could be measured in a hiring decision. The harvest records the caution but not her reply.
- She disagreed with Bratton that going beyond the mirror means revelation. Models exaggerate training-data biases and create new ones, such as preferring AI-written text.
- Newer models show more covert bias, not less, and human choices shaped by biased models feed the next model's training data.

**Adam Becker**
- There is no evidence these tools speed up science. This cuts against Cukier's retina example and Evans's individual-productivity figures.
- "AI" was a marketing term. The manifesto should name who benefits from the erosion of a shared sense of truth and reject claims of inevitability.
- "Prediction is intelligence" is an assertion, not a finding. The data gap between children and models is a difference he won't reduce to efficiency.

**Adam Bly**
- Watermarks won't help much. Whether something is AI-generated is a separate question from whether it's credible.
- The integrity of the scholarly record, including retractions and metadata, is critical infrastructure. Grounding AI in a corrupted literature won't save science from its own crisis.
- As the head of a company, he said he would welcome independent audits of what his company builds.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- He opposes "Reflectionism." Alignment should run both ways, with culture also aligning to what AI discloses, and "epistemic overhangs" are the point, not pathologies.
- The bigger problem is that models include too little human language, not too much. Small curated "correct" models would freeze language. He supports tagging human-produced language against model collapse.
- Auditor, reviewer and dissenter roles should be judged by function, not by whether a person, an agent or a mix fills them. This goes further than the harvest's "human auditors need public AI."
- Human-centered design at its most refined is the slot machine.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Hallucinations aren't random. Models differ sharply, some rarely inventing links, and using "it's all hallucination" to end the discussion is its own mistake.
- He wants to drop "dialogue" and "answer box" as corporate framings. Users should treat the tool as directing an investigation, and the system should be a conduit to sourced, contested positions, not an arbiter.
- Brandolini's law is the core problem. He wants funded AI verification tools for fact-checkers, Wikipedia editors, Community Notes writers and librarians, with independent testing of whether they work.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- AI delivers the median while excellence is a tail event, so curators and trusted brands matter more than ever.
- Transparency should be graded by stakes. Spam filters can stay trade secrets, while loan, hiring and pricing systems should be open to public auditors but not rivals.
- Framing is a trainable muscle that atrophies without use. He cited ChatGPT users unable to quote essays they had just "written."
- He wants open infrastructure outsiders can build on without permission. The only frame not to tolerate is one that denies all others.

**James Evans**
- AI use raises individual scientists' output and citations while shrinking the range of topics and cutting engagement by 22 percent. Models rarely propose null hypotheses, so errors are correlated and individual checking won't catch them.
- He distinguished visible disagreement from "balanced" answers, which left people doing worse while feeling better.
- He wants concentration measured in fields and on review panels, and evaluators drawn from deliberately different distributions.

**Steven Sloman**
- Outsourcing thought is as much a feature of cognition as a bug, so reliance on machines isn't the danger in itself. The danger is the knowledge illusion: confusing access to knowledge with having it.
- Models are strong models of intuition but lack deliberation and shared intentionality, so they aren't accountable members of the community of knowledge.
- Credibility cues should show whether a source has a record of being corrected and accepting it, on the NTSB model. The harvest captures his independent-evidence point but not this.
- He said individual critical-thinking skills won't carry this. That puts him against Weinberger, Caulfield and Cukier on how much weight individual skill should bear.
