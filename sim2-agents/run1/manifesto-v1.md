# Sustaining Human Knowledge: A Manifesto from the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit

*Draft from the harvests of September 23–24, 2026.*

The machines didn't break knowledge. They landed on a system that was already strained. That system counts papers instead of judgment, and it runs on checkers who are mostly unpaid volunteers. It never worked for much of the Global South.

Some of what we face was already broken: incentives that reward publishing more and correcting less. Some of it was breaking: the funding for correction, the first jobs where competence is built, and the flow of value back to the people who produce knowledge.

What AI adds is speed and scale. Fluent text now costs nothing. The people who catch mistakes are getting scarcer, and the pipeline that trains them is thinning just as the tools arrive. Dysfunctions we once tolerated can no longer wait.

This is not an anti-AI document. These systems work because their training data is us: our frames, our causal models, our corrections. Keep paying for that and it compounds. Stop paying and we are left with a house of mirrors. None of this is inevitable. It is a set of design choices, and we can make them.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers first, and enforce it.**
Every layer designed at this summit sits downstream of publication as the proxy for productivity. As long as hiring, promotion and grants count papers and citations, and rankings keep counting on our behalf, detectors, registries and provenance layers are bailing water from the stern. Publishers earn more by publishing more, and paper mills game any count. A declaration will not fix this; DORA is the warning. Funders must write it into grant conditions, reward data sharing and correcting the record, and audit what institutions do rather than what they sign. Universities must rebuild assessment around demonstrated judgment: oral defences, reconstructing work without the tool, and a few judged pieces instead of lists. Misconduct reports should be public by default, and retraction notices should say whether the institution investigated.

**2. Credibility is maintained infrastructure, so fund the checking as a commons.**
Fluency no longer signals care, but credibility never rested on polish. It came from policing, and that policing had to be paid for. After Gutenberg the journalist didn't just appear. The scarcest part of that policing is the terminal checkers: patrollers, sleuths, and the people who draw out null results. They are unpaid and fewer every year, and no meter will ever pay them. We call for diamond infrastructure: a pooled fund or endowment that pays checkers as a body, not per item. Count the unpaid checking first so the fund has a number to argue from; Reddit moderation alone runs to at least 466 hours a day. The fund's charter should say the following:
- Checkers hold binding power over the categories, in public, open to challenge and undoable.
- Those who pay in do not govern, and that is a legal bylaw, not a pledge.
- Integrity signals are never metered.
- The charter carries purpose limits and a fixed-term review not staffed by whoever holds the money.
- The checkers themselves face blind audits and conflict rules.
- Mirrors sit outside the US.
- Regional allocations are transparent and build regional infrastructure rather than compliance with Northern schemas.

**3. Nobody grades their own homework.**
Whoever profits from, pays for, or is subject to an audit does not control it. No model family grades its own work or its maker's outputs. Nobody who takes a margin on a count sets how it is counted, publishers included. Audits should run on a shared, versioned battery that measures behavior, not self-report. Error rates should be published for every release and failures kept on public record. Checkpoints should be preserved for auditors, and no new model generation should be more biased than the last. Transparency should be graded by stakes. It should reach the money: who pays, who takes a margin, and what each system is built to maximize.

**4. Signals should send people to check, not stand in for checking.**
Whatever replaces fluency as a trust signal will itself be trusted on sight. That includes our own provenance layers and correction records. So confidence must be computed from traceable, independent lines of evidence, never from a model's report about itself and never from repetitions of one source. Registering an agent identifies who acted, not whether a claim is true. Whether text is machine-made is a separate question from whether it is credible. Consequential decisions such as diagnoses, hires and policy need a claim-level trail back to data, methods and retraction status. Test every signal on someone who checks nothing, to see whether it moves them toward truth or only toward trust. Nothing is certified once. Everything is rerun, in public.

**5. Design against smoothness.**
The biggest danger is not noise but the median: a frame monoculture. AI-using scientists publish and are cited more, while their topic range shrinks and engagement falls 22 percent. The decade-long rise in null results reversed after ChatGPT. Models exaggerate human biases rather than mirror them. AI-mediated answers should do three things:
- Show disagreement, null results and dead ends.
- Route people to named, linked, conflicting sources rather than answer from nowhere.
- Draw on structured disagreement across model families.

Negative results need a public commons. Every such design must be tested against the verdict alone, because nobody has yet shown that seeing the argument makes people wiser.

**6. A human in the loop is not a safeguard by itself.**
People copy a biased model's preferences. Oversight atrophies as systems grow more reliable. Overreliance can produce worse results than either the human or the AI alone, and some mitigations, explanations among them, make it worse. The human's job is active verification outside the model, not approval. Which humans do the checking matters. Older reviewers, who get the most say, are most likely to resist disruptive work, so evaluators should be drawn deliberately from different pools. The flood is too large to refuse AI tools for verification. Every human-in-the-loop, provenance layer or disagreement display must beat both the human alone and the AI alone, tail cases included, with results published either way.

**7. Build competence before reliance, and change the setting rather than warn people.**
Capacities that were depressed can come back; capacities that were never built may not. Judge a tool by what competence remains when it is taken away, and favour transparent tools. First jobs are where competence is built, and that pipeline is cracking. Mentorship is conversation, and it is the first thing cut. Every patroller, sleuth and reviewer starts as a young worker someone took time to teach. Knowing about a problem almost never changes what people do, so the responsibility falls on institutions and tool builders through defaults, grant conditions and hiring, not on labels read as fine print. Every recommendation names who acts, who pays, and who studies whether it worked. We act early and study as we go. Waiting twenty years for proof is being late to the party again.

**8. Pay for what the models depend on, and let producers bargain together.**
Language models are an obligate complement to human knowledge. Models trained on edited, vetted human work do better than models trained on other models. Yet value has stopped flowing back: roughly 38,000 pages are crawled per referral. This is quasi-enclosure, which means precarious, not lost. A contract market will not pay producers without collective leverage and verification. Scholars, societies and small presses need an antitrust safe harbour to bargain together, and every licence needs event-level reporting and audit rights. Payment tied to volume or retrieval will be gamed by paper mills and will harden the canon. Pay coarsely instead, for measured dependence, with clawbacks for retracted work. Big lump-sum licences deepen the largest labs' moat. Meters must not become gates only the richest labs can cross.

**9. Keep the record open and forkable. Identify the crawlers, never the readers.**
The scholarly record, its metadata and its integrity data should stay open. Infrastructure that AI depends on should pass the insurance test: open data, open source, patent non-assertion, and a living will. Any affected community should be able to see it, undo it, and walk away. Non-commercial licences are unforkable in practice. But open has never meant uncredited. Attribution is how scholarship identifies the community whose judgments decide which claims hold up. AI systems should attribute sources, check references against open retraction and metadata records by default, and contribute metadata back. Crawlers and agents acting on the commons should identify themselves and who runs them. Readers should not have to. Closure has costs too: after Twitter closed its API, over 60 percent of the research projects that used it stalled or were abandoned. Make taking without asking costly, and keep openness cheap.

**10. Infrastructure is governance.**
Whoever defines a "finding" or a "work" shapes the market, and audits cannot undo that. Reporting schemas decide what counts as a contribution. They can leave care work, translation and non-DOI knowledge invisible. Metering and identity tools drift to uses nobody agreed to. Excluded communities, including the Global South, must be seated and paid before categories are fixed. Refusal must be a legitimate, fundable outcome. Funders and policymakers should stop using rankings. Seats without power just give us a more popular echo.

## What we commit to do

- **Funders among us** will remove paper counts and citation metrics from grants, hiring and promotion. They will attach audits, public failure records, the take-the-tool-away test, and data statements on provenance and consent to grant conditions. They will audit compliance.
- **Together** we will charter the checking commons on the terms above. We will begin by measuring volunteer-dependent checking labour, designing that measurement with the volunteer communities.
- **Companies among us** pay in and do not govern. In the room, Brent Hecht said this of Microsoft and Blaise Agüera y Arcas of Google.
- **Stewards among us** made specific commitments in the room:
  - Katherine Maher committed Wikimedia Enterprise to charging for reliable access and never for the integrity signal.
  - Adam Bly committed to take CC0 on data plus patent non-assertion to his board and report back publicly.
  - Esther Dyson pledged her own money.
- **Builders among us** will publish per-release error rates, submit to independent audit, and test every mitigation against both the human alone and the machine alone.
- **Institutions among us** will fund paid first jobs, apprenticeships and tool-withdrawal studies.

We have not answered who is the payer of last resort if the labs balk while federal science funding is cut. We ask who among us will be.

## Noted dissents

- **What these systems are.** Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton hold that prediction is intelligence. Adam Becker calls that an assertion, not a finding, and calls "intelligence" marketing. David Weinberger holds that the models don't think like us. Ramón Alvarado's "epistemic technology" framing lets this manifesto act without settling it.
- **Remedy for past training.** Becker calls training on the record "fruit of the poisoned tree" and says a forward royalty turns unconsented taking into precedent. Brent Hecht asks that commercial training without consent not be fair use. Anil Dash, Tim O'Reilly and Peter Salib argue for fair terms going forward. Bratton rejects the theft framing outright.
- **Consent and openness.** Leslie Chan: "openness without consent is just extraction made frictionless." Amy Brand wants opt-in, attributed licences. Jason Priem holds that gating training recreates enclosure and that information prices falling toward zero is the design goal. Selena Deckelmann states that Wikipedia's content stays free, including for training.
- **Sovereignty.** Bratton holds that open weights are the only form a Southern lab can fine-tune, and that walled regional archives become "Galapagos archives." Chan and Alvarado hold that imported openness is not sovereignty.
- **Payment design.** O'Reilly defends fine-grained per-answer royalties, with gaming fixed inside the protocol. Nick Vincent says per-answer and coarse payment can coexist.
- **Leverage.** Salib argues that labs competing for scarce expert data may raise pay without producer coordination. Henry Farrell disagrees, and Salib says he still owes Farrell an answer. Mike Caulfield expects licensing to arrive through the market because research products depend on the library they can show.
- **The checkers' guild.** James Evans warns that a funded guild of last generation's leaders would "rebuild the lamp post." Mahzarin Banaji warns that a guild with a veto can become a closed shop, which Farrell acknowledged. David Krakauer asks whether payment itself corrupts judgment. Tamar Gendler asks whether paying crowds out obligation.
- **Who checks.** Steven Sloman says the action list is written for people who check, when almost nobody checks, experts included outside their field.
- **The credential.** Gendler says "stop counting papers" treats the credential as the problem, when tying any credential to learning moves motivation outside the learner.
- **Retraction checks.** Ivan Oransky warns that they catch about 0.2 percent of papers against a real rate he puts near 2 percent, and that correction speed is itself subject to Goodhart's law.
- **Politics.** Peter Pomerantsev says the manifesto never names the politics. The deepest gamers, such as the Pravda network, want grounding, not money. Belief tracks belonging. Fact-checking pieces raised hostility while plain reporting built trust.
- **Future agents.** Salib warns that the plan assumes today's capabilities. He proposes registered, capitalized agents with seizable assets, supported by Bratton and provisionally by Agüera y Arcas. Sherry Turkle, Becker, Kenneth Cukier, Dash, Farrell, Sloman and Katherine Maher rejected this.
- **AI-free places.** Turkle asks that some places have no AI at all: grief, children's companionship, the seminar, the family table. Tui Shaub opposes prohibition as the default. Esther Dyson would have AI remove "stupid friction" and make time for "good friction."
- **Track records.** Alvarado holds that a track record cannot justify trust in untraceable errors because scientific feedback is slow. Cukier holds that a constantly revalidated one can.
- **Gatekeepers and roles.** Bratton holds that some of the alarm is old gatekeepers defending their authority to narrate, and that roles should be judged by function, not by whether a human fills them.
- **The scientific record.** Becker says there is no evidence these tools speed up science, and that the manifesto should name the companies responsible. Cukier and Weinberger ask that the diagnosis also record discovery.
- **Voluntary funding.** Dyson holds that lab leaders can fund teachers and trainers now. Farrell holds that the payer doesn't volunteer.
