# Sustaining Human Knowledge: A Manifesto from the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit

*Draft from the harvests of September 23–24, 2026, revised after review round 2.*

The machines didn't break knowledge. They landed on a system that was already strained. It counts papers instead of judgment, runs on unpaid checkers, and never worked for much of the Global South. Funding for correction, the first jobs where competence is built, and the flow of value back to producers were already breaking. AI adds speed and scale. Fluent text costs nothing, while the checkers and the pipeline that trains them thin out.

This is not an anti-AI document. Retinal scans now reveal heart disease, one of scores of such findings. These tools must be revalidated in public against ground truth, tail cases included. They are built from our frames and corrections, and they also find patterns nobody put in, including new bias that must be governed as new. Keep paying for the human record and both parts compound. Stop, and we are left with a house of mirrors.

Not all of this is accidental. States and networks such as Pravda seed the record to reach models, not to get paid. Governments are capturing the institutions that safeguard knowledge while the US retreats from the information space it built. People believe as they belong: plain reporting built trust where fact-checking raised hostility. So the target is citizens' agency. Citizens need the right to see how an answer was shaped and by whom, and a real part in checking. Every layer here must hold up against systems more capable than today's.

In one line: open record, swappable models, organized checkers paid as a body, crawlers that say who they are.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers first, and enforce it.**
Every layer here sits downstream of publication as the proxy for productivity. While hiring, grants and rankings count papers, detectors are bailing water, and declarations such as DORA fail. Funders must write the change into grant conditions, reward data sharing and correction, and audit what institutions do, not what they sign. Rankings are the loophole. Funders, governments and accreditors must stop using paper- and citation-based rankings to allocate money, visas or recognition. Universities that sign on stop feeding rankers their data and report any ranking they still take part in. Universities assess demonstrated judgment: oral defences, reconstructing work without the tool, and a few judged pieces instead of lists. Credentials motivate, but they move some motivation outside the learner, who may then game the credential. Assessment should make acquiring the capacity the easiest way to earn it. Tamar Gendler will draft for the roadmap which parts of scholarly authority must stay coupled.

Publishers profit from volume, and paper mills game any count. So publishers owe duties, and funders should publish around those who refuse. Publishers must:
- act on credible flags within two months;
- say in retraction notices what happened and why, or that it is unclear, and whether the institution investigated;
- send retraction metadata to Crossref promptly;
- press-release retractions as they press-release papers, outside paywalls;
- pay into the checking commons that funds sleuths.

Misconduct reports are public by default. ORI, or its successor, needs the budget and subpoena power the NSF inspector general already has. Correction timing is published as a record open to challenge, not as a badge.

**2. Credibility is maintained infrastructure, so fund checking as a commons.**
Credibility came from policing, and policing has to be paid for. The scarcest part is the terminal checkers: patrollers, sleuths, and those who draw out null results. They are unpaid, fewer every year and beyond any meter. We call for diamond infrastructure: a pooled endowment paying checkers as a body, not per item. Its charter says:
- Checkers, organized with seats reserved for Southern communities, ratify every schema defining "finding," "correction" or "novel." They can veto any revision, and each revision needs a public, challengeable, undoable justification.
- Seats rotate. Wardens serve fixed terms and come from outside the guild's leadership. Dissenting minorities can send a category to external review.
- Checkers are drawn across career stage, field, region and institution, not mainly from last generation's leaders. They face blind audits and conflict rules.
- Evaluators, human and model, are tested retrospectively on work later shown to be disruptive or overlooked, with results published.
- Under a legal bylaw, payers do not govern. Integrity signals are never metered.
- The charter sets purpose limits and a fixed-term review not staffed by whoever holds the money.
- Mirrors sit outside the US, and a named court or tribunal enforces the charter across borders.
- Regional allocations are transparent and build regional infrastructure, not compliance with Northern schemas.

This leverage binds only buyers who need checkers' judgment. Paper mills, state floods and crawler swarms need separate remedies.

**3. Nobody grades their own homework.**
Whoever profits from, pays for or is subject to an audit does not control it. Nobody taking a margin on a count sets how it is counted. No model family grades its own outputs or its maker's. Audits use a shared, versioned battery that measures behavior, not self-report. Each release publishes error rates and keeps failures on record. It also registers known error classes with their nature, sources and triggers, ratified by checkers rather than labs. Systems that cite sources publish citation error rates from independent samplers: dead or fabricated links, sources that don't support the claim, and sources that miss the current state of knowledge. Auditors get checkpoints from each training stage, retained older models and data-source disclosure.

A behavioral audit shows what a system does, not where its bias or error comes from. Error rates justify reliance within the conditions tested. Where ground truth arrives slowly or never, as in much of science, they justify no more than that. No generation should be more biased than the last, and auditors are audited blind. Transparency reaches the money: who pays, who takes a margin, and what each system maximizes.

Every public chatbot, agent or AI knowledge service names an accountable organization with something to lose. That organization carries mandatory liability insurance, even for free products, and discloses its premiums. Insurance prices frequent, visible harm, so it is a floor. Catastrophic tail risks need their own duties. Whoever instructs wrongful conduct stays personally liable. Accountability runs to people, never to the system.

**4. Signals should send people to check, not stand in for checking.**
Whatever replaces fluency will itself be trusted on sight, and that includes our own provenance layers. So no signal, badge or registry entry in this manifesto may be presented to readers as warrant. Confidence comes from traceable, independent lines of evidence, never from a model's self-report or from repetitions of one source. Registering an agent shows who acted, not whether a claim is true. Consequential decisions need a claim-level trail to data, methods and retraction status.

Prestige, credentials, fluency and institutional name are cues that bias human and model evaluators alike. Reviewers, accuracy samplers and triage systems should do the first reading blind to them. Authorship and provenance stay in the record. Where blinding would hide what is needed to judge potential or earlier opportunity, add explicit counterweights.

Test every signal on someone who checks nothing. Most people take cues from leaders, colleagues, family and neighbours. Funders should pay for cues built for clinicians, teachers, clergy, local reporters and community leaders. They should also pay for studies of whether those cues change what those communities believe and do, measured in the communities, not on individual readers in a lab. A cue that works on a reader in a lab but not through trusted people has failed. Nothing is certified once; everything is rerun in public.

**5. Design against smoothness.**
The biggest danger is the median: a frame monoculture. AI-using scientists publish and are cited more, while their topic range shrinks and engagement falls 22 percent. The rise in null results reversed after ChatGPT. Models exaggerate human biases. AI-mediated answers should show disagreement, null results and dead ends. They should route people to named, linked, conflicting sources and draw on structured disagreement across model families. Negative results need a public commons. Test every such design against the verdict alone, because nobody has shown that seeing the argument makes people wiser.

**6. A human in the loop is not a safeguard by itself.**
People copy a biased model's preferences, and oversight atrophies as systems grow reliable. Overreliance can do worse than either the human or the AI alone, and explanations can make it worse. The human's job is active verification outside the model, not approval.

First, though, ask whether the machine belongs in the loop at all. Institutions, funders and builders must make that choice in the open and keep to it. The choice starts with therapy, children's companionship, grief, and the seminar where students learn to read and argue. In these places no conversational AI should stand in for a person. Anyone who deploys one there must justify it publicly and name who is accountable. We ask whether before we ask how, and nothing here is inevitable.

The flood may be too large to refuse AI verification tools. But answering AI's problems with more AI can start arms races: detectors breed evasion, and automated reviewers breed text written to pass them. Funders should pay for independent trials of whether these tools cut the cost of refutation for fact-checkers, Wikipedians and librarians. Each trial should also ask how the tool will be gamed. Tools may narrow and focus human judgment but never replace it. The final check, including sampling for audits, stays with people. Every design must beat the human alone and the AI alone, tail cases included, with results published either way.

**7. Build competence before reliance, and change the setting rather than warn people.**
Depressed capacities can return; capacities never built may not. The order of learning decides the outcome. Given the same tool, the cohort that learned first stays competent, and the one that learned with the tool becomes dependent. Universities and funders should require students and early-career researchers to build reading, reviewing, analysis and programming skills before they are given AI pipelines. During the learning period, allow only tools that learners could do without. Opaque tools come only after competence is secure.

Judge a tool by what competence remains when it is taken away. Favour tools transparent enough to be rebuilt. Apply the removal test to checkers and credentials, and measure the collective floor: what a field can still do, verify and teach without the tools. Funders should start longitudinal cohort studies now that raise and then lower tool availability and include non-checkers. Collapse is expected to be hysteretic, and the cohort that learned without the tools is retiring. Mentorship is conversation, and it is the first thing cut. Warnings rarely change behavior, so responsibility falls on institutions through defaults, grant conditions and hiring. Every recommendation names who acts, who pays and who studies whether it worked.

**8. Pay for what the models depend on, and let producers bargain together.**
Language models are an obligate complement to human knowledge, yet roughly 38,000 pages are crawled per referral. This is quasi-enclosure: precarious, not lost. Without collective leverage, a contract market won't pay producers. Scholars, societies and small presses need an antitrust safe harbour, and Southern university presses co-write any shared template.

Developers disclose where their training data came from, including pirated copies. Licences record how authors, and not only rights holders, consented. Any opt-out must be easy to find, exercise and confirm. Licences are short-term and renegotiated at fixed points. They set training and grounding terms separately and require event-level reporting in an open schema with purpose limits.

The party that owes money reports its own counts. Publisher and CDN logs corroborate retrieval, but not grounding, citation or display. A signed event proves who sent it, not that it is true or that every qualifying event was reported. So licences need independent sampled audits, seeded canary content, raw logs for auditors and public aggregates. Each party's log stays a separate claim, with contradictions left visible. The schema carries signals, not prices, and never renders "grounded" as a badge. Telemetry pays no one.

Payment tied to raw volume or retrieval will be gamed and will harden the canon. Coarse pooled payment for measured dependence can coexist with fine-grained attribution that counts independent findings. Both carry clawbacks for retracted work, and part of every payment goes to the checking commons. Meters must not become gates only the richest labs can cross. Defaults harden, so the payer firewall, purpose limits and cross-verification ship in version one of any licence, meter or protocol, wherever a second witness exists.

**9. Keep the record open and forkable. Identify crawlers and campaigns, never readers.**
The scholarly record, metadata and integrity data stay open. Infrastructure AI depends on should pass the insurance test: open data, open source, patent non-assertion and a living will. Communities must be able to see it, undo it and walk away. Non-commercial licences are unforkable in practice.

Open does not mean everything for everyone on the same licence. Some knowledge comes from Indigenous or other communities who were never asked. There, FAIR is paired with CARE: the community signs off before anyone trains on it or deploys it, keeps audit access and shares in the benefits. Tools such as Local Contexts labels are funded. So is refusal to collect, train or deploy when governance is inadequate.

Open never meant uncredited. AI systems attribute with a visible link to the source, and for community-edited works to the revision history and edit button. Whether this brings contributors back should be measured, and it never replaces direct funding. Systems check references against open retraction records by default and contribute metadata back.

Crawlers identify themselves and who runs them, and give a working contact. They use APIs or bulk feeds where offered and respect capacity limits that keep human readers first. The content stays free, but the servers do not, so commercial reusers help fund the infrastructure they load.

Citizens have a right to know three things:
- whether they are dealing with a person or an automated system;
- whether apparent popularity is coordinated or artificially amplified;
- who is behind a source that presents itself as independent, including networks that seed the record for models.

Disclosure attaches to automation, coordination and concealed sponsorship, never to content or readers. Anonymity stays a right.

Every registry of crawlers, agents or provenance is a choke point, and choke points drift. Anti-piracy tracking ended up tracking dissidents. So each registry:
- states its purpose limits before it is built;
- is barred by bylaw from identifying readers or sharing records with states for other purposes;
- answers to an overseer it does not fund and whose members it does not appoint;
- publishes who queried it and why;
- shuts down if it serves no purpose after a fixed term.

**10. Infrastructure is governance.**
Whoever defines a "finding" shapes the market, and audits cannot undo that. Schemas can leave care work, translation and non-DOI knowledge invisible. Excluded communities must be seated with binding say, and paid, before categories are fixed. This includes the Global South and the regional and open-weight model builders working there. Plurality of model families means little if the same few firms build them all; Blaise Agüera y Arcas said this includes Google. Editors, maintainers, junior checkers, disabled users and ordinary users get binding say over how checking is measured. Refusal must be fundable. Seats without power just give us a more popular echo.

## What we commit to do

- **Funders among us** will remove paper counts and citation metrics from grants, hiring and promotion. They will attach audits, failure records, the take-the-tool-away test and provenance-and-consent statements to grant conditions, and audit compliance.
- **Together** we will charter the checking commons.
  - We begin by measuring volunteer checking labour (Reddit moderation alone: at least 466 hours a day). The volunteer communities co-design and ratify that measure.
  - Before any payment formula is set, we will test with them whether pay strengthens or crowds out obligation, and publish the results either way.
- **The organizers** will co-convene the next summit with Global South schema-setters, Indigenous knowledge communities, Wikipedia editors, open source maintainers, junior checkers, disabled people led by disabled advocates, and ordinary users of today's chatbots. Travel, translation, access needs and time will be paid. Each group gets binding say over the categories, including funded refusal. We will report whether authority shifted, not how many seats were filled.
- **Companies among us** pay in and do not govern. Brent Hecht said this of Microsoft and Blaise Agüera y Arcas of Google.
- **Stewards among us:**
  - Katherine Maher helped build Wikimedia Enterprise but no longer speaks for Wikimedia. She set the test it and similar services should meet: charge for reliable, accountable access, never for the integrity signal. As CEO of NPR, she reaffirmed NPR's existing commitment, shared with its union, that nothing NPR puts before its audience is written or produced by AI. NPR has no AI licensing deal today. She committed that any licensing NPR pursues, ideally with its member stations, will meet the same test.
  - Adam Bly committed to take CC0 on data plus patent non-assertion to System's board and report the answer publicly within six months. System's schema for a finding and its confidence scoring will be held outside System and take effect only with the checkers' ratifying body's approval. Independent auditors will test the graph against counting only independent studies, with results published. The evidence trace, not the score, is the product.
  - Esther Dyson pledged her own money.
  - Jason Priem committed OpenAlex to removing community curation from paid tiers on an announced date. OpenAlex will publish its curation rules and correction logs, mirror outside the US, and offer its definitions of "work," "affiliation" and "finding" to the ratifying body. Its data stays CC0, and retraction status stays unmetered.
  - James Evans, a visiting researcher at Google, committed Knowledge Lab to an open retrospective benchmark. It will test evaluators, human and model, senior and junior, on work later shown to be disruptive, overlooked or a "sleeping beauty," with results published even if the benchmark fails. He will also help seed the negative-results commons with elicited null intuitions, failed replications and laboratory dead ends.
- **Builders among us** will publish per-release error rates, submit to independent audit, and test mitigations against the human alone and the machine alone.
- **Institutions among us** will fund paid first jobs, apprenticeships, tool-withdrawal studies, and protected, evaluated mentoring time for senior staff. Each will report how many early-career people it trained and kept, with outside study of whether they became capable checkers.

We have only partly answered who pays if the labs balk while federal science funding is cut. The payer doesn't volunteer, and goodwill is a bridge, not the plan. The organizers will approach public funders and foundations outside the US to seed an endowment. Within six months we will publish whom we approached, every sum offered and its original source, and what gap remains. Checkers will organize first, so that labs pay because checkers have leverage. Scarce expert evaluation data is the strongest leverage they have.

Nick Vincent and Adam Becker would back this with law: statutory contributions from commercial AI. Vincent frames these as a presumptive commons-rent tax on AI operating profits. Firms can lower it by showing audited provenance, by licensing through organized producers, or by paying into programs such as the checking commons. The money is paid out coarsely to communities, including patrollers and people who draw out null results, never per item or by per-answer valuation.

## Noted dissents

- **Benjamin Bratton** (round-2 edit, not adopted because the harvests give checkers a veto over categories): checkers "can suspend a revision for a fixed period and send it to external review, but no body holds a standing veto." Final decisions would rest with a panel drawn across career stage, region and model family. Any definition of "novel" or "finding" would lapse if a yearly test showed it would have filtered out work later shown to be disruptive or overlooked.
  - Earlier, with Blaise Agüera y Arcas: prediction is intelligence. Adam Becker: that is an assertion, not a finding, and "intelligence" is marketing.
  - Open weights are the only form a Southern lab can fine-tune, and walled archives become "Galapagos archives." Leslie Chan and Ramón Alvarado: imported openness is not sovereignty.
  - Some of the alarm is old gatekeepers defending their authority, and roles should be judged by function.
- **Peter Salib** (round-2 edit, not adopted because most of the room rejected agent liability): "The law may also hold a registered, capitalized agent liable in its own name, in addition to its owners and never in place of them," with the corporate form pierced for thin capitalization, commingled assets or fraud.
  - Backed by Bratton and, provisionally, Agüera y Arcas. Rejected by Sherry Turkle, Becker, Kenneth Cukier, Anil Dash, Henry Farrell, Steven Sloman and Katherine Maher.
  - Salib also expects labs competing for expert data to raise pay without coordination. Farrell disagrees, and Salib owes him an answer. Mike Caulfield expects licensing through the market.
- **Adam Becker** (round-2 edit, adopted in part; this part conflicts with Esther Dyson's pledge and with companies paying in): "No part of the commons may be seeded by or depend on billionaire philanthropy, which is an exercise of power that nobody elected, not a public good." Nor should it be seeded by foundations funded by AI firms or their owners.
  - Earlier: training on the record is "fruit of the poisoned tree," and a forward royalty makes taking a precedent.
  - There is no evidence these tools speed up science, and the manifesto should name the companies.
  - Bratton rejects the theft framing.
- **Amy Brand** (round-2 edit, adopted in part; the room is split on past liability and on consent to train): licensing for training should be opt-in and attributed by default, and "a licence to read is not a licence to train." "Forward licences do not release them from liability for past use, so paying from here on is not an amnesty for LibGen."
  - Chan: "openness without consent is just extraction made frictionless."
  - Jason Priem: gating training recreates enclosure.
  - Selena Deckelmann: Wikipedia stays free, including for training.
- **Brent Hecht:** commercial training without the content owner's consent should not be fair use, because simple policy changes can reorder the field overnight. He sees Tim O'Reilly's framing, that learning from the record is fine but outputs that substitute for it are not, as a complementary route to the same bargain. Dash, O'Reilly and Salib: fair terms going forward.
- **Ramón Alvarado** (round-2 edit, adopted in part; this part conflicts with Cukier): track records justify bounded reliance, not trust, which "requires access to the nature and source of failure, not just its rate." Cukier: a constantly revalidated record can justify trust.
- **David Weinberger:** the models don't think like us or know as we do. Yet their predictive power, drawn from constellations of particulars, is an epistemic gift. Dialogue as literacy remains a hypothesis to be tested against the verdict alone.
- **James Evans:** a guild of last generation's leaders would "rebuild the lamp post." Mahzarin Banaji: a veto guild can become a closed shop, which Farrell acknowledged. David Krakauer asks whether payment corrupts judgment; Tamar Gendler asks whether it crowds out obligation.
- **Steven Sloman:** the actions are written for people who check, when almost nobody does.
- **Ivan Oransky:** retraction checks catch about 0.2 percent of papers against a real rate near 2 percent, and correction speed is subject to Goodhart's law.
- **Sherry Turkle:** no system should speak as an "I."
- **Tui Shaub:** opposes prohibition as the default way of governing knowledge tools, because regulation built mainly on bans treats a changing system as static. She does not oppose bounded restrictions decided in the open. Her preferred levers are funding conditions, independent audits and public failure records, backed by law where needed.
- **Esther Dyson:** would remove "stupid friction" to make time for "good friction." Lab leaders can and should fund teachers, trainers and front-line checkers now. Farrell: the payer doesn't volunteer. Dyson accepts this.
  - Voluntary money, including her own, is a bridge and not a mechanism.
  - The leverage comes from organized checkers.
  - The floor that depends on nobody's goodwill is mandatory liability insurance with disclosed premiums, carried by the accountable company and its senior officials and board. It comes with disclosure of the money flows and of what each system is built to maximize.
