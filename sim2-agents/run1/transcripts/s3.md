I want to start by disagreeing with a sentence in our own concept note. Early print was not a trusted signal that later got debased. It was various, full of piracy and hard to trust. Left to themselves, printers and booksellers had no particular reason to let the book become a reliable tool for knowledge. The bogus could easily crowd out the genuine. Print's credibility had to be made, over decades, by communities that took hold of the trade and forced it to behave. The Royal Society kept a register that logged contributions by date. Now to this session's frame, commons versus enclosure. When Nehemiah Grew's Epsom salts were copied, the copiers said they stood for freedom of knowledge and called Grew a monopolist. Grew's side said the copies were counterfeit and poisonous. Both sides were partly right. Openness and property came out of the same fight, and each shaped the other. So I don't think the question is whether to enclose. The question is where the line goes, who draws it, and who keeps enforcing it. A system like that only lasts with constant policing, and that policing has to be paid for.

Third, the people who enforce these rules may well be right, and that's exactly why they are dangerous. Registries, provenance layers and meters all tend to drift beyond their original purpose. Whoever guards the guards has to be settled before we build anything.
