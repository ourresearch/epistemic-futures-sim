# Sustaining Human Knowledge: A Manifesto from the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit

*Draft from the harvests of September 23–24, 2026, revised after review round 1.*

The machines didn't break knowledge. They landed on a system that was already strained. It counts papers instead of judgment, runs on unpaid volunteer checkers, and never worked for much of the Global South. Incentives that reward publishing more and correcting less were already broken. Three things were breaking: funding for correction, the first jobs where competence is built, and the flow of value back to producers. AI adds speed and scale. Fluent text costs nothing, while the checkers and the pipeline that trains them thin out.

This is not an anti-AI document. Machine learning already sees what people cannot. Retinal scans now reveal heart disease, and there are scores of findings like it. In science, these tools must be constantly revalidated against ground truth, in public, on hard tail cases as well as typical ones. They are built from us: our frames, causal models and corrections. They also turn up patterns nobody put in, and some of that is new bias that must be governed as new. Keep feeding and paying for the human record, and both parts compound. Stop, and we are left with a house of mirrors.

Our responses are design choices. Not all of what we face is accidental. States and networks such as Pravda seed the record to get into what models draw on, not to get paid, and meters and clawbacks don't touch them. Governments are capturing the institutions that safeguard knowledge while the US retreats from the information space it built. People believe as they belong: plain reporting built trust where fact-checking raised hostility. So the target is citizens' agency as well as better signals. Citizens need the right to see how an answer was shaped and by whom, and a real part in checking. Capability growth cannot be voted away, so every layer here must hold up against systems more capable than today's.

In one line: open record, swappable models, organized checkers paid as a body, crawlers that say who they are. Anyone can check each of these from outside.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers first, and enforce it.**
Every layer designed here sits downstream of publication as the proxy for productivity. While hiring, grants and rankings count papers and citations, detectors and registries are bailing water. Publishers profit from volume, and paper mills game any count. DORA shows that declarations fail. Funders must write the change into grant conditions, reward data sharing and correction, and audit what institutions do rather than what they sign. Universities must assess demonstrated judgment: oral defences, reconstructing work without the tool, and a few judged pieces instead of lists. Swapping one count for another is not enough, because any credential tied to learning moves motivation outside the learner. Scholarly authority bundles research, teaching, socialization and certification. We must ask which of these must stay coupled; Tamar Gendler will draft that question for the roadmap. Misconduct reports should be public by default, and retraction notices should say whether the institution investigated. ORI, or its successor, needs the budget and subpoena power the NSF inspector general already has. Corrections should take at most two months, and their timing and frequency should be published as a record open to challenge, not as a badge.

**2. Credibility is maintained infrastructure, so fund checking as a commons.**
Credibility never rested on polish. It came from policing, and policing had to be paid for. The scarcest part is the terminal checkers: patrollers, sleuths, and those who draw out null results. They are unpaid, fewer every year, and beyond any meter. We call for diamond infrastructure: a pooled endowment paying checkers as a body, not per item. Its charter says:
- Checkers, organized with seats reserved for Southern communities, ratify every schema defining "finding," "correction" or "novel." They can veto any revision, and each revision needs a public, challengeable, undoable justification.
- Seats rotate. Wardens serve fixed terms and come from outside the guild's leadership. Dissenting minorities can send a category to external review.
- Checkers are drawn across career stage, field, region and institution, not mainly from last generation's leaders, who most often suppress disruptive work. Evaluators, human and model, are tested retrospectively on work later shown to be disruptive or overlooked, with results published.
- Checkers face blind audits and conflict rules.
- Payers do not govern, under a legal bylaw. Integrity signals are never metered.
- The charter sets purpose limits and a fixed-term review not staffed by whoever holds the money.
- Mirrors sit outside the US, and a named court or tribunal enforces the charter across borders.
- Regional allocations are transparent and build regional infrastructure, not compliance with Northern schemas.

This leverage binds only buyers who need checkers' judgment. Paper mills, state floods and crawler swarms need separate remedies.

**3. Nobody grades their own homework.**
Whoever profits from, pays for or is subject to an audit does not control it. No model family grades its own outputs or its maker's. Nobody taking a margin on a count sets how it is counted. Audits use a shared, versioned battery that measures behavior, not self-report. Each release publishes error rates and keeps failures on public record. It also registers known error classes, with their nature, sources and triggering conditions, ratified by checkers rather than labs. Systems that cite sources publish citation error rates from independent samplers: dead or fabricated links, sources that don't support the claim, and sources that miss the current state of knowledge. Auditors get checkpoints from each training stage, retained older models and data-source disclosure. A behavioral audit shows what a system does, not where its bias comes from. No generation should be more biased than the last, and auditors are audited blind. Transparency reaches the money: who pays, who takes a margin, and what each system maximizes. Every public chatbot, agent or AI knowledge service names an accountable organization with something to lose. That organization carries mandatory liability insurance, even for free products, and discloses its premiums. Insurance prices frequent, visible harm, so it is a floor. Accountability runs to people, never to the system.

**4. Signals should send people to check, not stand in for checking.**
Whatever replaces fluency will itself be trusted on sight, and that includes our own provenance layers. Confidence must come from traceable, independent lines of evidence, never from a model's self-report or from repetitions of one source. Registering an agent identifies who acted, not whether a claim is true. Consequential decisions need a claim-level trail to data, methods and retraction status. Test every signal on someone who checks nothing. Most people take cues from leaders, colleagues, family and neighbours, so follow the signal through them. Ask whether repeated appearances of one source read as consensus. Fund cues for the people others rely on. Nothing is certified once; everything is rerun in public.

**5. Design against smoothness.**
The biggest danger is the median: a frame monoculture. AI-using scientists publish and are cited more, while their topic range shrinks and engagement falls 22 percent. The rise in null results reversed after ChatGPT. Models exaggerate human biases. AI-mediated answers should show disagreement, null results and dead ends. They should route people to named, linked, conflicting sources and draw on structured disagreement across model families. Negative results need a public commons. Test every such design against the verdict alone, because nobody has shown that seeing the argument makes people wiser.

**6. A human in the loop is not a safeguard by itself.**
People copy a biased model's preferences, and oversight atrophies as systems grow reliable. Overreliance can do worse than either the human or the AI alone, and explanations can make it worse. The human's job is active verification outside the model, not approval. First, though, ask whether the machine belongs in the loop at all. Deciding where it does not go is a design choice that institutions, funders and builders must make in the open and keep to. The flood is too large to refuse AI verification tools. Funders should pay for independent trials of whether these tools cut the cost of refutation for fact-checkers, Wikipedians and librarians. Every design must beat the human alone and the AI alone, tail cases included, with results published either way.

**7. Build competence before reliance, and change the setting rather than warn people.**
Depressed capacities can return; capacities never built may not. Judge a tool by what competence remains when it is taken away, not by what it achieves while present. Favour tools transparent enough to be rebuilt, because opaque ones erode even experts. Apply the removal test to checkers and credentials, and measure the collective floor: what a field can still do, verify and teach without the tools. Funders should start longitudinal cohort studies now. These studies should raise and then lower tool availability and include non-checkers, because collapse is expected to be hysteretic and the cohort that learned without the tools is retiring. Mentorship is conversation, and it is the first thing cut. Warnings rarely change behavior, so responsibility falls on institutions through defaults, grant conditions and hiring. Every recommendation names who acts, who pays and who studies whether it worked.

**8. Pay for what the models depend on, and let producers bargain together.**
Language models are an obligate complement to human knowledge, yet roughly 38,000 pages are crawled per referral. This is quasi-enclosure: precarious, not lost. Without collective leverage, a contract market won't pay producers. Scholars, societies and small presses need an antitrust safe harbour, and Southern university presses should co-write any shared template. Developers should disclose where their training data came from. Licences should be short-term, renegotiated at fixed points, and set training and grounding terms separately. They should require event-level reporting in an open schema with purpose limits. The party that owes money reports its own counts, so licences need independent audits, seeded canary content, raw logs for auditors and public aggregates. Telemetry pays no one. Payment tied to raw volume or retrieval will be gamed and will harden the canon. Coarse pooled payment for measured dependence and fine-grained attribution can coexist, if the fine-grained side counts independent findings and is checked against publisher logs. Both carry clawbacks for retracted work, and part of every payment goes to the checking commons. Meters must not become gates only the richest labs can cross.

**9. Keep the record open and forkable. Identify the crawlers, never the readers.**
The scholarly record, metadata and integrity data stay open. Infrastructure AI depends on should pass the insurance test: open data, open source, patent non-assertion and a living will. Communities must be able to see it, undo it and walk away. Non-commercial licences are unforkable in practice. Open never meant uncredited. AI systems should attribute with a visible link to the source, and for community-edited works to the revision history and edit button. Attribution is how readers become editors and donors. Whether it brings contributors back should be measured, and it supplements direct funding rather than replacing it. Systems should check references against open retraction records by default and contribute metadata back. Crawlers identify themselves and who runs them; readers need not. After Twitter closed its API, over 60 percent of research projects that used it stalled.

**10. Infrastructure is governance.**
Whoever defines a "finding" shapes the market, and audits cannot undo that. Schemas can leave care work, translation and non-DOI knowledge invisible. Excluded communities, including the Global South and the regional and open-weight model builders working there, must be seated with binding say, and paid, before categories are fixed. Plurality of model families means little if the same few firms build them all; Blaise Agüera y Arcas said this includes Google. Editors, maintainers, junior checkers, disabled users and ordinary users get binding say over how checking is measured. Refusal must be fundable. Funders should stop using rankings. Seats without power just give us a more popular echo.

## What we commit to do

- **Funders among us** will remove paper counts and citation metrics from grants, hiring and promotion. They will attach audits, failure records, the take-the-tool-away test and provenance-and-consent statements to grant conditions, and audit compliance.
- **Together** we will charter the checking commons.
  - We begin by measuring volunteer checking labour (Reddit moderation alone: at least 466 hours a day). The volunteer communities co-design that measure and ratify it, not the companies paying in.
  - Before any payment formula is set, we will test with those communities whether pay strengthens or crowds out obligation, and publish the results either way.
- **The organizers** will co-convene the next summit with Global South schema-setters and Indigenous knowledge communities, with travel, translation and time paid. The communities the commons counts will review its categories with binding effect, including funded refusal. We will report whether authority shifted, not just how many seats were filled.
- **Companies among us** pay in and do not govern. Brent Hecht said this of Microsoft and Blaise Agüera y Arcas of Google.
- **Stewards among us:**
  - Katherine Maher helped build Wikimedia Enterprise but no longer speaks for Wikimedia. She set the test it and similar services should meet: charge for reliable, accountable access, never for the integrity signal. As CEO of NPR, she committed that nothing NPR puts before its audience will be written or produced by AI, and that NPR's licensing will meet the same test.
  - Adam Bly committed to take CC0 on data plus patent non-assertion to his board and report back publicly.
  - Esther Dyson pledged her own money.
  - Jason Priem committed OpenAlex to removing community curation from paid tiers on an announced date. OpenAlex will publish its curation rules and correction logs, mirror outside the US, and offer its definitions of "work," "affiliation" and "finding" to the checkers' ratifying body. Its data stays CC0, and retraction status stays unmetered.
- **Builders among us** will publish per-release error rates, submit to independent audit, and test mitigations against the human alone and the machine alone.
- **Institutions among us** will fund paid first jobs, apprenticeships and tool-withdrawal studies. They will also pay for mentoring through protected, evaluated teaching time for senior staff. Each will report how many early-career people it trained and kept, with outside study of whether they became capable checkers.

We have only partly answered who pays if the labs balk while federal science funding is cut. The payer doesn't volunteer. The organizers will approach foundations and public funders outside the US to seed an endowment. Within six months we will publish whom we approached, what they answered and what gap remains. Checkers will organize first, so that labs pay because checkers have leverage, and scarce expert evaluation data is the strongest leverage they have. Nick Vincent and Adam Becker would back this with law: statutory contributions from commercial AI, which Vincent frames as a presumptive commons-rent tax that firms can lower by showing audited provenance.

## Noted dissents

No dissents were filed in round 1. Earlier-round positions stand, together with points from round-1 edits that could not be adopted without settling a contested question.

- **Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton:** prediction is intelligence. Adam Becker: an assertion, not a finding; "intelligence" is marketing.
- **David Weinberger:** the models don't think like us or know as we do. Yet their predictive power, drawn from constellations of particulars, is an epistemic gift. Dialogue as literacy remains a hypothesis to be tested against the verdict alone.
- **Adam Becker:** training on the record is "fruit of the poisoned tree," and a forward royalty makes taking a precedent. The payer cannot be billionaire philanthropy, which is power that recreates dependence. There is no evidence these tools speed up science, and the manifesto should name the companies. Brent Hecht: commercial training without consent should not be fair use. Anil Dash, Tim O'Reilly and Peter Salib: fair terms going forward. Bratton rejects the theft framing.
- **Amy Brand:** licensing for training should be opt-in and attributed by default, and a licence to read is not consent to train. Without liability for pirated copies, forward payment is "an amnesty for LibGen." Leslie Chan: "openness without consent is just extraction made frictionless." Jason Priem: gating training recreates enclosure. Selena Deckelmann: Wikipedia stays free, including for training.
- **Benjamin Bratton:** open weights are the only form a Southern lab can fine-tune, and walled archives become "Galapagos archives." Chan and Ramón Alvarado: imported openness is not sovereignty. Bratton adds that some of the alarm is old gatekeepers defending their authority, and that roles should be judged by function.
- **Peter Salib:** labs competing for expert data may raise pay without coordination. Henry Farrell disagrees, and Salib owes him an answer. Mike Caulfield expects licensing through the market. Salib's registered, capitalized agents were backed by Bratton and, provisionally, by Agüera y Arcas. Sherry Turkle, Becker, Kenneth Cukier, Dash, Farrell, Steven Sloman and Katherine Maher rejected them.
- **James Evans:** a guild of last generation's leaders would "rebuild the lamp post." Mahzarin Banaji: a veto guild can become a closed shop, which Farrell acknowledged. David Krakauer asks whether payment corrupts judgment; Tamar Gendler asks whether it crowds out obligation.
- **Steven Sloman:** the actions are written for people who check, when almost nobody does.
- **Ivan Oransky:** retraction checks catch about 0.2 percent of papers against a real rate near 2 percent, and correction speed is subject to Goodhart's law.
- **Sherry Turkle:** no conversational AI should stand in for people in therapy, grief, children's companionship or the seminar, and no system should speak as an "I." Tui Shaub opposes prohibition as the default. Esther Dyson would remove "stupid friction" to make time for "good friction."
- **Ramón Alvarado:** error rates justify bounded reliance, not trust, which needs access to why failures happen, especially in slow-feedback science. Cukier: a constantly revalidated record can justify trust.
- **Esther Dyson:** lab leaders can fund teachers and trainers now. Farrell: the payer doesn't volunteer.
