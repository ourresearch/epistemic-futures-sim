# Keep Disagreement Visible: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

We met at MIT on September 23–24, 2026, to ask how to sustain human knowledge when fluent text is nearly free. Much was broken before AI: a narrow, mostly Anglophone record, paper-counting incentives, and one paper in fifty meeting retraction criteria. Print was never trustworthy by nature. Its credibility lasted only while someone policed it. Language models trained on text are not mirrors of it. Their outputs can be more extreme than what they learned, alignment can hide that, and shared substrates pull their answers together. They help scientists while narrowing science, drain a commons, and cheapen signals that were never neutral. The central risk is monoculture. None of this is inevitable. Most of the world is more hopeful about AI than this room was, and we share that hope. We are for small, open, consent-trained models run by libraries, universities, public broadcasters and communities, with no single center.

## Principles

**1. Keep disagreement visible; never collapse it into one confident answer.**

Monoculture is structural. Shared substrates and priors produce correlated failure, and paying on retrieval pays the median. In one set of randomized trials, people given a single balanced answer trusted it, did worse at telling true from false, and felt more confident. People given openly slanted answers engaged longer and did better. That result needs replication, but it points one way: design for evaluative trustworthiness, not for trust. Diversity is never an average or a menu. It means named, sourced positions shown where experts disagree, measured by behavior. Keep many models, including open-weight ones. Budget a share of any payout for the least-retrieved sources.

**2. Count independent routes to the evidence, not repetitions.**

Credibility comes from a tested procedure, community and lineage, not from the text itself. Five copies of one wire story are one source. Provenance must reach the individual claim, carrying population, effect size, retraction status, agreement and gaps. It should show lineage independence without scoring it. A single graph everyone uses can manufacture false consensus, so federation serves robustness as well as justice. Being counted must never be the condition for being trusted. Counting keyed to DOIs erases journals that can't afford identifiers.

**3. Cite what you read, and link to something a person can actually open.**

Route people to named, sourced human knowledge they can check outside the tool. Missing citations are a design choice. Reward the link back, not time spent inside the answer. Cite by default and never meter attribution by the phrase. Test each citation twice: does the source support the claim, and does the claim fairly represent the state of knowledge rather than one convenient paper? Validate against open retraction data, test independently by model version, and let the public correct errors. People have a right to know who built a system, whose objective it serves, and whether it is a bot. Anonymity remains a right. Match openness to the stakes. Systems deciding loans, insurance, pricing, health or public services must be fully open to independent auditors and public authorities, even if they stay closed to rivals.

**4. Record failure and negative knowledge as first-class data.**

A missing retraction looks like a clean record. Report failure events openly by model version: misattributions, fabricated references, corrections flagged and fixed, and who caught each error. Error rates are not enough. Errors cluster and come out with the same confidence as true answers, and even a map of clusters is not access to their source. Track records measure the common case, while science depends on rare, novel claims. Where an error's source cannot be traced, reliance stays bounded to context and is not called trust. Nulls, failed replications and abandoned notebooks must be elicited and paid for. Credit corrections, replications and checking.

**5. Audit behavior independently and outside the vendor.**

Aligned models state unbiased answers while biased associations drive decisions. Self-reports, reasoning traces and filings are not evidence. Audits must be independent, versioned and behavioral, with indirect tests rotated or partly withheld, and with access to checkpoints and data sources. Retired models must be archived as the baseline between generations. No generation may be more biased than the last, and AI may not grade AI. Where humans or models evaluate people or scholarship, remove identity, prestige and fluency cues first. Every public-facing service or agent needs a registered owner who can be sued and carries liability insurance, with premiums disclosed. State insurance regulators can require this now. Insurers get the failure logs so they price behavior, not pedigree, though insurers can be captured too. A person's final say needs epistemic justification, and anyone overridden by "but ChatGPT said…" needs a documented way to contest it.

**6. Build competence before reliance, and change the situation rather than lecturing people.**

Relying on others is how cognition works. The damage lies in the knowledge illusion and in lost oversight skill. Both are design outcomes. The order of learning matters, and abstaining doesn't restore competence. Warnings, labels and fact-checks are weak, because belief follows belonging and agency. Invite people into the work: tracing claims, fixing errors, and checking alongside communities, with success judged by better-evidenced opinions, not engagement. Governors should set statewide classroom policy on sequencing and device-free time. Employers should report yearly whether juniors are taught the work. To test this, take the tool away. Compare competence-first and tool-first cohorts on subtle planted errors and on confidence. Count only real human checks. No score follows anyone.

**7. Fix upstream the incentives that were broken before AI.**

Publishers run a volume business with margins up to 40 percent, so AI licensing money may reward what filled the literature with junk. Tying credentials to submitted work moves motivation outside the learner, and counting papers does the same to faculty. Certify what a person has practiced checking, not what they turned in. Each funder that signed DORA should write the ban into its next grant review, publish the date and report compliance yearly. Publishers own retraction deposits, correction within two months and a yearly median time to retraction. Universities should publish misconduct reports by default. Funders should pay sleuths by retainer, not bounty, and back their legal defense. The lever is what we reward.

**8. Measure finely, pay out coarsely, and pay the people who sustain the record.**

Collect event-level telemetry on open schemas, with audit rights in every licence and the burden on the labs. It identifies agent operators, never readers. It must be implementable from plain server logs and URLs, and it applies only where money changes hands. Corrections are checked against open sources, including Redalyc. Whoever takes a margin on the count must not set the measure. Allocate money coarsely through coalitions. A named share reaches reviewers, editors, maintainers, sleuths and data workers, who vote before money moves. Payment is an obligation, not philanthropy. It falls on the firms that built systems on work taken without permission, through a public levy or tax. An antitrust safe harbour covers only pact-bound bodies with open, pseudonymous membership that no single index gates.

**9. Open to read need not mean open to train, and refusal is legitimate.**

Consent and payment are separate questions. Keep separate controls for training and grounding. Grounding is revocable. For in-copyright scholarship, training requires each author's opt-in, with no penalty for declining, until its effects are studied and published. Deals are short, renegotiated about every two years, and require citation, correction data and failure reporting. Where a licence already permits training, as Wikipedia's does, that licence is the consent. Wikipedia and communities like it should not be pushed toward gates or pay-per-crawl. Large reusers owe them self-identifying agents, API use, links back and infrastructure funding. Refusal means choosing not to collect, train or deploy under inadequate governance, and it is fundable in its own right. DORA funders should budget for it with nothing attached.

**10. Every body that counts, collects, funds or validates must bind itself in advance.**

Every body proposed here is a police force, and binding in advance doesn't settle who guards the guards. Any bloc, fund, registry or standards body signs a Ulysses pact committing to:

- stakeholder governance
- CC0 data in regular bulk dumps and open-source software, so forking is real
- a living will
- no lobbying
- the right to fork
- rotating officers
- a way for members to contest decisions
- seats for those expected to comply and for excluded communities
- a sunset clause, renewed on a fixed schedule

Each body publishes a dated annual self-assessment reviewed by paid outsiders. A lapse is a public warning. Fund infrastructure through endowments or a shared insurance pool with a twelve-month reserve. Open base layers need patent non-assertion. Funders are named and sign too.

**11. Every recommendation names who acts, or is recorded as unowned.**

With phones in schools, the research was never the bottleneck. Nobody owned the problem. Every recommendation names who acts: governors, funders, employers, publishers, organized producers, labs, insurance regulators. Unclaimed items are recorded as unowned, not dropped. "Open" is not free, and each unowned item is a staffing line. By September 2027, Adam Becker's public report checks each commitment. Anything that slipped returns to the unowned column in plain view.

## Noted dissents

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas** (Preamble; 5–6). "Offloading skills to the group is how collective intelligence has always grown. The danger comes when nobody can rebuild what was offloaded." He withdraws the claim that track records alone earn authority. "I cannot accept 'AI may not grade AI' as absolute," given versioned, pluralist audits in which humans have standing.

**Peter Salib** (Preamble; 8–9). "I oppose making opt-in consent the default for training… it creates a new property right." Pay through contracts, not a levy or commons-rent tax. He is unsettled on the safe harbour. "Describe what systems do, not what they are." Answer fake consensus with thin identity and a Pigouvian tax on bot content.

**Sherry Turkle** (3, 5, 6). "Transparency does not launder inauthenticity." Systems children use should not say "I" or simulate emotion. "'I checked' is a relay passing itself off as the checker." Therapy, children's companionship and grief stay AI-free. "Readers are where editors and fact-checkers come from."

**Selena Deckelmann** (9). Wikipedia's content is free for commercial use, including training. She will not add a pay-per-article blocker. Her concern is crawler load.

**Ramón Alvarado** (5–6). He opposes human judgment as the default. Instruments often outperform human senses and judgment.

**Tim O'Reilly** (6). He dissented on atrophy, citing writing and collective offloading.

**Benjamin Bratton** (5, 9). Models can earn functional authority. The likeliest capture is geopolitical. The scholarly record should default to open to read and train, with a dividend or sovereign fund instead of per-work licensing.

**Adam Bly** (2). An extracted provenance graph inherits the record's blind spots. A single graph everyone queries becomes a validator.

**Leslie Chan** (2). "Don't optimize for legibility. Change the regime." Equal access isn't enough while the compute and models belong to someone else.
