# Keep Disagreement Visible: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

We met at MIT on September 23–24, 2026, to ask how human knowledge can be sustained when fluent text is nearly free. Much of what we found was broken before AI arrived. The journal record was already narrow and largely Anglophone. Funders and hiring committees already counted papers. About one paper in fifty already met retraction criteria, far above the actual retraction rate.

AI adds speed and scale. It helps scientists and hurts science. It draws down a human knowledge commons without putting anything back. It breaks the costly signals we used to judge work by. The central risk is not noise but monoculture, a pull toward the common case.

None of this is inevitable. This technology makes good people better and bad people worse. But almost everything below is currently unowned, and the work is putting names next to it.

## Principles

**1. Keep disagreement visible; never collapse it into one confident answer.**

Monoculture is structural. Shared substrates and priors produce correlated failure, and paying on retrieval pays the median. In one set of experiments, people given a single balanced answer offloaded the work, performed worse, and felt better about it. So diversity has to be real. It must be deliberately designed and measured, never an average or a menu, with named, sourced positions shown where experts disagree. Surface diversity is the wrong measure, so measure it by behavior. Diversity helps little if every model fails the same way for unseen reasons, so keep many models, including open-weight ones. Budget a share of any payout for the least-retrieved sources.

**2. Count independent routes to the evidence, not repetitions.**

Credibility comes from a tested procedure or community and from lineage, not from the text itself. Five copies of one wire story are one source, not five. Provenance must reach the individual claim, carrying population, effect size, retraction status, and a measure of agreement and gaps. It should show lineage independence without scoring it. A single graph that every lab and model uses can manufacture false consensus: claims from centralized research communities replicated less often even when many papers agreed. Federation is a matter of robustness as well as justice. Being counted must never be the condition for being trusted, and counting keyed to DOIs erases journals that can't afford identifiers.

**3. Cite what you read, and link to something a person can actually open.**

No answers from nowhere. Systems should route people to named, sourced human knowledge they can leave the tool to check. Few readers check citations, but their presence lets anyone do so, and clicking through is how readers become checkers. The wide variation in citation rates across models shows that missing citations are a design choice, not a technical limit. Reward the link that sends a reader back to the source, not time spent inside the answer. Cite by default, and never meter attribution by the phrase. Validate citations against open retraction data by default, and give the public a way to correct errors.

**4. Record failure and negative knowledge as first-class data.**

A missing retraction currently looks exactly like a clean record, and the measurement schemas proposed so far record only successes. Report failure events openly: misattributions, fabricated references, and corrections flagged and whether they were fixed. Break these out by model version and note who caught each error. Error rates are not enough; disclose known failure modes and where errors originate. Extraction from the published record inherits publication bias, so nulls, failed replications and abandoned notebooks must be deliberately elicited and paid for. Make corrections, retractions and replications visible, with credit for the checking. Willingness to be corrected can count as a cue only if retracting stops ending careers.

**5. Audit behavior independently and outside the vendor.**

Aligned models learn to state unbiased answers while biased associations still drive decisions. A system's self-reports, reasoning traces included, are not evidence of what drives it, and company filings are self-reports too. Audits must be independent, versioned and behavioral, using indirect and rotated tests. Auditors need access to checkpoints, and retired models must be kept for comparison. No generation may be more biased than the last. Track records belong outside the vendor. Don't let AI grade AI and call it verification. Human judgment is not a free fix: people paired with a moderately biased model copied its preferences almost exactly, and their decisions flow back into the next model's training data.

**6. Build competence before reliance, and change the situation rather than lecturing people.**

Atrophy is real, and it is a design outcome, not an accident. The order of learning matters, abstaining does not restore lost competence, and people who lose the skill can no longer catch the tool's errors. Transparency, a map rather than GPS, decides whether a tool can be internalized. Warnings and willpower are weak: knowing a cue misleads does not switch it off, and a label on its own doesn't change behavior. Change the situation instead. Redesign what institutions reward before lecturing students, make device-free spaces, and stop the collapse of mentorship for young workers. Sequencing must protect competent judgment, not senior gatekeepers. It is also a prediction, and it should be tested.

**7. Fix upstream the incentives that were broken before AI.**

Some of the hardest problems predate AI. Funders and hiring committees count papers. Publishers run a volume business with margins up to 40 percent, so AI licensing money flowing to them may reward the same incentives that filled the literature with junk. Students who outsourced problem sets thought they were beating a system that rewards the credential over understanding, and AI makes that worse. Stop counting papers, and have DORA signatories actually enforce it. Make retraction deposits a condition of indexing and licensing, with correction within two months. Spending on AI detection is flood insurance. The lever sits upstream, in what we reward.

**8. Measure finely, pay out coarsely, and pay the people who sustain the record.**

Collect fine-grained telemetry on open schemas, with audit rights in every licence and the burden on the labs. Allocate money coarsely, through coalitions and a commons backstop. Per-retrieval royalties invite gaming, concentrate rewards, and pay sources for writing to the retriever. Money should reach reviewers, editors, maintainers and sleuths through a named, checkable share, and should widen who can contribute. Retracted papers earn no royalties. Write payment as an obligation, because a gift can be withdrawn and lets the giver set the agenda. Knowledge producers, volunteers included, need an antitrust safe harbour to bargain collectively. Reviewers, small and Global South journals, and data workers must be voting members, not payees. Sleuths need legal protection as well as pay.

**9. Open to read, not to train, and refusal is legitimate.**

Consent and payment are separate questions, and open for reading need not mean open for training. "Open to read" must mean free for a human to click through. Keep separate controls for training and grounding, with grounding revocable and training held to a higher bar. This is not indiscriminate blocking, which locks out researchers and archivists while bad actors keep scraping, and it is not state "information sovereignty." Agents should identify themselves. Open access ended in more consolidation because liberal licences helped the biggest players accumulate content, so study consequences as policies roll out. Refusal to be used for training is a legitimate outcome, not a bargaining chip, and funders should support it with no deal attached.

**10. Every body that counts, collects, funds or validates must bind itself in advance.**

Who guards the guards? Every body proposed at this summit is a police force, and police forces get captured unless they bind themselves. Any bloc, fund, registry or standards body signs a Ulysses pact: stakeholder governance, open data and code, a living will, no lobbying, and the right to fork. It also needs:

- rotating officers
- audits anyone can inspect
- a way for members to contest decisions
- seats for those expected to comply and for historically excluded communities
- a sunset clause

Exit without funded maintenance and voice is not enough. A patent alone can make a fork unusable, so open base layers need non-assertion of patents. Every body publishes its funders, and funders sign the pact too.

**11. Every recommendation names who acts, or is recorded as unowned.**

With phones, the research was never the bottleneck; nobody owned the problem. Every recommendation here names who carries it out: governors and school boards for sequencing in schools, funders, employers, organized knowledge producers, the labs. Where no one has signed up, the item is recorded as unowned, not dropped. Anything needing US federal action is unowned for years. "Open" is not free, and every unowned item is a staffing line. The test of this manifesto is how fast names and dates fill that column.

## Noted dissents

**Selena Deckelmann** (Principle 9). Wikipedia's content is free for commercial use, including training, and she will not put up a pay-per-article blocker. Her concern is infrastructure load: crawlers saturate network links and defeat caching.

**Jason Priem** (Principle 9). He rejects the claim that open mostly helps the biggest players. Data and intelligence are now decoupled, so a grad student in Lagos can point a model at the same record the biggest lab uses. Closing the record means only the rich can license it.

**Peter Salib** (Principles 8–9). Consent "was never an entitlement" here, since 235 years of copyright left no settled expectation about training. He prefers service contracts plus general-revenue welfare to any levy, favors more buyers as the remedy for unequal bargaining, and is undecided on the safe harbour.

**Adam Becker** (Principle 8). He dissents from the market-design framing. "Quasi-enclosure" is too polite, since the systems were built by taking copyrighted work without permission. Payment must be an obligation backed by a tax on concentrated wealth, and he would not sign unless moderators and annotators vote in the coalition.

**Ramón Alvarado** (Principles 5–6). He opposes making human judgment the default. Where people get the final say, that must be justified on epistemic grounds, since instruments often outperform human senses and judgment. He also names a harm omitted here: "but ChatGPT said…" demotes the person who did the checking, which he calls testimonial injustice.

**Steven Sloman** (Principle 6). Relying on others is how human cognition works, so relying on AI is not a loss in itself. The damage lies in the knowledge illusion and lost oversight skill. He doubts general judgment training works and favors tools for fact-checkers and Wikipedia editors.

**Tim O'Reilly, David Weinberger and Blaise Agüera y Arcas** (Principle 6). They dissented on atrophy, citing writing and collective offloading. Weinberger calls the technology an epistemic gift: unlike Socrates' text, these systems can be questioned and respond to the particular reader.

**Sherry Turkle** (Principle 6). In therapy, children's companionship and grief, the first question is whether AI belongs at all, before any design fix. "Human in the loop" already assumes the machine belongs. She would drop the first-person "I," starting with children.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton.** They argue that a model can earn functional authority through per-version track records, against Mike Caulfield's and Sherry Turkle's view that it should be a conduit, not an arbiter. Agüera y Arcas urges building agent institutions with checks and balances, not "fencing off the machine."

**Benjamin Bratton.** The enclosure most likely to end in capture is geopolitical: computation splitting into sealed American, Chinese and European stacks. Open weights are the commons most worth defending, and alignment must run both ways. He opposed per-work licensing, favoring a dividend or sovereign wealth fund.

**Adam Bly** (Principle 2). A provenance graph extracted from the indexed record inherits that record's blind spots and missing nulls. A single graph everyone queries becomes a hub, or validator.

**Leslie Chan** (Principle 2). "Don't optimize for legibility. Change the regime." Making checking legible on the existing identifier and index stack leaves open who decides whose position counts. Equal access to the record isn't enough when the compute and the models still belong to someone else.

**Katherine Maher.** Most of the world is more optimistic about AI than the room, and a downside-only diagnosis costs credibility.
