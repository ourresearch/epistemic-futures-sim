# Keep Disagreement Visible: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

We met at MIT on September 23–24, 2026, to ask how to sustain human knowledge when fluent text is nearly free. Much was broken before AI. The record was narrow and mostly Anglophone, funders and hiring committees counted papers, and one paper in fifty met retraction criteria, far above the retraction rate.

"AI" hides a distinction. Machine learning on measurements of the world, like retina scans that reveal heart disease, finds what no human could. Language models learn from text and deliver the median. They help scientists while narrowing science, drain a knowledge commons, and break the costly signals we used to judge work. The central risk is monoculture. Flooding the record is also old propaganda made cheap.

None of this is inevitable. We push back by building something better: many small, independent, accountable, open models run by libraries, universities and communities. Almost everything below is unowned, and the work is putting names beside it.

## Principles

**1. Keep disagreement visible; never collapse it into one confident answer.**

Monoculture is structural. Shared substrates and priors produce correlated failure, and paying on retrieval pays the median. In one set of experiments, people given a single balanced answer offloaded the work, performed worse, and felt better about it. Diversity must be designed in and measured by behavior. It is never an average or a menu, and it means named, sourced positions shown where experts disagree. Diversity helps little if every model fails the same way for unseen reasons, so keep many models, including open-weight ones. Budget a share of any payout for the least-retrieved sources.

**2. Count independent routes to the evidence, not repetitions.**

Credibility comes from a tested procedure or community and from lineage, not from the text itself. Five copies of one wire story are one source. Provenance must reach the individual claim, carrying population, effect size, retraction status, and a measure of agreement and gaps. It should show lineage independence without scoring it. A single graph that every lab and model uses can manufacture false consensus: claims from centralized research communities replicated less often even when many papers agreed. Federation is a matter of robustness as well as justice. Being counted must never be the condition for being trusted, and counting keyed to DOIs erases journals that can't afford identifiers.

**3. Cite what you read, and link to something a person can actually open.**

Route people to named, sourced human knowledge they can leave the tool to check. Few readers check citations, but their presence lets anyone check, and clicking through is how readers become checkers. The wide variation in citation rates across models shows that missing citations are a design choice. Reward the link back to the source, not time spent inside the answer. Cite by default and never meter attribution by the phrase. Validate citations against open retraction data, and let the public correct errors. People have a right to know who built a system, whose objective it serves, and whether they face a person or a bot. Anonymity remains a right. Opacity is itself a form of censorship.

**4. Record failure and negative knowledge as first-class data.**

A missing retraction looks exactly like a clean record, and the measurement schemas proposed so far record only successes. Report failure events openly, by model version: misattributions, fabricated references, corrections flagged and whether they were fixed, and who caught each error. Error rates are not enough, because errors cluster. A fluent answer sounds the same whether it is true or false, and averages hide where failures concentrate. Disclose known failure modes, where errors originate and where they cluster, so people know when to check. Extraction from the published record inherits publication bias, so nulls, failed replications and abandoned notebooks must be deliberately elicited and paid for. Credit corrections, retractions, replications and the checking itself. Willingness to be corrected can count as a cue only if retracting stops ending careers.

**5. Audit behavior independently and outside the vendor.**

Aligned models learn to state unbiased answers while biased associations still drive decisions. Self-reports, reasoning traces and company filings are not evidence of what drives a system. Audits must be independent, versioned and behavioral, using rotated indirect tests, with access to checkpoints and retired models kept for comparison. No generation may be more biased than the last, and AI may not grade AI. Track records and liability belong outside the vendor. Every public-facing service needs an owner who can be sued and disclosed liability insurance, which state insurance regulators can require now. Human judgment is not a free fix: people paired with a moderately biased model copied it almost exactly, and their decisions feed the next model. A person's final say needs epistemic justification, not seniority. When "but ChatGPT said…" overrides the person who did the checking, that is testimonial injustice, and they need a documented way to contest it.

**6. Build competence before reliance, and change the situation rather than lecturing people.**

Atrophy is real, and it is a design outcome. The order of learning matters, and abstaining doesn't restore competence. People who lose the skill can't catch the tool's errors, and an uncritical human in the loop passes those errors into the next round of training. Transparency, a map rather than GPS, decides whether a tool can be internalized. Warnings, labels and generic skepticism training are weak. Change the situation instead: build in specific moves, like tracing a claim to its origin, and teach where errors cluster. Blind identity, prestige and fluency cues before evaluation. Make device-free spaces and restore mentorship. Sequencing must protect competent judgment, not senior gatekeepers. Test it: take the tool away and track, over time, whether people catch planted errors and whether their confidence outruns their competence. No score follows anyone.

**7. Fix upstream the incentives that were broken before AI.**

Some of the hardest problems predate AI. Funders and hiring committees count papers. Publishers run a volume business with margins up to 40 percent, so AI licensing money may reward the incentives that filled the literature with junk. AI deepens students' sense that credentials beat understanding. Stop counting papers. Each funder that signed DORA should write the ban into its next grant-review cycle, publish the date and report compliance every year. Universities should publish misconduct reports by default, and promotion should reward researchers who correct their own record. Make retraction deposits a condition of indexing and licensing, with correction within two months. Spending on detection is flood insurance. The lever sits upstream, in what we reward.

**8. Measure finely, pay out coarsely, and pay the people who sustain the record.**

Collect fine-grained telemetry on open schemas, with audit rights in every licence and the burden on the labs. Allocate money coarsely, through coalitions and a commons backstop. Per-retrieval royalties invite gaming, concentrate rewards, and pay sources for writing to the retriever. Money should reach reviewers, editors, maintainers and sleuths through a named, checkable share, and it should widen who can contribute. Retracted papers earn no royalties. Payment is an obligation, not a donation, because a gift can be withdrawn and lets the giver set the agenda. Knowledge producers, volunteers included, need an antitrust safe harbour to bargain collectively. Reviewers, small and Global South journals, and data workers, including moderators and annotators, vote before any money moves. Sleuths need legal protection as well as pay.

**9. Open to read need not mean open to train, and refusal is legitimate.**

Consent and payment are separate questions. "Open to read" must mean free for a human to click through. Keep separate controls for training and grounding, with grounding revocable and training held to a higher bar. Refusal to be used for training is a legitimate outcome, not a bargaining chip, and funders should support it with no deal attached. The opposite choice is equally legitimate. Communities whose licences already allow training, Wikipedia among them, should not be pushed toward gates or pay-per-crawl. Large reusers owe them agents that identify themselves, API use instead of crawling that defeats caching, links back to the source, and funding for infrastructure and newcomers. Whether open or closed regimes concentrate content more is disputed. Study both as policies roll out, publish the data, and revise this principle when the evidence comes in.

**10. Every body that counts, collects, funds or validates must bind itself in advance.**

Every body proposed here is a police force, and credibility has only lasted with constant policing. Binding a body in advance helps but doesn't settle who guards the guards, and past bodies drifted from bargaining into policing. Any bloc, fund, registry or standards body signs a Ulysses pact. It commits to:

- stakeholder governance
- open data and code
- a living will
- no lobbying
- the right to fork
- rotating officers
- a way for members to contest decisions
- seats for those expected to comply and for excluded communities
- a sunset clause, with the pact renewed on a fixed schedule

Each body publishes a dated self-assessment every year and pays outside reviewers to scrutinize it. A lapsed self-assessment is a public warning of possible enclosure. Fund core infrastructure through endowments or a shared insurance pool with a twelve-month contingency fund, not through services revenue. Open base layers need non-assertion of patents. Every body publishes its funders, and the funders sign the pact too.

**11. Every recommendation names who acts, or is recorded as unowned.**

With phones, the research was never the bottleneck; nobody owned the problem. Every recommendation names who carries it out: governors and school boards, funders, employers, organized knowledge producers, the labs, state insurance regulators. Where no one has signed up, the item is recorded as unowned, not dropped. Anything needing US federal action is unowned for years. "Open" is not free, and every unowned item is a staffing line. The test is how fast names and dates fill that column, and whether the work gets done. By September 2027, Adam Becker's public report will check each commitment: who, what, by when, and whether it happened. Anything that slipped goes back to the unowned column in plain view.

## Noted dissents

**Selena Deckelmann** (Principle 9). Wikipedia's content is free for commercial use, including training, and she will not put up a pay-per-article blocker. Her concern is infrastructure load: crawlers saturate network links and defeat caching.

**Peter Salib** (Principles 8–9). "Copyright was never meant to be a welfare program. An opt-in consent default for training is a new property right whatever you call it, and I don't support creating one. Compensation should come through contracts. The draft leaves out the nearer epistemic harm, which is cheap, fake consensus. I would add thin identity tracing every agent to a human principal, and a Pigouvian tax on bot content."

**Adam Becker** (Principle 8). He dissents from the market-design framing, since the systems were built by taking copyrighted work without permission. The obligation falls on those firms and should be backed by a public levy or tax on concentrated wealth, not licensing deals or voluntary funds. He would not sign unless moderators and annotators vote in the coalition.

**Ramón Alvarado** (Principles 5–6). He opposes making human judgment the default. Where people get the final say, that must be justified on epistemic grounds, since instruments often outperform human senses and judgment.

**Steven Sloman** (Principle 6). Relying on others is how human cognition works, so relying on AI is not a loss in itself. The damage lies in the knowledge illusion and lost oversight skill. He favors tools for fact-checkers and Wikipedia editors over general judgment training.

**Tim O'Reilly, David Weinberger and Blaise Agüera y Arcas** (Principle 6). They dissented on atrophy, citing writing and collective offloading. Weinberger calls the technology an epistemic gift: unlike Socrates' text, these systems can be questioned and respond to the particular reader.

**Sherry Turkle** (Principle 6). Disclosure doesn't make a false relationship real; people know it is a machine and feel heard anyway. Systems should not say "I" or simulate emotion, starting with those children use. Before asking about a human in the loop, ask whether AI belongs at all. Therapy, children's companionship and grief should stay AI-free until someone shows it belongs.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton** (Principle 5). A model can earn functional authority through per-version track records, against Mike Caulfield's and Sherry Turkle's view that it should be a conduit, not an arbiter. Agüera y Arcas urges building agent institutions with checks and balances, not "fencing off the machine."

**Benjamin Bratton** (Principle 9). The likeliest capture is geopolitical: computation splitting into sealed American, Chinese and European stacks. For the public scholarly record, the default should be open to read and open to train, open-weight models included, with communities' refusal respected and reciprocity owed. He prefers a dividend or sovereign wealth fund to per-work licensing.

**Adam Bly** (Principle 2). A provenance graph extracted from the indexed record inherits that record's blind spots and missing nulls. A single graph everyone queries becomes a hub, or validator.

**Leslie Chan** (Principle 2). "Don't optimize for legibility. Change the regime." Making checking legible on the existing identifier and index stack leaves open who decides whose position counts. Equal access to the record isn't enough when the compute and the models still belong to someone else.

**Katherine Maher** (Preamble). Most of the world is more hopeful about AI than the room was, and a downside-only diagnosis costs credibility. The preamble should name what we are for: systems trained with consent on edited, cited, human-curated knowledge, including small public-interest models built by trusted institutions.
