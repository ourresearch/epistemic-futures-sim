# Harvest — Session 2: AI as Epistemic Actor: Risks, Capabilities, and Governance

1. **Transparency should target money flows and failures, not model weights.** This came from Dyson (business models), Alvarado (disclosed classes of failure), Weinberger and Cukier (evidence of reliability, constantly revalidated), Caulfield (report fabrication rates at release), and O'Reilly (SEC filings of operating metrics and incidents). Others challenged whether this is enough: Banaji said aligned models learn to pass output tests while covert bias persists, so she wants an independent battery of indirect tests; Agüera y Arcas said filings are self-reports and need outside behavioral audits; Gendler said disclosure does not dissolve automatic responses.

2. **Unresolved: whether accountability can run only to people.** Dyson, Cukier and Becker held that it must run to the humans who deploy, and warned that agent entities would become liability shields. Salib and Bratton proposed registered agent entities with seizable assets and revocable credentials, on the grounds that suing a principal who couldn't have prevented a harm deters nothing, and Agüera y Arcas proposed attaching accountability to defined roles. Thin identity for agents had support from Dyson, Salib and Springer.

3. **Most speakers located the interface harm in the single smooth answer rather than the first-person "I."** These were Weinberger, Agüera y Arcas, Shaub, Gendler, Caulfield, O'Reilly and Bratton, drawing on Evans's finding that a balanced answer left people worse off and more confident. Turkle granted the point but held that "I" is what makes the answer persuasive, and that removing it should be a design standard, starting with children. Separately, Krakauer, Sloman and Gendler argued that none of this helps unless people keep the skill to judge, and Krakauer said this requires competence before reliance because lost competence does not return.

4. **Owners for the rules.** Miller asked that every rule carry an owner's name or be recorded as "unowned." Dash, Pomerantsev and Farrell said anything needing US federal action is unowned for now. Proposed non-federal owners included funders (Shaub, Oransky, Krakauer, Chan) and organized knowledge producers bargaining under an antitrust safe harbour (Vincent, Farrell, Hecht). Any new infrastructure would be held to Bilder's exit test and Chan's question of who governs, with Johns's warning that registers tend to be captured by the parties they serve.

5. **Systems that draw on the commons should give back.** Proposals included:
   * citing and linking by default (Dash, Deckelmann)
   * public correction loops (Maher)
   * open forensic and retraction data (Priem, Bilder)
   * paid sleuths and measurement of unpaid labor (Oransky, Hecht)
   * a commons rent on undocumented provenance (Vincent)

   Unresolved is whether the commons stays open for training. Deckelmann and Maher would keep content free and charge for access or service, Brand called for consent, attribution and compensation, and Chan called for funded refusal.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
- Proposed that anyone offering these systems to the public, even for free, must buy liability insurance and disclose the premiums, because good insurers inspect and turn down bad risks. The harvest does not record this proposal.
- Named the reverse risk "information diabetes": people starting to think like LLMs, fluently and without understanding. She framed the remedy as protecting human attention and time.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Said insurance premiums price how often errors occur, not strange errors nobody has seen yet. He also warned that Nissenbaum's many-hands problem makes it hard to pin accountability on people even where it should run to them.
- Said "actor" should mean an epistemic technology, not a mind.

**James Evans**
- Reported that scientists using AI publish and get cited far more, while the range of topics science covers shrinks by nearly five percent and scientists engage with each other about 22 percent less. He described this as a collective harm with no liable party, beyond the reach of insurance or registries. The harvest cites only his balanced-answer finding.
- Called for large-scale verification of data, code and instruments by humans and AIs together, funded as a science in its own right.

**Peter Salib**
- Argued that outputs nobody controls are nobody's protected speech, so outputs can be regulated directly without it being censorship. Pomerantsev opposed this.
- Said that if owners use agent entities to hide, those entities should be made unlimited-liability.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Used ELIZA to argue that the vulnerability came before any business model, so the money trail found the problem but did not create it. She also said "transparency" has flipped from meaning you can open the hood to meaning a surface so smooth you never ask what is under it.

**Kara Miller**
- Said knowing hasn't moved anyone, citing unheeded WHO screen guidance and late phone bans. She asked that the room take seriously that people closest to tech are often strictest with their own kids.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Harvest item 3 lists him among those who located the harm in the single smooth answer. What he said was that his conversations with Claude were something new, not averaging with a personality attached, and that he would require disclosure of what a system optimizes for rather than ban first-person speech.
- Said collective narrowing can only be measured with standardized telemetry on what was read, cited and dropped.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Said human accountability already fails in science: the most likely outcome of misconduct is a long and fruitful career. He called unenforced liability as performative as Salib's undeterring liability. This cuts against item 2's "accountability to people" position.
- Located the problem on the demand side, in publish-or-perish. He proposed that NIH stop counting papers and enforce DORA, that ORI get a budget and subpoena power, and that universities publish misconduct reports by default.
- Opposed AI detectors as the headline fix.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Said state data poisoning, such as the Pravda network, is a political act, not a market failure, and that a commercial agent registry won't see it.
- Framed transparency as a citizen's right under Article 19 rather than a regulatory filing, and called opacity a form of censorship.
- Would regulate covert, coordinated, inauthentic behaviour rather than outputs, because content rules feed "information sovereignty." He supported mandatory bot disclosure as in California.

**Steven Sloman**
- Disputed that reliance itself is the danger, since people have always outsourced their knowing. He said the question is whether the cues people rely on still point to knowledge.
- Said provenance should count independent sources, not echoes, and proposed the NTSB as the institutional model for failure analysis.

**Alex Springer**
- Supports agent identity with no central registry: signed manifests indexed by many non-authoritative directories. This cuts against Dyson's registry. O'Reilly and Weinberger also favored open protocols.
- Said a valid signature proves identity, not that licence claims are true. He added that retrieval is the only event verifiable without the platforms, and that everything from grounding onward is platform self-reporting.

**David Weinberger**
- Said humans in the loop sometimes make outcomes worse, citing studies of fake-review detection and diagnosis. He called for "humans in the flow," actively working the problem.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Disputed Dyson's "just following orders," saying these systems routinely do things their builders never imagined and that their agency depends on how they are deployed. He also said Alvarado's cultural-technology framing breaks down once a running instance is dealing with people.
- Attributed narrowing to architecture: a monoculture of a few giant, frozen models. His remedy was many models, including open-weight ones, that keep learning.

**Jason Priem**
- Said the greatest danger is enclosure of the scholarly record in walled gardens, not model opacity. He argued an open record lets data be separated from intelligence and makes poisoning harder. He was openly bullish, calling it "intelligence."

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Said research-integrity tools are becoming closed and expensive, so anyone who can't afford them is treated as suspect. He called this evaluation being enclosed again.

**Amy Brand**
- Cautioned against treating "open" as protection. She said open access ended in more consolidation because liberal licences helped the biggest players accumulate content.

**David Krakauer**
- Warned that a competence-before-reliance requirement must protect competent judgment, not senior gatekeepers, or it will lock in the narrowing.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Proposed a refrigerator-style standard: no new model generation may be more biased than the last, with retired models kept for comparison.
- Reported that people paired with a moderately biased model copied its racial preferences almost exactly and resisted only glaring bias. She warned that the human in the flow is where bias enters the next round of training data. This cuts against the "humans in the flow" remedy.

**Adam Becker**
- Framed the problem as power, not market tuning, noting that these companies fund the politicians and advisers who would write the rules. His proposed owners were labor unions, tax authorities, public funding for journalism and sleuths, and moderators and data workers with a vote in governance.
- Called the systems' output bullshit in Frankfurt's sense. He said the "actor" framing and "prediction is intelligence" are the sales pitch and give builders an excuse. He also said open alternatives must pay and enfranchise workers or they only swap the landlord.
