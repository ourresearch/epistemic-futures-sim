# Harvest — Session 4: Epistemic Authority — Credibility in an Age of Infinite Scale

1. **Credibility comes from a tested procedure or community, not from an individual, and independent routes count, not repetitions.** Tamar Gendler's regress led the room to locate the checking node in a room or procedure tested over time (Mahzarin Banaji, Steven Sloman, David Weinberger, Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton), and to count independent lineages back to the evidence (Steven Sloman, James Evans, Adrian Johns). Alex Springer's citation-laundering audit showed that today five copies of one wire story look like five sources, so lineage needs finding-level provenance and outside audits (Adam Bly, Geoffrey Bilder, David Weinberger).

2. **Correction records should be a weighted cue, but they are broken at the source and easy to game.** Steven Sloman, Jason Priem and Amy Brand proposed making corrections, retractions and replications visible, first-class data (including CRediT roles for checking). Ivan Oransky and Geoffrey Bilder warned that a missing retraction currently looks like a clean record, and David Krakauer and Ivan Oransky warned that any such count will be gamed. Proposed fixes were retraction deposits as a condition of indexing and licensing, the two-month correction standard, and funders enforcing DORA (Ivan Oransky), plus reviewers withholding labor from non-compliant journals (Henry Farrell).

3. **Unresolved: whether a model can hold authority.** Mike Caulfield and Sherry Turkle held that the system writing an answer has no stakes or standing and should be a conduit, not an arbiter. Caulfield narrowed this to allow instrument-style trust for systems checked against ground truth and independently revalidated, a line Kenneth Cukier drew too, excluding language models. Blaise Agüera y Arcas and Benjamin Bratton argued for functional authority earned through per-version track records. Ramón Alvarado replied that reliability without access to how failures arise is not trustworthiness. Peter Salib and Esther Dyson argued that stakes can be created through liability law and insurance. Several speakers who accepted track records insisted they be held outside the vendor (Geoffrey Bilder, Tui Shaub, Katherine Maher).

4. **"Expensive to fake" can become a new seniority.** Tui Shaub warned that signals that are costly to fake are also costly to earn, and could shut out newcomers, second-language scholars and excluded regions. Leslie Chan added that the identifier and index infrastructure is itself captured and needs governing, with rankings kept out of funding and seats for those made invisible. James Evans reported that the most-credited reviewers disproportionately criticize novel work, and proposed measuring and publishing concentration. Kara Miller's Blue Zones case showed the checking came from an outsider. Proposed remedies were audits open to anyone (Tui Shaub), persistent pseudonyms (Anil Dash), and showing what people did rather than scoring them (Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann).

5. **Checkers must be recruited, trained and paid, and no one named who pays.** People become checkers by clicking through to sources, so cite-and-link by default is a credibility intervention (Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann, Brent Hecht, Katherine Maher, Tim O'Reilly). The competence decays without use, which calls for seeded-error audits (David Krakauer), investment in reading (Sherry Turkle), participation in the checking (Peter Pomerantsev), and legal protection for sleuths (Peter Pomerantsev, Ivan Oransky). Many said to pay the checkers: publishers through a bounty pool (Ivan Oransky), funders first (Brent Hecht, Amy Brand), labs and foundations through a pooled fund (Tim O'Reilly), and collective bargaining so the pay lasts (Nick Vincent). But when Kara Miller asked which funder writes the check and by when, no one named one.

## Minority positions

**Tamar Gendler**
Warned that knowing a cue misleads does not switch it off (the G. I. Joe fallacy), so labels and literacy campaigns should be judged by whether they change the situation in which people meet a claim. She also asked which university strands must stay bundled to keep producing checkers, and the harvest does not take up that question directly.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
Held that even a human who checks absorbs bias from name, affiliation and fluency. She proposed withholding those cues through blinding, and where blinding fails, measuring outcomes against a baseline and publishing them.

**Adam Becker**
Named wealth as today's most powerful relaying node and called for funding disclosure. His example was a magazine, secretly funded by Peter Thiel, that ran creationism beside real research.

**Alex Springer**
Said most of his own measurement spec fails the hard-to-fake test. Only retrieval has two witnesses, grounding and citation are reported by the platform that may owe money, and a signature proves who reported an event, not that it happened.

**Sherry Turkle**
Wants systems made more mechanical, starting with children and classrooms. She would drop the first-person "I" ("I checked," "I'm confident") because it presents a relay as a checking node. Blaise Agüera y Arcas replied that a flat oracle voice is fluent too and argued for visibly contestable answers instead. Turkle accepted contestability but kept her position on the "I."

**Nick Vincent**
Said AI labs already buy first-order checking as gig work under the name "evaluation," and framed the choice as gig labor versus guilds that are legitimate only while members check and publish failures by name. He also proposed crediting terse, verifiable artifacts (raw data, code, logs) over polish, a point Jason Priem shared.

**David Weinberger**
Objected to the word "terminal." In his view, authority as a stop sign was mostly a feature of paper, and in a linked medium transparency does much of that work.

**Jason Priem**
Argued authority should sit neither in the journal brand nor in a credentialed person. He said a model can read the checking record faster than any person and that OpenAlex should publish its own error rates first.

**Amy Brand**
Argued attribution is what makes someone a member of the checking community, so stripping credit dissolves the room rather than opening it. She accepted per-version track records but said hallucination is structural and the large labs' interests diverge from verifiable knowledge.

**Ivan Oransky**
Said willingness to be corrected can only be weighted as a cue if retracting stops ending careers. Researchers who correct honest errors should be celebrated.

**David Krakauer**
Argued that a track record audited by people raised on the tool measures what they can still see, not what is true. He separated expertise, a testable capacity, from authority, a position.

**Ramón Alvarado**
Named a harm the harvest omits: when an administrator says "but ChatGPT said," the relay overrules the person who did the checking. He and John Symons call this testimonial injustice.

**Adam Bly**
Qualified Adam Becker's "pay them first, before building anything new": without a finding-level audit trail, paid sleuths are checking echoes by hand. He also committed to publishing his company's error rates beside its benchmark scores.

**Benjamin Bratton**
Called the loss of fluency as a scarce signal a gain for everyone outside a few institutions. He said new signals should be open to newcomers, "agents included," from day one.

**Leslie Chan**
Said Mike Caulfield's "people in a position to know" leaves open who decides whose position counts. His line "Don't optimize for legibility. Change the regime" cuts against proposals to make checking legible on the existing identifier and index stack.

**Anil Dash**
Identified tech creators treated as authorities outside their fields as "the biggest relay in this room," and the one he comes from. He also said chat is a bad interface for real work.

**Selena Deckelmann**
Advised against bounties, which cuts against Ivan Oransky's bounty-style pool. She said a human in the loop is not enough and that patrollers need tools that surface disagreement.

**James Evans**
Cautioned against building on the contestable-answer result, which he said is his own group's single set of experiments. He also noted that his independence finding comes from one domain.

**Henry Farrell**
Framed credibility as legitimacy. Suspicion that review was outsourced strips authority even from good reviews, and per-version track records measure the common case while summarizing systems are worst at the rare, novel claims that matter most.

**Brent Hecht**
Proposed measuring volunteer checking labor independently rather than through company telemetry, and assessing company dependence the way a tax assessor would. Citing Ivan Oransky, he warned against adding duties for unpaid reviewers, which sits in tension with Henry Farrell's reviewer refusal.

**Adrian Johns**
Said there was never a golden age of credible print, and that the machinery of authority gets turned to private interest, as when Nehemiah Grew used the Royal Society's register to protect his business. In his view, registries work only on top of existing, paid policing.

**Kara Miller**
Named political power as a gatekeeper. A president publicly deciding who is good at their job does what relaying billionaires do, from the other direction.

**Tim O'Reilly**
Argued that platforms profit from "algorithmic authority" by putting worse results in trusted slots. He said the wide variation in citation rates across models shows missing citations are a design choice.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
Said every proposal was supply-side, while people mostly trust those they work with on a shared project. An audit-everything culture could feed cynicism unless people can take part, and every auditor, fund and registry should publish its funders.

**Peter Salib**
Argued rules premised on current models will be outrun within a couple of model generations. He proposed a Pigouvian tax on platforms carrying undisclosed bot-generated volume.
