# Harvest — What might a well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace look like?

1. The room broadly agreed to "measure finely, pay out coarsely." That means fine-grained telemetry, with allocation left to coalitions and a commons backstop instead of per-retrieval royalties, so that sources aren't rewarded for writing to the retriever (Tim O'Reilly, Alex Springer, Nick Vincent, Jason Priem, Geoffrey Bilder, David Weinberger, Blaise Agüera y Arcas, David Krakauer). Geoffrey Bilder added that the raw event data must be open and backstop money should go to endowments, and Tui Shaub asked that part of it go to the least-retrieved sources.

2. Every proposed event measures use, not reading. Sherry Turkle, David Weinberger and David Krakauer argued for rewarding the link that sends a reader back to the source, not time spent inside the answer, and "cite and link by default" drew support from Tim O'Reilly, David Weinberger and Jason Priem. Alex Springer said "engaged" means acting on the source and includes no dwell time, conceded that no event records whether anyone read it, and said he would back a coalition norm that pays for link-outs only.

3. The schema records only successes. Ramón Alvarado proposed openly reported failure events: misattributions, fabricated references, and corrections flagged and whether they were fixed. Mahzarin Banaji, Adam Becker (who wants them mandatory) and Adam Bly supported this. Mahzarin Banaji also asked that failures be broken out by model version and that retrieval and citation choices be independently audited for bias before royalties follow them, and Adam Bly argued that provenance must reach the individual claim, not stop at the document.

4. Being counted should not be the price of access. Jason Priem and Geoffrey Bilder warned against attaching reporting conditions to open content, and Alex Springer agreed that reporting clauses belong only in paid licences. Amy Brand and Tui Shaub backed the training/grounding split as a contract and governance principle, with grounding revocable and training held to a higher bar, and Amy Brand held that open for reading need not mean open for training.

5. Unresolved: who governs the money and who counts as a seller. Geoffrey Bilder and Amy Brand would require any collecting body to sign a Ulysses pact. On their own organizations, Alex Springer committed to an independent audit of his self-attested accreditation, and Adam Bly said his graph could not be cleanly forked. Adam Becker would frame payment as an obligation owed and would not sign unless moderators and annotators vote in the coalition, and Tui Shaub asked which funder will impose these terms and meet the pact itself.

## Minority positions

**Tim O'Reilly**
- He is not in the "single smooth answer" camp and wants protocols to be engineered arguments, not agreements imposed by whoever is dominant.
- He opposed lump-sum training licences because they entrench the richest labs and rarely pay authors, and he wants payment for output instead. No royalty should flow until the retrieval has been checked against open retraction data.
- He asked for a budget line for recording null results, because telemetry only rewards what gets written down.

**Alex Springer**
- He said what enters a context window can only ever be self-reported. Checking grounding against retrieval flags cases for audit but proves nothing.
- He held that parties who take a margin on the count, his own organization included, should not set the measurement currency.

**Nick Vincent**
- He proposed a presumptive commons-rent tax on "unexplained capability," which firms can reduce to zero by documenting licensed or reciprocal provenance.
- He wants an antitrust safe harbour for content producers. He named evaluation data as where collective bargaining has the most leverage now.
- He warned against turning everything into a market and asked to keep open routes into the commons.

**Sherry Turkle**
- She argued that a marketplace paying for summaries will buy more summaries, at a cost to imagination and empathy, not just to accuracy.

**David Weinberger**
- He is wary of per-retrieval telemetry because of who ends up holding the meter. He said an algorithm that insists on attribution would be worse than widespread non-attribution.
- His support is conditional on the content itself staying free, with charges only for reliable, correctable access.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- He argued that all creative work recombines prior work and that tracing value to individual contributors will always be approximate, for humans as well as models. This cuts against Adam Bly's call for claim-level provenance.
- He agreed with Nick that the terms of the subsidy should not sit with the provider alone.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- He called POSI's "you can't sell data, so sell services" naive in hindsight.

**Jason Priem**
- He said OpenAlex meters to recover costs, not because retrieval deserves a royalty. He wants funders to pay directly for correction and policing, not just plumbing.

**Amy Brand**
- She recommended opt-in, short-term AI deals renegotiated every couple of years, with retrieval preferred over pre-training.
- She warned that AI deals are concentrating among the largest publishers, leaving university presses and societies behind. Coalition terms should include a named share for reviewers.

**David Krakauer**
- He argued that the whole design counts impact, not judgment, and that tying money to the count bends judgment toward the predicted count. He backs "measure finely, pay out coarsely" only because it keeps the payout away from the page.
- He said a marketplace that pays for summaries erodes the competence of the people who could catch the tool's errors, shrinking its own supply of good sources.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- He said Tim's retraction check catches only errors in the source, not errors the system introduces. He suggested automated reference checking may be part of the fix.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- She reported that cheaper, free tiers gave poorer advice, so poorer researchers inherit more of the errors.
- She said models prefer content written by models, which biases what gets retrieved and cited.

**Adam Becker**
- He rejected Nick's term "quasi-enclosure" as too polite. He said the systems were built by taking copyrighted work without permission, and a marketplace set up afterward turns that into a starting point everyone is supposed to treat as neutral.
- He cited OpenAI researchers showing these systems will always make factual errors, and said telemetry that counts only successes is an advertisement. He asked that the room state plainly who holds the gains now.

**Adam Bly**
- He disclosed that his company is a buyer in Nick's market. He conceded that users of System cannot currently flag an error and watch it get fixed in public.
