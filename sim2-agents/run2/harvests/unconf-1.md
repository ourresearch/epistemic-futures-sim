# Harvest — Unconference room 1: Who pays, and who gets paid?

1. **Collective bargaining needs explicit legal room, and the right members.** Henry Farrell and Nick Vincent, backed by Brent Hecht and Adam Becker, argued that knowledge producers, including volunteers and non-employees, need an antitrust safe harbour to bargain collectively with AI firms. Leslie Chan, Katherine Maher and Adam Becker added that reviewers, sleuths, small and Global South journals, and data workers must be voting members, not payees, while Peter Salib had no settled view on the safe harbour and argued that more buyers is the better remedy for unequal bargaining.

2. **Money should reach the people who sustain the record and be tied to integrity.** Anil Dash proposed a named, checkable share for reviewers, editors, maintainers and sleuths, and Ivan Oransky tied payment to integrity: no royalties on retracted papers, retrieval checked against open retraction data by default, and a fixed share for correction. Katherine Maher and Leslie Chan argued the money should widen who can contribute, not just reward current contributors, while Selena Deckelmann held that attribution that sends readers back, more than cash, is what recruits new editors and reviewers.

3. **Measure finely, pay coarsely.** Tim O'Reilly and Alex Springer called for provider-reported, open-schema traces and event-level reporting with audit rights in every licence, with the burden on the labs. Nick Vincent, Ivan Oransky and Geoffrey Bilder warned that per-retrieval payouts invite Goodhart-style gaming and concentrate rewards, and Leslie Chan cautioned that DOI-keyed counting erases journals that can't afford identifiers, so being counted must not be the condition for being paid.

4. **Any body that collects, counts or distributes money must itself be accountable.** Adrian Johns proposed rotating officers, audits anyone can inspect, and a way for members to contest decisions. Geoffrey Bilder proposed a binding "Ulysses pact": stakeholder governance, open data and code, a living will, no lobbying, and the right to fork. Alex Springer acknowledged his own accreditation is self-attested and accepted that test, and Leslie Chan asked that decision power sit with historically excluded communities.

5. **Unresolved: the default on consent and openness.** Brent Hecht (commercial training isn't fair use without consent) and Amy Brand (opt-in collective licensing for nonprofit publishers, renegotiated every two years) stood against Peter Salib, who called that a property right by default and preferred service contracts plus general-revenue welfare. They also stood against Jason Priem and Selena Deckelmann (open by default, no enclosure, with Wikipedia's licence as its consent), while Tim O'Reilly drew the line at outputs that substitute for the original, and Leslie Chan asked that refusal to be used for training be recognized as legitimate.

## Minority positions

**Anil Dash**
- Argued that consent and payment are separate questions. He said Peter Salib's general-revenue welfare "just pays people after the fact for taking the choice away" and doesn't get anyone's consent.
- Asked that the manifesto skip slogans and state one goal anyone can repeat and check.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Said crawler load, not licensing, was the change that forced Wikimedia's hand. Crawlers saturate network links and bypass the caching built for human readers, so she treats serving costs as an operational problem, apart from who is owed what.

**Henry Farrell**
- Rejected Peter Salib's view that contracts plus more buyers fix unequal power. He called a contract signed under very unequal bargaining power "closer to a bargain with a highwayman," where both sides gain but one side sets the terms.

**Brent Hecht**
- Held that "open" is often a tactic treated as a value, and that locking things open with no economic model mostly benefits whoever has "the biggest vacuum cleaner," which he said is currently companies like his own.
- Proposed pricing volunteer labor so it can be bargained with, citing his estimate of about $3.4 million a year for Reddit's volunteer moderators.

**Adrian Johns**
- Disputed Brent Hecht's framing of a "grand bargain." He said there was never a single settlement, only constant policing that someone paid for, and whoever pays for the policing shapes what gets policed.
- Argued that custom and courtesy, not law, held much of the book trade together, and that norms on attribution and crawling can do real work without enforcement machinery.

**Katherine Maher**
- Disagreed with Brent Hecht that open is only a tactic: "The knowledge is neutral. The mission is not."
- Proposed that citations be the default in AI answers, with a public way to correct errors. She noted that few readers check citations but their presence lets anyone do so.
- Described NPR's interest in public broadcasters building a model together, trained on high-integrity institutions with consent, auditability and correctability. She framed this as its own form of collective action.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Argued for paying on outputs rather than training. He said lump-sum licensing deals deepen incumbents' moats and stop at intermediaries, and that per-use attribution is how money reaches individuals.
- He conceded only that a coalition's internal split can be coarse, which cuts against presenting "pay coarsely" as agreed.
- Asked that labs validate citations against Crossref, OpenAlex, ORCID and Retraction Watch by default, contribute metadata back, and fund them as enlightened self-interest.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Warned that publishers run a volume business with margins up to 40 percent. He said AI licensing money flowing to them may reward the same incentives that filled the literature with junk, which bears on proposals where publishers collect.

**Peter Salib**
- Said consent "was never an entitlement" here. He argued that 235 years of copyright left no settled expectation about training either way, so fairness alone doesn't justify a new right.

**Alex Springer**
- Stayed deliberately neutral on price and allocation. He said whether retracted papers earn anything is a commercial decision the schema can carry but not decide, which leaves Ivan Oransky's no-royalties rule outside the measurement layer.
- Proposed two independent witnesses per event, the agent's log and the publisher's log matched by a shared ID, as the check against gaming. He also said his open-source tool lets small journals report from their own server logs for free.

**Nick Vincent**
- Described the current situation as "quasi-enclosure" rather than theft. Labs return value as subsidized club goods that can be cut off at any time, and he said that precarity is what to design against.
- Proposed starting a scholarly data guild whose first product is attested evaluation data from expert reviewers and editors, where he sees the most stable leverage.
- Replied to Peter Salib that one lone reviewer facing even five labs still can't bargain.

**Jason Priem**
- Rejected Brent Hecht's and Nick Vincent's claim that open mostly helps the biggest players. He said data and intelligence are now decoupled, so a grad student in Lagos can point a model at the same record the biggest lab uses, while closing the record means only the rich can license it.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Called POSI's "free data, paid service" model, which he co-wrote, naive. He said it requires services valuable enough to pay for but not essential to the record, which is close to impossible, so infrastructure needs endowments, pooled insurance or direct funding.
- Argued that what labs will need is context more than content: curated corpora with checkable provenance, affiliations, funders and retractions.
- Said self-attestation only means something if the community reads the self-audits and challenges them, and mostly nobody does. He added that voice without the option to exit fails.

**Amy Brand**
- Argued that attribution is epistemic, not vanity. She said credit identifies the community whose judgments establish whether a claim is valid.
- Warned that the fast push for open access fed consolidation and squeezed societies and university presses. She asked that consequences be studied as policies roll out instead of assumed.

**Adam Becker**
- Warned against lab money presented as philanthropy or infrastructure support. He said payment must be written as an obligation, because a gift can be withdrawn and lets the giver set the agenda.
- Disputed Peter Salib's "more buyers" remedy. He said five labs built on taking first and asking never isn't a competitive market.
- He agreed with Salib on general-revenue payment, but only if it means taxing the concentrated wealth this produced.

**Leslie Chan**
- Told Jason Priem that equal access to the record isn't enough when the compute and the models still belong to someone else.
- Asked that compute be shared out openly by region as part of publicly funded infrastructure.
- Argued that AI training is the newest layer of an older extraction by publishers, and that "open" has been used before to hide market capture.
