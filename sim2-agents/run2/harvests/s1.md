# Harvest — Session 1: The Epistemic Landscape

1. **Homogenization, not noise, is the central risk, and the narrowing started before AI.** Farrell named a "centripetal pull" toward the common case; Chan, Priem, and Cukier traced it to an already narrow Anglophone journal record that the models inherited; and Evans's data on 41 million papers showed that AI boosts individual scientists while shrinking the range of science. Weinberger and Agüera y Arcas held that the flattening comes from product design, not from machine learning itself, while Banaji warned that alignment can make outputs look unbiased while biased associations still drive behavior.

2. **The models depend on a human knowledge commons that they are drawing down without putting anything back.** Farrell ("obligate complement"), Deckelmann (falling Wikipedia pageviews and crawler load), Vincent ("quasi-enclosure"), Hecht ("paradox of reuse"), and O'Reilly and Springer (uncited retrieval) described the same loop, and O'Reilly, Springer, and Brand argued that citation and attribution are design choices, not technical limits. Priem and Bilder saw open, forkable data as the defense against enclosure, though Bilder noted that the open record is mostly US-hosted and depends on political funding.

3. **Cheap fluent text breaks costly signals, but the room split on whether those signals deserve saving.** Farrell called the breakage structural; Chan and Vincent said the signals always also encoded English fluency and elite affiliation; and Priem would let them die in favor of verifiable artifacts. Oransky and Johns countered that someone still has to do the policing and pay for it, and Oransky and Bilder located the real lever upstream, with funders and hiring committees who count papers.

4. **Atrophy stays in scope, but who acts on it is unresolved.** Krakauer, Turkle, Gendler, Sloman, and Miller argued the harm is real (skill decay, lost reading, habit formation, the knowledge illusion, the automation paradox), and Krakauer and Miller called for building competence before introducing tools. O'Reilly, Weinberger, and Agüera y Arcas dissented, citing writing and collective offloading. The open question is who enforces sequencing: Shaub proposed funders, and Miller proposed school boards, governors, and graduate programs. Gendler, Sloman, and Salib warned that awareness campaigns fail unless incentives, community cues, or prices change.

5. **The room did not agree on what these systems are, and that affects the diagnosis.** Farrell's "cultural technology" was challenged from several directions. Agüera y Arcas and Salib warned of underestimation, Bratton called the framing "sociomorphism," and Alvarado proposed "epistemic technologies" whose defining problem is opacity about error. Becker argued the systems cannot tell true from false and that much of what the room is reacting to is hype, and Caulfield disputed him. Dash and Dyson separated the technology from forced adoption and the business models it is being used to scale.

## Minority positions

**Henry Farrell**
Farrell proposed replacing the question of whether brains are rotting with which judgment-bearing roles are being hollowed out and whether publics are being malformed. He also said he worries less about recursive model collapse than about homogenization. Krakauer rejected the choice between roles and brains.

**Leslie Chan**
Chan said the same few firms own the journals, citation databases, rankings and analytics, and that AI training is the newest layer extracted from that stack. For much of the world, she said, the system was not cracking because it had never worked.

**Selena Deckelmann**
Deckelmann filed predictions of Wikipedia's death under panic: "the strain is real, but it isn't destiny." She noted that the decline in English Wikipedia admins predates ChatGPT.

**Katherine Maher**
Maher warned that most of the world is more optimistic about AI than the room, and that a downside-only diagnosis costs credibility. She also said public-interest information institutions are under direct political and financial attack now.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
Pomerantsev framed cognitive surrender as civic, not neurological: autocrats' "censorship through noise" aims to make people give up on truth, and networks now seed training data so chatbots repeat their content. He argued better signals of rigor won't win back people whose belief follows belonging.

**Sherry Turkle**
Turkle held that the first question should be *whether* to use these tools, not just how. Shaub called "whether" weak as a general stance, and Dash said it has already been decided for most people.

**Nick Vincent**
Vincent said value still flows back to the public, but as subsidized club goods the owner can cut off at will. He framed atrophy as a question of where contribution energy goes.

**David Weinberger**
Weinberger called the technology an epistemic gift, because these systems, unlike Socrates' text, can be questioned and respond to the particular reader. He proposed grading students' dialogues with the tool. Krakauer countered that probing requires prior competence.

**Jason Priem**
Priem expects a "Cambrian explosion" of tools on the open record, not homogenization. He called the journal system itself the monoculture and its breakdown no tragedy.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
Agüera y Arcas rejected the lossy-summary picture: models sharpen degraded input rather than store a blurry copy. He said accuracy rises with diversity of perspective, which can be measured and designed for. Banaji warned that surface diversity is the wrong measure.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
Bilder named "quiet contamination" as the genuinely new harm: subtly buggy data and code seeping everywhere, which will take a decade to clean up without these tools. He disputed that crawler overload is deliberate, attributing it to badly behaved and vibe-coded clients.

**Amy Brand**
Brand argued open-access policy produced unintended consolidation, and that "default to open" risks repeating this with training data. She reported that only 3% of about six thousand surveyed authors supported unregulated training use of their work.

**Tui Shaub**
Shaub named the loss of serendipity, the accidental encounter, as the cost of frictionless answers. She framed it as a design brief to build error and wandering back in.

**Ramón Alvarado**
Alvarado argued that a human's final say must be justified on epistemic grounds, not assumed. He flagged "but ChatGPT said…" as a harm to knowers, with administrators using analytics to override experts.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
Banaji reported that model bias exceeds that of the training data, includes new biases such as a preference for machine-written text, and in many cases worsens with each generation. In her hiring studies, people absorbed a moderately biased system's preferences almost exactly, feeding the next model's training data.

**Adam Becker**
Becker argued that "it's here to stay" lets builders off the hook. He reported that crackpot physics emails have become more fluent with no better physics. Dash replied that "whether" has already been decided for most users.

**Adam Bly**
Bly said watermarks miss the point, because machine authorship is a separate question from credibility. What is needed, he argued, is a claim-level audit trail to data, methods and population, and recording null results before any tool. Oransky cautioned that the "retraction-corrected" literature Bly would build on is largely uncorrected.

**Benjamin Bratton**
Bratton said some model departure from training culture is "epistemic overhang" that institutions can't yet absorb, and that alignment must run both ways. He argued much of the "breakage" is scarce expertise reaching new people, a gain for most and a loss for incumbents.

**Mike Caulfield**
Caulfield said model errors cluster in learnable situations, like radar clutter, and are not uniformly hallucination. He added that cognitive surrender sometimes shows the work was meaningless to begin with.

**Kenneth Cukier**
Cukier asked that "mirror" critiques be limited to language models reading text, not deep learning on data, citing retina scans revealing heart-disease signals. He asked who will play the post-Gutenberg journalist's intermediary role.

**Anil Dash**
Dash said much "disruption" is coercion: mandated, default-on adoption that can be reversed. He argued tools people must be forced to use are suspect.

**Esther Dyson**
Dyson worried less about machines thinking like humans than about people starting to think like LLMs: fluent, quick and without understanding.

**James Evans**
Evans found that the share of papers reporting no null claims rose sharply after ChatGPT. He also found that older reviewers were already more likely to criticize disruptive work, so a human in the loop made of senior gatekeepers could lock in narrowing.

**Tamar Gendler**
Gendler resisted a sharp line between machines that mimic and humans who know, likening the brain to ChatGPT in doing what is most likely given prior behavior.

**Brent Hecht**
Hecht argued the current paradigm undermines itself: if contributors lose their reason to contribute, models lose their supply. He said the gap between value creation and value capture is a legitimacy crisis, not just a market failure.

**Adrian Johns**
Johns said there was no golden age of costly signals. Print became credible only through decades of community policing, and "crises of literacy" have recurred every decade since 1920. He asked who would police any new intermediary.

**Kara Miller**
Miller added a pipeline problem: young-worker unemployment rose from about 6 to over 9 percent in two years, and mentorship was already collapsing. She said "whether" has not been decided in schools.

**Tim O'Reilly**
O'Reilly argued the market is early and still open, so the question is whether protocols, telemetry and payment flows get built before lock-in. He called funding scholarly infrastructure enlightened self-interest for the labs.

**Ivan Oransky**
Oransky estimated about one paper in fifty meets retraction criteria, far above the actual retraction rate. He called spending on AI detection "flood insurance" and argued for paying reviewers rather than scrapping peer review.

**Peter Salib**
Salib named manufactured consensus via nearly free astroturfing as the nearest epistemic harm, affecting peer review and public comment as well as feeds. He proposed default proof of personhood and a Pigouvian tax on bot participation.

**Steven Sloman**
Sloman argued that outsourcing thought is how cognition normally works and not a pathology in itself. He located the damage in the knowledge illusion and in lost oversight skill rather than lost memory.

**Alex Springer**
Springer reported that publishers are blocking the wrong bots because no honest bot taxonomy exists, and that robots.txt isn't a licensing tool. He cautioned that measurement must accept plain URLs and server logs, not only DOIs, and that content written to please retrievers remains an unsolved payout-design problem.
