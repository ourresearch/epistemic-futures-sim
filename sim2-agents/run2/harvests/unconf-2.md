# Harvest — Unconference room 2: The role of knowledge in the age of LLMs

1. **Keep disagreement visible.** Cukier proposed the manifesto line "design these systems to keep disagreement visible, never to collapse it into one confident answer." Evans, Sloman, Weinberger, Agüera y Arcas, Alvarado, Banaji, Bly and Bratton signed it, and Caulfield's call to show named, sourced positions where experts disagree was cited as its practical form. Most signers attached amendments:
   - Evans and Agüera y Arcas said diversity must be deliberately designed and measured.
   - Sloman said diversity helps only when it gives access to real expertise.
   - Weinberger said it means differences held in connection, not an average or a menu.
   - Alvarado said it helps little if every model fails the same way for unseen reasons.
   - Banaji said it must be measured behaviorally, not from stated outputs.
   - Bly said it becomes computable only when the unit is the finding.
   - Bratton said open weights are needed to keep it plural.

2. **No answers from nowhere; trace claims to independent evidence.** Caulfield argued that systems should route people to named, sourced human knowledge they can leave the tool to check. Bly argued that claims should be machine-readable and carry population, effect size, retraction status and a measure of agreement and gaps. Sloman and Caulfield stressed counting independent sources rather than echoes. Bly conceded Evans's point that extraction from the published record inherits publication bias, so a deliberate record of negative knowledge is a precondition.

3. **Human judgment is not a free fix (unresolved).** Banaji reported that people copy a moderately biased model's preferences, and Weinberger and Alvarado cited cases where adding humans made results worse. Evans argued the added judgment must come from a wider range of people, and Bratton argued that the structure of the role matters more than who fills it. Caulfield holds that judgment is a skill to be trained, while Sloman doubts that general training works and favors tools for fact-checkers and Wikipedia editors.

4. **What these systems are remains contested.** Agüera y Arcas and Bratton rejected "mere reflection," and Agüera y Arcas pointed to internal debate in reasoning traces as deliberation-like. Cukier narrowed his "house of mirrors" claim to language models and text, and Bratton accepted the correction. Weinberger said the models "don't know anything," and Sloman called them models of intuition that don't deliberate. Alvarado proposed dropping "intelligence" and "deliberation" for "epistemic technologies" described operationally, and Banaji warned that a system's self-reports, including reasoning traces, are not evidence of what drives it.

5. **Audit behavior and disclose failure.** Banaji called for independent, versioned behavioral audits, retired models kept available for comparison, and a rule that no model be more biased or homogeneous than its predecessor. Alvarado called for disclosing error provenance and known failure modes, arguing that neither error rates nor explanations of inner workings suffice. Evans warned against letting AI grade AI and calling it verification.

## Minority positions

**Mike Caulfield**
- Argued that the tools should be judged by whether people who learn to use them end up with opinions better grounded in evidence, not by a publication standard of perfect answers.
- Rejected "they always hallucinate" (Weinberger) and "it's all constrained hallucination" (Agüera y Arcas). He argued that hallucinations are not random, like radar clutter, and occur in learnable situations that people can be taught to work around. The harvest does not record this disagreement.
- Warned that sycophancy makes these tools "a justification machine that tells you you've been right all along."
- Said that as polished arguments get cheap, the ability to evaluate them becomes the scarce input and should be paid for, not only taught.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Argued that every information explosion has needed a new intermediary, as the journalist was after Gutenberg, and that an institution of that kind is needed again.
- Said AI delivers the median while excellence is a tail event, so trusted, named sources become worth more, not less.
- Urged discernment rather than cynicism: which tool, for what use, in whose hands. He said technology "makes good people better and bad people worse," and that nothing here is inevitable.
- Answered Banaji's amplification finding by saying it supports "house of mirrors" rather than contradicting it, since distortion is what such a house does. The harvest does not record this exchange.

**James Evans**
- Reported that AI "helps scientists and hurts science." Scientists using it publish three times as many papers and get nearly five times the citations, while the range of topics narrows and scientists engage with each other about 22 percent less.
- Argued for designing for "evaluative trustworthiness, not for trust." In his experiments, people given a single balanced answer offloaded the work, performed worse, and felt better about it.
- Called for "cognitive aliens," AI that differs from us on purpose rather than mimicking the median scientist, aimed at surprise rather than what's typical.

**Steven Sloman**
- Held that relying on AI is not a loss in itself, since relying on others is how human cognition works.
- Located the danger in the illusion of knowledge. People who had just searched the internet bet more on their own performance, did no better, and earned less. He expects LLMs to make that illusion much stronger.
- Read "humans in the loop" as keeping causal deliberation in the loop, something communities do together. In his planning work, feeding in human-built causal models improved decisions.
- Proposed rewarding people who correct themselves and giving visible credit to contributing experts, since credit is a big part of why they contribute.

**David Weinberger**
- Said he is more sanguine than the concept note. He finds it "humbling, even joyous" that machine learning lets particulars speak where human generalizations threw them away.
- Argued that LLMs answer two of Socrates' three complaints about writing: they adapt to the reader and can be questioned. He proposed teaching dialogue with the machine as a literacy, including dialogue about what we actually value.
- Granted that models may hold an internal sense of how likely a statement is to be true, and asked that they be made to show calibrated confidence.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Urged the summit not to put its effort into "fencing off the machine" and instead to build agent institutions with checks and balances. This cuts against containment framings.
- Rejected the "blurry JPEG of the web" description, citing his work with Lupyan on Jabberwockified English. He argued that models have learned the patterns that let them deblur the web, and that pattern-matching is a key part of real intelligence.
- Called monoculture "collapse, a failure to scale," not a product of scaling.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Argued against making "human judgment first" the default. Where people are given the final say, he said, that choice should be justified on epistemic grounds, since instruments often outperform human senses and judgment. This cuts against the room's general framing.
- Argued that a good track record is not enough to justify trust in science, where feedback is slow.
- Noted that "strange error" can arise from ordinary, unmodified inputs, not only adversarial ones, so a map of where errors appear will not cover everything.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Rejected "the training data is us." She argued that models amplify human biases beyond the data and develop biases not in the data at all, such as a preference for machine-written text and biases against novel groups, and that many of these biases worsen with each model generation.
- Described "LLM hypocrisy": alignment teaches models to state unbiased answers while the biased associations remain and drive decisions.
- Warned of a feedback loop in which human decisions shaped by biased models flow back into the next model's training data.
- Reported that models rate their own outputs above other models' outputs.
- Called for a public register of model biases.

**Adam Bly**
- Said clicking through to documents "isn't enough," which qualifies Caulfield's position. The harvest groups the two together.
- Raised the unresolved problem of funding a real commons. He noted that System's public graph is licensed CC BY-NC.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Argued that alignment must run both ways, with culture aligning to what models turn up. He said discoveries institutions can't yet absorb are an "epistemic overhang, not a malfunction."
- Argued that expertise has been artificially scarce, concentrated in about twenty-five global-north cities. Cheap access to it, he said, is a gain for everyone outside them and a loss mainly for places like MIT.
- Identified a second failure mode opposite to monoculture: agents so far apart they cannot agree on what they disagree about. He placed the workable space between the two as a shared decision grammar with world models that really differ.
- Named the geopolitical sealing of models inside US, Chinese and EU stacks as the biggest threat to diversity at planetary scale, one that ends in capture.
