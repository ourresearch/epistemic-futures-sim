# Harvest — Session 3: Knowledge Infrastructure and Tools: Commons, Enclosure, and Sovereignty

1. Adrian Johns asked who guards the guards. Jason Priem answered that CC0 exit (OpenAlex) is the guard, but David Weinberger, Kenneth Cukier and Geoffrey Bilder held that exit without funded maintenance and voice is insufficient, Leslie Chan said exit only works for those with capacity to pick up the fork, and Selena Deckelmann, Tamar Gendler, Steven Sloman, Sherry Turkle and David Krakauer said the correctors and readers the commons depends on cannot be forked. Adam Bly conceded his company's graph fails the exit test and committed to report on its licence and patent by Session 5; Bilder, disagreeing with Cukier, held that a patent alone can make a fork unusable.

2. Benjamin Bratton, Jason Priem and David Weinberger held that withholding the record only makes it disappear. Leslie Chan, Brent Hecht, Amy Brand and Adam Becker disputed this: Chan argued funded refusal is a legitimate outcome and Hecht argued coordinated strikes are real leverage, while Katherine Maher remained unconvinced that withholding beats service. Much of the room converged on Nick Vincent's "open to read, not to train," with these qualifications: Anil Dash separated community consent from indiscriminate blocking, Peter Pomerantsev separated it from state "information sovereignty," Alex Springer said it requires separate controls for training and grounding, and Mike Caulfield said "open to read" must mean free for a human to click through.

3. Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann, Esther Dyson, James Evans and Henry Farrell favored collective payment to maintainers over per-click fees. Evans, Farrell, Peter Pomerantsev and Steven Sloman warned that paying on retrieval pays the flood and proposed allocating by independent sources with a share reserved for the least retrieved, while Tamar Gendler and David Krakauer warned against piece rates and Ivan Oransky warned that money flowing to publishers under volume incentives funds more volume. Peter Salib dissented from any levy in favor of contracting directly for correction work; Nick Vincent proposed a presumptive commons-rent tax that such contracts reduce; Adam Becker said the honest lever is taxing concentrated wealth and that data workers need seats and votes.

5. Unresolved: Kara Miller asked who collects the fee and by when, noting federal cuts to the graduate students and postdocs who do much of the correction work. ## Minority positions

**Adrian Johns**
Commons and enclosure are not opposites: piracy and property were created together, so the open/closed line should be drawn in public through custom and convention, not only code. He also warned that authenticity technology (watermarks, registries, detectors) is often vapourware and that a tool built to track counterfeits can be used to track dissidents.

**Benjamin Bratton**
The enclosure that matters is geopolitical: computation is splitting into sealed American, Chinese and European stacks, and open weights are the commons most worth defending. He opposed per-work licensing and favored a dividend or sovereign wealth fund, and Esther Dyson disagreed that a cash dividend is the right channel.

**Jason Priem**
The correction work universities give Scopus and Web of Science for free should go into the commons, with public edit histories, corrections released as their own open dataset, and published error rates (David Weinberger agreed). He also said he is now less sure that a paying member librarian is an adequate proxy for trust.

**David Weinberger**
An algorithmic insistence on attribution, such as metering at "a nickel per phrase," would be worse than rampant non-attribution: cite by default, never meter. He also held that many imperfect, distributed systems should be kept alive, AI companies included.

**Leslie Chan**
Refusal should not be treated as a bargaining chip. This cuts against Brent Hecht's and Nick Vincent's framing of withholding as strike leverage ("a strike with a budget"). She asked for federated, regionally governed infrastructure (the Redalyc model, Local Contexts labels), with interoperability as a floor, not a ceiling.

**Kenneth Cukier**
Any governing body needs separation of powers, a guaranteed voice for minority views, and a design that lets it shrink or dissolve. He framed funded refusal as a minority position inside a shared system, not a walkout.

**Anil Dash**
Indiscriminate blocking, such as publishers blocking the Internet Archive or Twitter's API shutdown, locks out researchers and archivists while bad actors keep scraping. He called for many small models owned by universities, libraries and regional publics.

**Selena Deckelmann**
Wikipedia's content is free for commercial use including training, and she will not put up a pay-per-article blocker. This cuts against the room's convergence on "open to read, not to train." Her concern is infrastructure load: crawlers saturate network links and defeat caching, so she asked for agents that identify themselves.

**Esther Dyson**
At .ORG the nonprofit label protected nothing, and voice only works if someone pays the people who exercise it. She would direct fee proceeds to teachers and care workers as well as correctors, and offered Onix (contributors keep 70 percent and can leave, with their model deleted) as an exit test for contributors.

**James Evans**
Claims from centralized research communities replicated less often even when many papers agreed, so a single open graph that every lab and model uses can manufacture false consensus. On this view federation is a matter of robustness as well as justice. He also said the commons lacks its negative half (nulls, failed replications, abandoned notebooks), which must be deliberately elicited and paid for.
