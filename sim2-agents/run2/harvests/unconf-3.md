# Harvest — Unconference room 3: Cognitive atrophy: real phenomenon or not?

1. **Atrophy is real, and it is a design outcome, not an accident.** Everyone who spoke treated atrophy as real, and Turkle, Dyson (the business model as "parasite, not predator"), and Shaub argued that frictionless, validating AI is a deliberate design choice that should no longer be called an unintended consequence, while Krakauer added that collapse doesn't require the tool to fail. Shaub and Pomerantsev both said they have no direct data on AI deskilling, and Turkle framed her evidence as clinical and ethnographic.

2. **Competence before reliance, and transparency as the test.** Krakauer argued that competence and reliance form a bistable system in which the order of learning matters and abstaining does not restore lost competence, and that transparency (map vs. GPS), not friction, determines whether a tool can be internalized; Gendler said the habit literature supports the point about order. Shaub paired transparency with room for the unexpected, and Dyson and Pomerantsev extended opacity to money flows, objective functions, and who is shaping what people see, with Pomerantsev framing this as a right to receive information.

3. **Change the situation, collectively, and name who acts.** Gendler, Kara Miller, and Dyson agreed that warnings and willpower are weak and that situational, collective measures work better, citing Miller's "collective trap" that only institutions can break; Turkle proposed device-free "sacred spaces" in private life, and Pomerantsev added public ones funded by a civic media fund through a levy on data-driven advertising. Miller insisted every recommendation name who is responsible for carrying it out (governors, school boards, employers), since with phones "the research was never the bottleneck."

4. **Don't hand deliberation to machines.** Pomerantsev argued that the atrophy that matters for democracy is the loss of agency and of the civic habit of working things out together, which he said takes hold where community spaces have disappeared; he and Turkle both rejected tools like the "Habermas machine" on the grounds that going through the argument yourself is the skill.

5. **Unresolved: therapy and whether friction can be designed in.** Turkle held that in therapy, children's companionship, and grief the first question is whether AI belongs at all, Dyson argued for purpose-built, non-sycophantic therapy bots given the shortage of human therapists, Gendler said the question is what habits the (belief-discordant) feeling of being cared for trains, and Miller said she lacks the evidence to take a side. Shaub maintained that error and serendipity can be deliberately built into tools, while Turkle doubted that interface design can supply the friction of human vulnerability.

## Minority positions

**Sherry Turkle**
- The faculties she sees weakening are relational: listening, especially to people who disagree, tolerating friction, and being alone with one's thoughts. Close reading and sustained reasoning are not the main ones. The empathy decline she cited (Konrath, about 40 percent over twenty years) predates chatbots.
- She said the phrase "human in the loop" already assumes the machine belongs there. She wants that assumption questioned before any design fixes.

**David Krakauer**
- He directly disputed Shaub's detour argument: "You can add as many detours as you like to an opaque tool and still have nothing to internalize." The harvest records Shaub's view and Turkle's doubt about it, but not this disagreement.
- He reframed Turkle's question of whether AI belongs in a setting as partly a question of timing: opaque tools are acceptable once competence is secure. He also said the handover of agency to the tool cannot be undone.

**Tui Shaub**
- She framed her point as "a design question, not a counsel of abstinence," which sits in some tension with device-free spaces as the remedy. She asked that artists and designers be brought in alongside engineers.
- She did not claim atrophy is real in the measurable sense. She described the loss as the incidental detail that fixes an experience in memory, and she said she has no longitudinal data.

**Esther Dyson**
- "Nobody sets out to harm us": in her account the harm is a byproduct of a retention business model. Turkle said the consequences are "intended." The harvest groups the two as making the same design argument.
- She said friction is neither good nor bad in itself, and some of it (booking a flight) should be optimized away. Separately, she warned that people are becoming "context-poor" while models gain context, which leaves people with fluent opinions and little understanding.
- She said asking who profits means building a movement "rather than more committees." That is a different route from Gendler's situational fixes and Miller's institutional ones.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Before lecturing students about their habits, redesign what institutions reward. Students who outsourced problem sets thought they were beating a system that rewards the credential over understanding, and AI makes that worse.
- She doubted that Dyson's "ask who profits" would change behavior much, calling this the G. I. Joe fallacy. She also said explaining that "it's only a machine" won't dispel the feeling of being cared for.

**Kara Miller**
- With AI, act without waiting for the evidence to build up the way it did for phones. This cuts against leaning on the thin-evidence caveats in item 1.
- Atrophy also happens at work: mentorship for young workers is collapsing. She quoted a security chief who fears a generation nobody taught to work, which then gets replaced by AI.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- He qualified the room's view that awareness is weak. Following Sefton Delmer, he argued that noticing in the moment how your mind reacts to fear and manipulation is a real defense. He agreed it fails without community.
- He said the propaganda he studies aims at passivity through doubt and confusion, not belief in any particular lie. He asked that the issue not be framed as individual brain-training.
