### 32. Steven Sloman (floor)

I'd like to push on how we've framed atrophy. Most of what any of us knows was never in our own heads. Ignorance is our natural state, and outsourcing thought to others is how human cognition works. So I don't count handing thinking to a tool as a pathology in itself.

I think the damage shows up in two other places. The first is the knowledge illusion. Having access to information makes people feel they understand, even when they don't. Just having the internet available raises people's sense of understanding, even when they can't use it. James's finding fits that: people given one balanced answer felt more confident and did worse. A fluent answer gives you a feeling of knowledge that no community stands behind.

The second is the automation paradox, and here I'm with David Krakauer. The more reliable the system gets, the more operators check out, and when it fails nobody knows how to recover. That's what happened on Air France 447. What we lose is oversight skill, not memory. So when humans make the loop worse, the answer isn't to drop oversight. We'll need it most when the system fails.

One caution for Tamar and Peter. Individual literacy campaigns have a weak record. Beliefs follow community cues, so the cues are what we have to design.
