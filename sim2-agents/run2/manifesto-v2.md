# Keep Disagreement Visible: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

We met at MIT on September 23–24, 2026, to ask how to sustain human knowledge when fluent text is nearly free. Much was broken before AI: a narrow, mostly Anglophone record, paper-counting funders and hiring committees, and one paper in fifty meeting retraction criteria. Machine learning on measurements of the world finds what no human could. Language models learn from text and deliver the median. They help scientists while narrowing science, drain a knowledge commons, and break the costly signals we used to judge work. The central risk is monoculture. None of this is inevitable. We push back by building many small, open, accountable models run by libraries, universities and communities.

## Principles

**1. Keep disagreement visible; never collapse it into one confident answer.**

Monoculture is structural. Shared substrates and priors produce correlated failure, and paying on retrieval pays the median. People given one balanced answer offloaded the work, did worse, and felt better. Design diversity in and measure it by behavior. It is never an average or a menu. It means named, sourced positions shown where experts disagree. Keep many models, including open-weight ones. Budget a share of any payout for the least-retrieved sources.

**2. Count independent routes to the evidence, not repetitions.**

Credibility comes from a tested procedure, community and lineage, not from the text itself. Five copies of one wire story are one source. Provenance must reach the individual claim, carrying population, effect size, retraction status, agreement and gaps. It should show lineage independence without scoring it. A single graph everyone uses can manufacture false consensus, so federation serves robustness as well as justice. Being counted must never be the condition for being trusted. Counting keyed to DOIs erases journals that can't afford identifiers.

**3. Cite what you read, and link to something a person can actually open.**

Route people to named, sourced human knowledge they can check outside the tool. Few readers check citations, but citations let anyone check. Varying citation rates across models show that missing citations are a design choice. Reward the link back, not time spent inside the answer. Cite by default and never meter attribution by the phrase. Validate citations against open retraction data and let the public correct errors. People have a right to know who built a system, whose objective it serves, and whether it is a bot. Anonymity remains a right. Opacity is a form of censorship.

**4. Record failure and negative knowledge as first-class data.**

A missing retraction looks like a clean record, and proposed measurement schemas record only successes. Report failure events openly by model version: misattributions, fabricated references, corrections flagged and fixed, and who caught each error. Error rates are not enough, because errors cluster and fluent answers sound the same whether true or false. Disclose known failure modes and where errors originate and cluster. Extraction inherits publication bias, so nulls, failed replications and abandoned notebooks must be elicited and paid for. Credit corrections, retractions, replications and checking. Willingness to be corrected can count as a cue only if retracting stops ending careers.

**5. Audit behavior independently and outside the vendor.**

Aligned models state unbiased answers while biased associations drive decisions. Self-reports, reasoning traces and filings are not evidence. Audits must be independent, versioned and behavioral, using rotated indirect tests and access to checkpoints and retired models. No generation may be more biased than the last, and AI may not grade AI. Every public service needs an owner who can be sued and disclosed liability insurance, which state insurance regulators can require now. Human judgment is no free fix: people copied a moderately biased model, and their decisions feed the next one. A person's final say needs epistemic justification, not seniority. When "but ChatGPT said…" overrides the person who checked, that is testimonial injustice. They need a documented way to contest it.

**6. Build competence before reliance, and change the situation rather than lecturing people.**

Atrophy is real, and it is a design outcome. The order of learning matters, and abstaining doesn't restore competence. People who lose the skill can't catch errors, and uncritical humans pass those errors into training. Transparency, a map rather than GPS, decides whether a tool is internalized. Warnings, labels and generic skepticism training are weak. Change the situation: build in moves like tracing a claim to its origin, teach where errors cluster, and blind identity, prestige and fluency cues. Make device-free spaces and restore mentorship. Sequencing must protect competent judgment, not senior gatekeepers. Test it: take the tool away and track whether people catch planted errors and whether confidence outruns competence. No score follows anyone.

**7. Fix upstream the incentives that were broken before AI.**

Publishers run a volume business with margins up to 40 percent, so AI licensing money may reward the incentives that filled the literature with junk. AI deepens students' sense that credentials beat understanding. Stop counting papers: each funder that signed DORA should write the ban into its next grant review, publish the date and report compliance yearly. Universities should publish misconduct reports by default and promote researchers who correct their record. Make retraction deposits a condition of indexing and licensing, with correction within two months. Detection spending is flood insurance. The lever is what we reward.

**8. Measure finely, pay out coarsely, and pay the people who sustain the record.**

Collect fine-grained telemetry on open schemas, with audit rights in every licence and the burden on the labs. Allocate money coarsely through coalitions and a commons backstop. Per-retrieval royalties invite gaming and concentrate rewards. A named, checkable share should reach reviewers, editors, maintainers and sleuths and widen who can contribute. Retracted papers earn no royalties. Payment is an obligation, because a donation can be withdrawn and lets the giver set the agenda. Knowledge producers, volunteers included, need an antitrust safe harbour to bargain collectively. Reviewers, small and Global South journals, and data workers, including moderators and annotators, vote before money moves. Sleuths need legal protection too.

**9. Open to read need not mean open to train, and refusal is legitimate.**

Consent and payment are separate questions. "Open to read" must mean free for a human to click through. Keep separate controls for training and grounding, with grounding revocable and training held to a higher bar. Refusing training is legitimate, not a bargaining chip, and funders should support it with no deal attached. The opposite choice is equally legitimate. Communities whose licences allow training, like Wikipedia, should not be pushed toward gates or pay-per-crawl. Large reusers owe them self-identifying agents, API use instead of cache-defeating crawling, links back, and funding for infrastructure and newcomers. Whether open or closed regimes concentrate content more is disputed. Study both, publish the data, and revise accordingly.

**10. Every body that counts, collects, funds or validates must bind itself in advance.**

Every body proposed here is a police force, and credibility has lasted only with constant policing. Binding in advance helps but doesn't settle who guards the guards. Any bloc, fund, registry or standards body signs a Ulysses pact committing to:

- stakeholder governance
- open data and code
- a living will
- no lobbying
- the right to fork
- rotating officers
- a way for members to contest decisions
- seats for those expected to comply and for excluded communities
- a sunset clause, with the pact renewed on a fixed schedule

Each body publishes a dated annual self-assessment, scrutinized by paid outside reviewers. A lapse is a public warning of possible enclosure. Fund core infrastructure through endowments or a shared insurance pool with a twelve-month contingency fund, not services revenue. Open base layers need patent non-assertion. Every body publishes its funders, who sign the pact too.

**11. Every recommendation names who acts, or is recorded as unowned.**

With phones, the research was never the bottleneck; nobody owned the problem. Every recommendation names who acts: governors and school boards, funders, employers, organized knowledge producers, the labs, state insurance regulators. Where no one has signed up, the item is recorded as unowned, not dropped. Anything needing US federal action is unowned for years. "Open" is not free, and every unowned item is a staffing line. By September 2027, Adam Becker's public report will check each commitment: who, what, by when, and whether it happened. Anything that slipped returns to the unowned column in plain view.

## Noted dissents

**Selena Deckelmann** (Principle 9). Wikipedia's content is free for commercial use, including training, and she will not add a pay-per-article blocker. Her concern is infrastructure load: crawlers saturate links and defeat caching.

**Peter Salib** (Principles 8–9). He opposes an opt-in training-consent default as a new property right. Compensation should come through contracts. He would address cheap, fake consensus with thin identity tracing every agent to a human principal and a Pigouvian tax on bot content.

**Adam Becker** (Principle 8). The systems were built on copyrighted work taken without permission. The obligation falls on those firms, through a public levy or a tax on concentrated wealth, not licensing deals or voluntary funds. He won't sign unless moderators and annotators vote in the coalition.

**Ramón Alvarado** (Principles 5–6). He opposes human judgment as the default. A person's final say must be justified epistemically, since instruments often outperform human senses and judgment.

**Steven Sloman** (Principle 6). Relying on others is how cognition works. The damage lies in the knowledge illusion and lost oversight skill. He favors tools for fact-checkers and Wikipedia editors over general judgment training.

**Tim O'Reilly** (Principle 6). He dissented on atrophy, citing writing and collective offloading.

**David Weinberger** (Principle 6). He dissented on atrophy, calling the technology an epistemic gift: unlike Socrates' text, these systems can be questioned and respond to the particular reader.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas** (Principles 5–6). He dissented on atrophy. A model can earn functional authority through per-version track records, against Caulfield's and Turkle's conduit-not-arbiter view. He urges building agent institutions with checks and balances, not "fencing off the machine."

**Sherry Turkle** (Principle 6). Disclosure doesn't make a false relationship real. Systems should not say "I" or simulate emotion, starting with those children use. Ask first whether AI belongs. Therapy, children's companionship and grief should stay AI-free until someone shows it belongs.

**Benjamin Bratton** (Principles 5, 9). Models can earn functional authority through per-version track records. The likeliest capture is geopolitical: sealed American, Chinese and European stacks. The scholarly record should default to open to read and train, with refusal respected and reciprocity owed. He prefers a dividend or sovereign wealth fund to per-work licensing.

**Adam Bly** (Principle 2). A provenance graph extracted from the indexed record inherits its blind spots and missing nulls. A single graph everyone queries becomes a hub, or validator.

**Leslie Chan** (Principle 2). "Don't optimize for legibility. Change the regime." Making checking legible on the existing identifier and index stack leaves open who decides whose position counts. Equal access isn't enough while the compute and models belong to someone else.

**Katherine Maher** (Preamble). Most of the world is more hopeful about AI than the room was, and a downside-only diagnosis costs credibility. Name what we are for: consent-trained systems on edited, cited, human-curated knowledge, including small public-interest models from trusted institutions.
