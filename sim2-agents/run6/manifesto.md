# Somebody Maintains It: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

Human knowledge is kept alive by people who check, correct, review, maintain and teach. Much of what strains it predates AI: systems that count papers instead of checking them, and prestige nobody earned. For much of the Global South, the system never worked, and part of the public has already left. Belief follows belonging, and floods aim to exhaust, not persuade. Two things are newly breaking: the link between answer engines and their sources, and the pipeline that trains the next checkers. AI speeds up old failures because plausible text costs almost nothing. As James Evans put it, AI helps scientists and hurts science. Its gains, like finding heart disease in retinal scans, maintain nothing. In short: makers decide how their work is used, every citation links to a source anyone can read, labs pay for what they depend on, and many small stewards hold it. Free to read, paid to maintain.

## Principles

**1. The oldest failure is ours: stop counting papers and citations.**

We count papers because we pay for papers, and AI makes that counting run faster. Roughly one paper in fifty meets retraction criteria, and fewer than two in a thousand are retracted. Catching bad work just before publication is close to unwinnable, because anything trained to detect a pattern can be trained to avoid it. So funders, universities and promotion committees must stop counting papers and citations in hiring, promotion and funding. Rankings must stop counting in funding and policy decisions. Signing DORA doesn't count. Enforcing it does. Evaluation has to be decoupled from publication. Until that happens, everything else in this manifesto is whack-a-mole.

**2. Write the rules around roles, stakes and the power to overrule, not around what these systems are.**

Govern the role, not what these systems are. Any judging role needs:

- real stakes;
- independence in models, funders and ownership;
- the power to overrule;
- rotating wardens;
- independent behavioural audits, using indirect measures and never self-report, of whether its humans can work unaided or drift toward the machine, with retired models kept for auditors and no consequences before a measure is validated;
- where possible, an arbiter indifferent to who produced the work.

A method can't be sued, so a named person or institution answers for every trusted claim. Public answer engines carry disclosed liability insurance. Provenance, failure classes and correction status travel with answers, but they are not warrant. "But the system said" never overrules an unheard nurse, caseworker or expert. That discounting is itself an epistemic injustice.

**3. The checkers are the scarce resource. Pay them from a pool, never per act, never by volume.**

Reviewers, sleuths, Wikipedia patrollers, stewards of null results and apprentices keep the record honest, unpaid, for firms that profit. Sleuths get lawsuits in return. Those who profit from volume pay for repair. Publishers fund the pool, labs contribute, and neutral infrastructure like Crossref holds it. Payers never decide who is paid or what counts as a catch. Never pay per catch, per retrieval or per grounding, and never score people by volume. Test even pooled payment first. Correcting the record earns named credit, not a lost career. Journals act on legitimate concerns within two months, and universities publish misconduct reports by default. Verification is its own science: humans and machines audit code and data together, panels rotate across career stages, and blind trials test whether reviewers penalize novelty.

**4. Competence before reliance. Waiting is not neutral.**

Outsourcing is not the danger. The dangers are the knowledge illusion, mistaking a tool's access for our understanding, and the automation paradox: a tool that works well enough lets the skill needed when it fails fall away. Waiting is not neutral. Order matters. Build competence first and bring the tool in second, so its output is rebuilt and checked. Transparent tools like maps can come early, and opaque models only once competence is secure. Teach people to verify with tools, not to avoid them, because refusal is not a skill. Employers budget mentorship time, and juniors judge before seeing the answer. Credentials certify capacity that survives without the tool. Test every curriculum. Fund a study of collapse and recovery thresholds alongside mentorship, including whether arguing with models builds judgment that lasts.

**5. Independence is the missing safeguard. Ten reviews from one distribution are one opinion said ten times.**

When one model family fills every judging role, errors correlate and consensus is manufactured. That happens by accident, or through corpus flooding and bots producing unlimited unique opinions. AI-using scientists gain citations while science's topic range narrows, and nulls drop out of abstracts. So trust signals count independent sources, not echoes. Measure concentration in fields and corpora. Different models from the same few labs are not independence, and a fork keeps copies, not competence. Build a negative-knowledge commons of nulls, failed replications, registered reports and dead-end notebooks. Draw these out with regional communities, beyond English and beyond publishing, and pay from the pool, never per notebook. "Checked and null" is its own value. No concentration of intelligence, human or machine, should regulate itself.

**6. Blank is not clean. Design the situation, not just the label.**

"No correction found" often means nobody looked, so every tool shows "not checked" as its own value. A citation opens to the exact passage it supports, in a record readable without an institutional login. Answers are maps of named sources and objections, not verdicts. Checking means leaving the system. Labels don't switch off trust in fluent answers, so change the situation. Judge before seeing the machine's answer. Pilot explain-back prompts that ask readers to say in their own words why a claim holds, and measure whether calibration improves. Institutions asking for public trust keep audience-facing work written and checked by named people. Machine output reaching an audience, such as translation, needs consent from contributors and audience. Disclosure is not consent. No count ranks a person.

**7. Free to read, paid to maintain. Those who depend on the commons pay for it.**

Answer engines broke the old bargain. Labs pay for their load and for the commons they depend on. These are disclosed obligations in their filings, not withdrawable patronage. Paying buys no seat. One route is a presumptive commons-rent tax, reducible with licences audited by someone the labs don't pay, and disbursed coarsely to collectives. Routing, and who counts as a community, are undesigned. No pool pays out, and no standard, identifier, correction field or meter becomes a condition of funding or visibility, until SciELO, AmeliCA, Redalyc, African and Asian networks and Southern public funders are invited as paid co-authors with power to amend or refuse. Declining costs no visibility. The money funds people and reading: checkers, mentors, libraries, and civic places where people argue with people.

**8. Organize before you meter.**

A few buyers facing scattered producers is monopsony. Without collective bargaining, every pool and meter becomes patronage. Producers bargain jointly under an antitrust safe harbour, with rotating wardens, and fund repair first. Guilds of reviewers and editors, not gig labour, license the fresh expert judgment labs cannot scrape. Consent belongs to those who made the work. For in-copyright scholarship the default is opt-in, with short licences renegotiated on schedule and checked independently. Consent is exercised collectively, and it binds labs whether the answer is "no" or "yes, but credit us and fund us." An open licence is consent. Honour it with maintenance money and links to the living source, where readers become editors, and have engines publish click-through rates. Never reclose what is open. Training and grounding need separate controls. Charge for load, never for reading.

**9. Every body we create gets a fixed term, rotating wardens and published money flows.**

Every registry, fund, guild, auditor and consortium we propose is also policing, and meters become chokepoints. So design a process, not an organization. Each body has a sunset with no automatic renewal and a living will naming who inherits its data and duties. It stays forkable, with open code, CC0 data dumps and a patent non-assertion covenant. It publishes a scheduled self-audit of what outsiders can verify: governance, finances, whether code runs and dumps load, and closed dependencies. Funders, users and summarized communities are paid to read the audit and publish what they find. An unread or overdue audit counts as failed, and no signatory, Crossref included, is exempt. Each body has rotating wardens, payers in several jurisdictions and published flows. It assumes a hostile state may control it, and it is governed from the start by those it summarizes.

## What we commit to do

- Signatories carry principle 1 to funders and promotion committees. Within 60 days, they publish the named doors and who knocks at each, and they report within a year. Unclaimed doors are marked "unowned."
- Every pool gets named payers, a sunset and published flows before any payout.
- Organizers seat SciELO, AmeliCA, Redalyc, artists and collective knowledge holders, with agenda power, before any standard naming them is final. Organizers also take the apprentice question to philanthropic and public funders. Philanthropy is disclosed and never the sole payer.

## Noted dissents

**Adam Becker.** Disclosing the money doesn't change what it is. Money from the firm whose output you're checking is leverage. The labs are still the only named payer for apprentices. Checkers and apprentices should be paid by several public-interest payers, raised by taxing concentrated wealth. A levy imposed after the fact makes the taking legal. It doesn't make it consensual.

**Benjamin Bratton.** Moving the scholarly record into models and out to eight billion people is one of the greatest knowledge transfers in history, not just extraction. Compensation should come through generative AI: a public wealth fund, not consent gates or royalties. The draft leaves out open weights plus a compute commons. Paying for a commons that only three labs can train on is patronage.

**Adrian Johns.** Nearly everything proposed here is policing. I don't object to that. My worry is that we still haven't said who guards the guards. A self-audit is the guard reporting on himself. Whoever pays holds the warrant. History doesn't let us draw the line between earned authority and gatekeeping as cleanly as the draft does.

**Sherry Turkle.** Before we ask how to keep humans in the loop, we should ask whether AI belongs in some rooms at all: therapy, grief, children's companionship. I would adopt a design standard now: no first-person "I", no simulated feeling, no AI companions sold to children. Transparency does not launder inauthenticity. The money should pay for reading itself, not a new meter of click-throughs.

**Leslie Chan.** The trust layer proposed here adds up to "comply or be invisible," designed in a few Northern cities. The regime should change rather than be optimized. Naming SciELO and Redalyc is not having them present.

**Peter Salib.** You cannot count independent sources until fake ones cost something. Platforms and venues hosting claims must pay for undisclosed bot-generated opinion, with random audits and a fee of cents on each undisclosed automated voice. A training levy creates a new right to charge for what already exists.

**Kenneth Cukier.** He would regulate use and misuse rather than put consent first. He would open telemetry to auditors and public authorities but not necessarily to rivals. He holds that "artificial intelligence" assumes what the room is disputing.

**Tim O'Reilly.** He would allow per-use retrieval royalties to authors once independent sampling confirms the passage supports the answer, flooding flags and correction status are shown, and the meter is audited by someone the payer doesn't fund. Checkers are never paid per catch. Cognitive atrophy is a mentorship design problem.
