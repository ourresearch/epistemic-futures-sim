# Somebody Maintains It: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

Human knowledge is kept alive by people: those who check, correct, review, maintain and teach. Much of what strains it predates AI by decades. We built systems that count papers instead of checking them, reward volume, and trust prestige nobody earned. For much of the Global South, the system never worked. Some things are newly breaking: the link between answer engines and the sources they draw on, and the pipeline that trains the next generation of checkers. AI now speeds up the old failures, because plausible, unvetted text costs almost nothing to produce. As James Evans put it, AI helps scientists and hurts science. We wrote this over two days at MIT. It is not a consensus report. Where we split, we took the majority position and recorded the dissents at the end. Nothing here is free. Somebody maintains it.

## Principles

**1. The oldest failure is ours: stop counting papers and citations.**

We count papers because we pay for papers, and AI makes that counting run faster. Roughly one paper in fifty meets retraction criteria, and fewer than two in a thousand are retracted. Catching bad work just before publication is close to unwinnable, because anything trained to detect a pattern can be trained to avoid it. So funders, universities and promotion committees must stop counting papers and citations in hiring, promotion and funding. Rankings must stop counting in funding and policy decisions. Signing DORA doesn't count. Enforcing it does. Evaluation has to be decoupled from publication. Until that happens, everything else in this manifesto is whack-a-mole.

**2. Write the rules around roles, stakes and the power to overrule, not around what these systems are.**

We did not settle whether these systems know anything, and our rules should not depend on the answer. What we can govern is the role a system plays and what stands behind it. Any role that judges, whether reviewer, auditor, adversary or archivist, must meet five conditions:

- real stakes
- independence in models, funders and ownership
- the power to overrule
- audits of what its humans can do unaided
- rotating wardens

A method can't be sued. For every trusted claim, a named person or institution who can still check the work answers for what was checked. Where opacity can't be removed, govern what can be verified: named sources, open provenance, citation logs, and correction status that travels with every answer.

**3. The checkers are the scarce resource. Pay them from a pool, never per act, never by volume.**

Reviewers, sleuths, Wikipedia patrollers, stewards of null results and apprentices keep the record honest. Today they are an unpaid subsidy to the firms that profit from their work, and the sleuths get harassment and lawsuits in return. Pay them from a pool held by neutral infrastructure, the way Crossref holds the retraction database, and not by the parties being audited. Never pay per catch, per retrieval or per grounding, because that rewards whatever gets retrieved or gamed. Never score people by volume. Pricing individual acts can turn a norm into a transaction, so even pooled payment should be tested first. Correctors get named credit, and correcting the record should earn credit rather than end a career.

**4. Competence before reliance. Waiting is not neutral.**

Capability with the tool can rise while competence without it falls, and nobody notices. Taking the tool away does not bring competence back, and a lost generation is not reversible. We ran this experiment once with phones in classrooms. Other countries acted while America debated, and children paid for the delay. So employers budget protected mentorship time the way they budget compliance. Juniors do the work before the tool and judge before the machine's answer appears. Explain-back prompts go wherever AI helps people read. Credentials certify a capacity that survives without the tool. Judge any tool by what it leaves behind, using sampled audits of unaided competence that are never scored by volume and are governed by the people audited. Every checker was once somebody's intern.

**5. Independence is the missing safeguard. Ten reviews from one distribution are one opinion said ten times.**

When one model family fills every judging role, errors correlate and consensus is manufactured. That can happen by accident, or by design through corpus flooding and bots producing unlimited unique opinions. The pull is toward the centre: AI-using scientists gain citations while science's topic range narrows, and null claims drop out of abstracts. So trust signals should count independent sources, not echoes. Build a negative-knowledge commons of null results, failed replications and dead ends, paid from the pool and never per notebook. Measure concentration in fields and corpora. Different models from the same few labs are not independence. Neither is a fork, which keeps copies, not competence. No concentration of intelligence, human or machine, should regulate itself.

**6. Blank is not clean. Design the situation, not just the label.**

"No correction found" often means nobody looked. Every tool should show "not checked" as its own value, separate from "no correction found." A citation should open to the exact passage it supports, and every answer should be a doorway to its sources and objections, not a verdict. But knowing isn't half the battle. Labels and disclosures don't switch off the automatic feeling of trust in fluent answers. What works is changing the situations in which people meet claims, starting with judging before seeing the machine's answer. Telemetry shows use, not truth. Make no payout on self-reported counts until a separately funded auditor has sampled whether cited passages actually support their sentences, and never let an event count rank a person.

**7. Free to read, paid to maintain. Those who depend on the commons pay for it.**

AI's relationship with scholarly infrastructure is almost entirely extractive. Answer engines broke the old bargain with their sources: pageviews fall, crawlers saturate network links, and attribution goes missing. Labs should pay for the load they create and the commons they depend on. Those payments should be disclosed obligations, listed as line items in their filings, not withdrawable patronage. One route is a presumptive commons-rent tax that labs can reduce toward zero with licences audited by someone they don't pay. Human traffic comes first, and crawlers identify themselves. Attribution links through to a page that opens. Training and grounding need separate consent controls, because robots.txt was never a licensing tool. The money funds people and reading, not only servers: checkers, mentors, teachers, libraries, time for slow reading, and knowledge not yet online.

**8. Organize before you meter.**

Individual producers can't win negotiating alone. Without collective bargaining, every pool, tax and meter becomes patronage that can be cut or redirected. Societies, presses, libraries, Wikimedia, reviewers and public broadcasters should bargain jointly under an antitrust safe harbour, with accountability to their members. Whoever meters AI use will, in practice, define the rights. So event-level reporting must be open, forkable, and checkable by more than the party taking a margin, and nobody who takes a margin on the count may set the measurement currency. Logs are for payment and audit only. Metering keyed to DOIs, English and indexing makes legibility the price of being paid, so no schema should require an identifier.

**9. Every body we create gets a fixed term, rotating wardens and published money flows.**

Commons and enclosure are made together. Every registry, fund, guild, auditor and consortium we propose is also a form of policing, and meters that become chokepoints get repurposed. So design a process, not an organization. Each body must:

- carry a sunset term with no automatic renewal
- have rotating wardens, a named payer and published money flows
- review what else it could be used for, before launch and at each sunset
- assume a hostile state may one day control it
- draw on several payers in several jurisdictions, with critical infrastructure kept redundant outside the US
- be governed by the people it summarizes, not written in a few Northern cities and offered to them afterwards

## What we commit to do

Signatories commit to:

- Carry principle 1 to the funders, provosts and promotion committees who were not in the room. Someone here has to knock on their doors.
- Give the checker pool a named payer, a sunset term and published money flows before anyone pays out.
- Run small pilots with published costs before any mandate.
- Bring SciELO, AmeliCA and Redalyc into governance before drafting, not after.

Commitments made by name at the summit:

- Tim O'Reilly will draft a disclosure standard naming the labs as payer for junior checkers, governed with SciELO and Redalyc from the start.
- Amy Brand will have the MIT Press draft terms for a press and society licensing consortium.
- Alex Springer will build a three-value correction field and a review of other uses into his spec before launch, and will write to SciELO and Redalyc.
- Jason Priem will publish who downloads the OpenAlex snapshot.
- Esther Dyson will push sunset terms and mandatory insurance with disclosed premiums inside the .agent bid she advises.

Apprentice funding has a named payer, the labs, but no guarantor. It stays open until someone says "us."

## Noted dissents

**Leslie Chan.** The trust layer proposed here adds up to "comply or be invisible," designed in a few Northern cities. That layer runs from verified identities and DOI-keyed meters to ORCID-linked credit and correction fields. The regime should change rather than be optimized. Naming SciELO and Redalyc is not having them present. Refusal is a legitimate outcome that deserves funding.

**Adrian Johns.** Nearly everything here is policing, and nobody guards the guards. Registries, watermarks and meters belong to a long history of failed technological fixes for trust. Enforcement systems get repurposed, as with reports that Microsoft's anti-piracy unit was used against dissidents in Russia. The line between earned authority and gatekeeping cannot be drawn cleanly.

**Adam Becker.** A lab paying for plumbing is exercising power even when the payment is disclosed, and billionaire philanthropy is power too. Checkers should be paid from public money raised by taxing concentrated wealth. A levy set up after the fact makes the taking legal but not consensual.

**Anil Dash and Amy Brand.** Consent comes first: opt-in, not opt-out, before any meter or payment. Amy reported that more than 850 authors said they are not against AI but want consent, attribution and compensation. Anil adds that zero compensation is also enclosure, just slower, and that community-controlled alternatives should be built now: "don't wait for Washington."

**Kenneth Cukier.** He would regulate use and misuse rather than put consent first. Against Tim O'Reilly's "anyone can verify," he would open telemetry and revalidation results to auditors and public authorities but not necessarily to rivals. He still holds that the term "artificial intelligence" assumes what the room is disputing.

**Peter Salib.** Pay for missing inputs by contract, and tax coordinated harms rather than use. A training levy creates a new right to charge for what already exists and, like the register, will become property. He conceded that Nick Vincent's commons-rent tax is closer to his position than he had admitted.

**Jason Priem.** Consent layers become toll booths only the largest players can afford. He would get nervous if collective bargaining's chip became withholding knowledge. Machines reasoning over the open record is what the open movement promised.

**Sherry Turkle.** Before asking how to keep humans in the loop, ask whether AI belongs in some rooms at all. She would adopt a design standard now, not after audits: no first-person "I," no simulated feeling, and no AI companions sold to children. "Transparency does not launder inauthenticity." Tui Shaub would test the "I" before banning it.

**Peter Pomerantsev.** Better signals won't win back publics who distrust the messenger. Belief follows identity and belonging, and fact-checking often increased hostility. Credibility is granted by a public, part of which has already left, and even honest failure statistics can be used as proof that no authority exists.

**David Weinberger.** The synthesis counts only damage. Machine learning shows that tangles of particulars can predict where generalizations fail. A system that can be questioned answers some of Socrates' complaints about writing. Humans in the dialogue matter more than humans in the loop.

**Tim O'Reilly.** Cognitive atrophy is a mentorship design problem, not a cognitive one. If AI shrinks your thinking, you may not be taking on hard enough problems.
