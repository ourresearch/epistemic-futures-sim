# Harvest — Session 4: Epistemic Authority — Credibility in an Age of Infinite Scale

1. Earned authority sits where checking is visible, correctable, and carries stakes, not in credentials or seniority. The room pointed to methods another lab can rerun (Banaji, Priem), public edit histories (Deckelmann, Maher), stakes plus track record (Caulfield), access to how a claim can fail (Alvarado), and a record of accepting correction (Sloman). Gatekeeping shows up as certainty, a place where people stop asking (Sloman, Weinberger), and it entrenches over time (Evans, Dyson). Cukier dissented in part: for most people a stopping point is rational, provided correction there is routine and doesn't end the corrector's career.

2. The room did not agree on the line "a model can't be a terminal node." Banaji, Becker, Caulfield, Farrell and Turkle gave reasons for it: self-preference, no stakes or commitment, and errors that drift toward banality. Salib, Agüera y Arcas, Shaub, Alvarado, Bratton and Cukier asked that any manifesto line be written about roles, stakes and the power to overrule, not about what these systems are. Springer and Turkle noted that an agent's liability currently belongs to its operator.

3. Following Gendler's point that alief responds to cues regardless of belief, the room called for designing the situations in which claims are met, not adding labels. Proposals:
   - Humans commit to a judgment before seeing the model's answer (Banaji, Hecht, Deckelmann).
   - Count independent sources, not echoes (Sloman, Johns, Salib, Bly).
   - "Not checked" is shown as a value distinct from "no correction found," because blank reads as clean (Oransky, Springer, Priem, Bilder, Weinberger, Bly).
   - Every answer is a doorway to its sources and objections, not a verdict (Weinberger, Agüera y Arcas, Shaub, Dash).
   
   Krakauer added that checkers should be audited on what they can do with the tool taken away.

4. The room broadly agreed that checkers are currently an unpaid subsidy to the firms that profit from their work (Hecht, O'Reilly) and that the pipeline producing new checkers is shrinking (Gendler, Deckelmann, Miller). Proposals included:
   - Several payers with published flows (Dyson, Maher, Farrell, Miller), against Becker's call for publicly paid checkers.
   - A disclosed line-item obligation on labs (O'Reilly).
   - A firm-level charge or commons-rent tax (Hecht, Vincent).
   - A publisher-funded pooled bounty for sleuths (Oransky).
   
   On Miller's ask for one funder and one institution to pay for junior checkers starting next year, only O'Reilly committed, and only to drafting a disclosure standard. Apprentice funding is unowned, Vincent's routing question (including how to avoid funding the Global North twice) is open, and Oransky and Shaub noted that the funders were not in the room.

5. Two minority positions challenged the room's supply-side framing. Chan warned that the proposed trust layer (verified identities, ORCID-linked credit, correction fields, agent manifests) adds up to a new compliance regime designed from a few Northern cities. He asked that rankings stop counting in funding decisions and that the infrastructure be governed by those it has excluded, such as SciELO, AmeliCA and Redalyc. Pomerantsev, with Shaub and Dash, argued that credibility is granted by a public, part of which has already left or relies on chatbots, and that even honest failure statistics can be used as proof that no authority exists.

## Minority positions

**Tamar Gendler**
- The university bundles research, teaching, socialization and credentialing, and AI pulls hardest on credentialing. The room did not take up her question of which strands must stay coupled, or her point that certification means something only if the capacity it certifies still exists.
- She offered certifying too early, such as deciding elite access at seventeen, as a form of gatekeeping. The harvest does not record it.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Blinding and anonymized review work only in narrow settings. Group membership leaks through indirect cues, and blinding can erase the context needed to judge potential. She asked that the manifesto say so.
- In her hiring experiments, people copied a model's bias almost exactly. She asked that human checkers be audited with indirect measures, not only asked what they did.

**Adam Becker**
- He proposed showing reviewers who funded a work, the way the funding behind Inference was visible only in tax returns, as a cue placed in front of them. He also said money cannot be a terminal node.

**Mike Caulfield**
- He disagreed with Becker's claim that nothing checks a model's output. Model errors cluster in identifiable situations, so a grounded system can route people to someone in a position to know, though it cannot be that person.
- The fix for cue-reading is a move, not better reading: leave the page and ask what the network says about the source.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Against Becker, "publicly paid" is not the only independent model. Wikipedia's checkers are volunteers sustained by recognition and a sense that their work matters. This also cuts against framing volunteer checking mainly as an unpaid subsidy.
- She has been wary of "meritocracy" talk because it hides who could afford to show up.

**Esther Dyson**
- Against Banaji's "methods over persons," a method can't be sued. For every trusted claim, somebody has to be accountable for what they checked.
- She proposed fixed terms for reviewers, auditors, registries and editorial boards, so positions open to challenge and to newly trained people. The harvest records entrenchment but not this proposal.

**James Evans**
- Rotation should go toward younger reviewers and flatter structures, not only toward new ones.
- At current scale humans cannot do enough replication, so AI will be needed in verification (auditing code, data and instrument provenance), though not as sole judge.
- The goal should be evaluative trustworthiness, not trust. People given a single "balanced" answer did worse and felt better.

**Henry Farrell**
- Funding for careful checking needs organized bargaining that can outlast the next legislature, not just several payers.

**Brent Hecht**
- Reviewers should not judge a paper's impacts. They should judge how rigorously the authors disclose them, including what role AI played.

**Adrian Johns**
- The line between earned authority and gatekeeping cannot be drawn cleanly. The Royal Society's imprimatur was both quality control and state licensing, and keeping gates open is as much work as keeping them shut. This cuts against item 1.
- The payer always has a stake, so publishing money flows is necessary but won't settle the question. What made trust at a distance work was constant policing, not the signal.

**Katherine Maher**
- She reframed the question from where authority sits to whom it answers.
- Visible checking is still done by those with leisure time: about 85 percent of Wikipedia editors are men. This cuts against citing public edit histories as earned authority without qualification.
- A citation should open to the exact passage it supports.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Existing services (Crossref, Retraction Watch, OpenAlex, ORCID) already are the terminal nodes, and most AI tools don't run checks against them. Bilder disputed this; see below.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Universities should make misconduct reports public by default.
- Funders should stop counting papers and citations in hiring and promotion. Until they do, trained checkers are bailing water against a volume machine.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Trust rose when journalists explained who they were, how they gathered material and why they did the piece ("honest subjectivity"). Earned authority must also offer people somewhere to belong, not just a verdict.

**Peter Salib**
- Farrell's banality claim is empirical and should be tested with blind novelty-detection trials of models against senior reviewers, with the results published.
- Bot-generated volume should be priced so that manufactured consensus costs those who profit from it.

**Alex Springer**
- In his own system, grounding (what actually entered a model's context) is self-reported by the provider and can't be independently observed, and publisher profiles are self-attested. An independent, separately funded audit should come before anyone pays out on these numbers.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Labeling doesn't break the spell of a fluent first-person answer, and "transparency does not launder inauthenticity." She called for no "I" in the interface. Shaub and Bratton disagreed; see below.
- The next generation of checkers comes from slow reading, so libraries and teachers should be funded alongside apprenticeships.

**Nick Vincent**
- Credit should shift toward terse artifacts that are expensive to fake: raw data, code, and transcripts of AI use.
- Evaluation guilds of reviewers and editors are where terminal nodes have real leverage, because independent expert judgment is getting scarce.

**David Weinberger**
- Credentials are indispensable for scaling settled knowledge.
- Review should "filter forward": rejected material stays findable instead of being filtered out.

**Jason Priem**
- Gatekeeping is a small sample deciding before publication in secret, and he favors publishing first and letting judgment gather in the open. Brand disputed this; see below.
- Signed, public evaluation is the separating signal. He criticized anonymous review, and he retracted the altmetrics claim, saying attention metrics are the same kind of cheap signal.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Against O'Reilly, a DOI shows legitimacy, not quality, and a badge like Crossmark is just another cue. Only the inspectable context underneath has value.
- Most gatekeeping is self-inflicted: we count papers because we pay for papers, so evaluation must be decoupled from publication.

**Amy Brand**
- Fewer than 2 percent of preprints have any review, so publishing first and filtering afterwards only works if someone is paid to filter. This cuts against Priem, Bilder and Weinberger.
- CRediT should extend to reviewing, replication and correction. AI should be recorded as a contributor but never counted as the accountable party.

**David Krakauer**
- Against Evans, rotating toward younger reviewers helps only if they built competence before relying on the tool. Otherwise the room is rotating in dependence.
- The checker pool should never be scored by volume or use.

**Tui Shaub**
- Against Turkle, the first-person "I" should be tested before it is banned.
- Review pipelines should be audited for what they filter out.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Credibility gets redistributed: when "but ChatGPT said" enters a dean's office or a caseworker's file, the fluent system gains standing and the human who checked loses it. He called this epistemic injustice that hits those with the least standing first.
- Delegating to an instrument doesn't make it the source of warrant. Public trust in a forecast is trust in the community that certified the simulation.

**Adam Bly**
- He retracted his own 2023 phrase "corrected for retractions" and said provenance labels are cues too.
- Peer review and checking should be funded collectively from a small slice of global R&D spending, not for commercial gain. He acknowledged his own company's license doesn't meet this standard.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Credentialing has been rationing that keeps expertise concentrated in about twenty-five Northern cities, and spreading it out is a net gain even if incumbents feel it as a loss.
- The fix for banality is plurality: models from different labs and hemispheres set up as adversarial checkers. Dash disagreed; see below.
- Against Turkle, an interface without an "I" is a design choice, not a law.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Framing work (deciding a question is worth asking, knowing when a finding doesn't transfer) stays with people, even where instruments see past human experts.

**Anil Dash**
- Most people using chatbots aren't dupes, and a trust model that amounts to scolding will fail at scale. The doorway should open onto the actual people behind a claim, as a way to rebuild the checker pipeline.
- Against Bratton, a few large labs grading each other isn't independence. What experts can actually audit is open weights on open code.
- These systems are best used to prompt capable humans, not the other way around.
