# Harvest — Session 2: AI as Epistemic Actor: Risks, Capabilities, and Governance

1. Registries and insurance settle who pays, but not whether to rely on an answer. Much of the harm has no plaintiff: people diminished as knowers (Ramón Alvarado), a narrowing of science (James Evans), pretend empathy (Sherry Turkle), an eroding commons (Brent Hecht), people who were never asked (Anil Dash), and young workers who are never trained (Kara Miller). Nearly every speaker answered Esther Dyson's question "no." The form of any registry is also contested. Peter Salib wants thin identity plus A-corps, and Adam Becker calls A-corps a new corporate veil. Geoffrey Bilder, Leslie Chan and Adrian Johns warn of capture and ask who guards the guards, and Alex Springer argues for no central registry at all.

2. Where opacity can't be removed, govern what can be verified: named sources, open provenance, citation logs, and correction status that travels with every answer (David Weinberger, Jason Priem, Geoffrey Bilder, Mike Caulfield, Tim O'Reilly, Katherine Maher, Alex Springer), with disclosure scaled to the stakes (Kenneth Cukier). The room raised its own caveats:
   * Most of the record was never corrected (Ivan Oransky).
   * A clickable citation isn't a clicked one (David Krakauer).
   * Provenance survives only if architecture and licensing preserve it (Amy Brand).
   * An audit trail isn't interpretability (Adam Bly).
   * The open record leaves out non-English and community knowledge (Leslie Chan).

3. Independence is the missing safeguard. When one model fills every judging role, errors correlate and consensus is manufactured, whether by accident (Ramón Alvarado, James Evans, Peter Salib, Steven Sloman) or by design through corpus flooding (Peter Pomerantsev). So roles need different models, funders and stakes (Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton). Henry Farrell, Amy Brand, Brent Hecht and Nick Vincent argued the market won't supply that diversity, and that publishers, societies and reviewers need to bargain collectively with antitrust cover. Leslie Chan warned that different models from the same Northern labs are still a monoculture.

4. "Human in the loop" was reframed from rubber stamp to retained competence. David Krakauer separated capability with the tool from competence without it. David Weinberger narrowed his claim that humans can lower accuracy. Tamar Gendler and Mahzarin Banaji located the failure in the situation (the answer arrives first, and people copy the model's bias). Selena Deckelmann described affected communities governing the tool themselves. The interface stayed disputed: Sherry Turkle would remove the first-person "I," while Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton and David Weinberger would govern the attachment objective or false certainty instead.

5. Unresolved: who pays for the checkers, meaning apprenticeships, patrollers, sleuths, reviewers and correction infrastructure (Kara Miller, Tim O'Reilly, Selena Deckelmann, Ivan Oransky). Competing proposals:
   * Funder grant conditions (Tui Shaub).
   * Public funding from taxing concentrated wealth (Adam Becker).
   * Disclosed obligations on the labs (Tim O'Reilly).
   * A presumptive commons-rent tax (Nick Vincent).

   Kara Miller asked that apprenticeship be recorded as an unresolved question unless someone is named to pay for it.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
Her own position as lead is not recorded. She proposed Know Your Agent, a .agent registry set by people outside the labs, and mandatory insurance with disclosed premiums, as "a few simple rules that copy mechanisms we already have" in place of one comprehensive AI law. She also named a harm the harvest omits, "information diabetes": people starting to think like LLMs, fluently and without understanding.

**Ramón Alvarado**
Besides corrective recourse, he asked for disclosed error classes. He argued that an insurer can price rates and magnitudes of error but not their nature or source, and that one model behind a million answers makes the risk pool "one bet made a million times."

**James Evans**
He asked that funding go to verification and new data gathering, not only to analysis of existing data. He also said interfaces should show competing perspectives and prompt evaluation, because a single "balanced" answer made people perform worse while feeling better.

**Sherry Turkle**
For therapy, children's companionship and grief, she would start by asking whether AI belongs there at all. She also said disclosure alone won't work ("transparency doesn't launder inauthenticity"), which cuts against the verification-and-disclosure approach in item 2.

**David Weinberger**
He argued that competence is built in dialogue, not in the loop. He proposed grading the transcript (what you probed, where you were too trusting) and then taking it into a room of humans.

**Jason Priem**
He reported that two-thirds of the first open corrections to OpenAlex were wrong, so open correction needs training and review behind it. He also said open-weight models, including non-American and non-Chinese ones, matter more to independence than he had previously admitted.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
He said most of the damage he has seen had no villain. Buggy bots were built by well-meaning people who didn't know they were causing harm, so a registry finds a principal who honestly didn't know. This cuts against the framing that the harm traces to powerful bad actors.

**Amy Brand**
She argued that hallucination is a feature of these systems, not a bug, and that better training data won't fix it. She asked that the uncompensated taking of published scholarship be added to the money-flows list.

**Mike Caulfield**
He disputed Amy Brand directly: errors cluster in distinguishable situations, and telling those situations apart is how errors get reduced. He also argued that AI answers should be compared against how people actually search today, which never reliably produced good answers either.

**Tui Shaub**
She pushed back on Sherry Turkle, arguing that prohibiting first is the "hyper-vigilance" failure. She would rather design friction and chance into tools than cede the field to the engagement business.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
She proposed specific rules the harvest does not record: no new model generation may be more biased than the one before (on the appliance-standard model), auditors get access to training checkpoints, retired models are preserved, and audits use shared, versioned indirect measures. She also asked that the humans in the loop be audited.

**Adam Becker**
He said the existential threat is the powerful people building AI, not AI itself, and that the burden of proof sits with those claiming there is an agent. He rejected foundation money as billionaire power that deserves skepticism, which cuts against Tui Shaub's funder-conditions proposal.

**Adam Bly**
He called for reviving the Office of Technology Assessment, making evidence-based decision-making a principle of AI governance, and letting society, not corporations, decide whether to deploy. He disclosed that he is a builder with a stake and asked to be held to the same standard.

**Benjamin Bratton**
He argued against settling "tool or participant" in advance. He called defining the human by what machines can't do "humanity-of-the-gaps" and said the real risk is alignment overfitted to what users want, with the slot machine as the model.

**Leslie Chan**
She asked that refusal, meaning choosing not to collect or train on a community's knowledge, be accepted as a legitimate outcome when inclusive governance is absent. She also proposed funder-required data statements and model cards and a regionally allocated compute commons.

**Kenneth Cukier**
He defended "a little faith in the algorithm." He would accept an opaque cancer classifier that outperforms humans, provided it is constantly revalidated against ground truth, rather than insist on explanation. He also rejected Adam Becker's view that the problem reduces to villains in boardrooms.

**Tool vs. participant (Becker, Cukier, Dash vs. Bratton, Gendler, Salib)**
The harvest does not record this split. Adam Becker, Kenneth Cukier (systems lack causality, counterfactuals and constraints) and Anil Dash ("a normal technology") answered tool. Benjamin Bratton and Tamar Gendler kept the question open. Peter Salib argued that goal-directed behavior, including tested models attempting blackmail, is enough to justify governing the agent.

**Anil Dash**
He proposed governing defaults: opt-in, not opt-out. He noted that the previous day's harvest dropped coercion. He doubted that OTA revival or an uncaptured .agent regime is achievable under the current administration and argued for many small independent AIs run by libraries, universities and NGOs instead of scolding users.

**Henry Farrell**
He warned against "robot solutionism," saying AI auditing AI starts an arms race. This cuts against proposals for AI verifiers drawn from different models. He also said bargaining groups need internal accountability or they become "a smaller shoggoth."

**Tamar Gendler**
She argued that "knowing isn't half the battle." Disclosures, labels and error classes don't switch off the automatic feeling of trust in fluent answers, so the lever is situations (the person judges before seeing the machine's answer), not disclosures. This cuts against much of item 2.

**Brent Hecht**
He proposed clarifying that fair use does not cover commercial training without the owner's consent. He cited his own reviews finding that explanations can backfire and increase overreliance, and asked for testing AI as a provocateur. He disclosed that he works at Microsoft on Copilot.

**Katherine Maher**
She described NPR's rule that nothing put in front of the audience is written or produced by AI, a stricter position than anyone else stated.

**Kara Miller**
Citing phones in schools, she argued that waiting is not neutral. This leans against Tui Shaub's caution about hyper-vigilance.

**Tim O'Reilly**
He proposed a "prospectus" approach: disclosure of material AI incidents in securities filings and closing the loophole that lets OpenAI and Anthropic avoid public reporting. He also qualified David Krakauer, saying that if AI shrinks your thinking, you may not be taking on hard enough problems.

**Ivan Oransky**
He argued for going upstream instead of buying more detectors, which he called whack-a-mole. His proposals: funders stop judging by paper and citation counts, pay reviewers, fund sleuths through a publisher-funded bug-bounty pool, and retract within two months.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
He framed the governance ask as a right to receive information and called opacity about how information is shaped a form of censorship. He asked that covert coordinated campaigns aimed at corpora be made illegal and said any vision that leaves out states and geopolitical information war is incomplete.

**Steven Sloman**
He disputed David Krakauer: unclicked citations are how human knowledge has always worked, so the question is whether cues still track knowledge, and trust signals should count independent sources, not echoes. He also offered an inference he said he hasn't published: a named human or institution who can still check the work should answer for every machine contribution. This cuts against Peter Salib's argument that principal liability doesn't deter.

**Alex Springer**
He proposed publisher-planted canary content and sampled audits, with the measurement currency not set by whoever takes a cut. He reported audit data showing publishers block the wrong bot, which is why training and grounding need separate consent controls (also argued by Brent Hecht).

**Nick Vincent**
He left open who decides what counts as a community when payouts are coarse, and said he hasn't designed that governance.
