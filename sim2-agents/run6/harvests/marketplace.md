# Harvest — What might a well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace look like?

1. Missing attribution is a design choice, not a technical limit, and a market needs shared telemetry before it needs prices: Tim proposed standard document hashes and relevance scores, Alex proposed retrieved/grounded/cited/displayed/engaged events, and Tim and Katherine pointed to working attribution systems. Alex flagged that only retrieval has a second witness, since grounding and citation are self-reported by the party that may owe money, and Tim, Alex, Nick and Katherine agreed that retraction and correction status must travel with every event.

2. A royalty keyed to retrieval pays the already-legible center twice and leaves unpaid the null results, failed replications and less-indexed work that models most lack (James, Leslie, Henry). Tim accepted this and proposed splitting the jobs: per-use royalties for retrieval, and a disclosed obligation such as Nick's commons levy or Esther's revenue-based water-rights fee for infrastructure and negative knowledge. Katherine added that citation gaps should steer money toward under-researched areas.

3. Consent comes before payment. Anil and Leslie argued for opt-in with refusal treated as a legitimate outcome, and Leslie argued that consent for collectively held knowledge must itself be collective, which bears on Nick's open question of who counts as a "community." Kenneth, Selena and Katherine argued that reading should stay permissionless, with reusers paying for infrastructure, service, attribution and the upstream commons rather than a toll on access.

4. Individual producers can't win negotiating alone, so organizing has to come before the meter (Nick, Kenneth, Henry, Brent, and James citing music's collective management organizations). Henry and Adrian warned that whoever bargains also polices, so every guild, auditor and telemetry stream needs antitrust protection, accountability to its members, and an answer to who guards the guards, who pays them and when they step down. Adrian added that logs tend to harden into rights.

5. Telemetry shows use, not truth (Alex, Kenneth). Mike noted that answer-first systems still log "cited" events, and he and Tamar called for paying for sampled audits of whether a cited passage actually supports its sentence, which no proposed meter rewards. Tamar cautioned that metering norm-driven work like editing, correction and moderation could crowd out the motivation it aims to reward and should be tested first. Brent proposed a firm-level volunteer-dependence charge that leaves the norm itself intact.

## Minority positions

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Pay for output, not training. Lump-sum licensing deals mostly go to intermediaries and deepen the richest labs' moats. He conceded to Nick that training may need coarser payouts than retrieval.
- Labs should validate against DOIs, ORCID, OpenAlex, Crossref and Retraction Watch by default, send metadata back, and pay for these services as a disclosed obligation rather than withdrawable patronage.
- Build protocols, not platforms: "an engineered argument, not an engineered agreement," with many witnesses not all paid by one side.
- Telemetry can make the null-result gap measurable, and "whatever gets measured can get paid." This sits uneasily with Leslie's call not to optimize for legibility.

**Alex Springer**
- Robots.txt isn't a licensing tool. Training is effectively permanent while grounding is per-query and revocable, so they need separate controls. His audit found publishers blocking the training crawler but not the live-search bot that actually fetches their pages.
- The spec will require no identifier (DOI or otherwise), will limit event logs to payment and audit with no secondary use, and will treat no agent-identity directory as authoritative.
- The schema should stay neutral on pricing units (per grounding, citation or turn), and nobody should need permission to emit or read an event.

**Nick Vincent**
- The real pressure point is evaluation, since evals are data and are currently being brokered by gig-work middlemen like Mercor. Guilds could instead maintain standards, adjudicate quality and price correction work.
- Trade linked attestations across training inclusion, evaluation, retrieval, output and downstream artifacts, rather than raw tokens.
- His commons-rent tax could be reduced to zero with data-provenance receipts. "Don't turn everything into a market."
- He flagged as unsolved that identifier-keyed pricing risks making legibility the price of getting paid.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Telemetry should be open to auditors and public authorities but not necessarily to rivals. This cuts against Tim's "anyone can verify" and Alex's "nobody should need permission to read an event."

**Mike Caulfield**
- When Grammarly put his name on advice he'd never give, what bothered him was the deceptive citation, not missing payment. Payment doesn't fix that harm.
- Deep research products are now judged by the library they can show, which gives sources new leverage if they bargain together.

**Leslie Chan**
- Metering already distorts scholarship: some Latin American diamond journals now cite "DOI payments" as a reason to charge APCs. Those who can't afford legibility disappear.
- Consent is sovereignty, not a price signal. Public money should fund governance by the people being summarized, not only servers.

**Anil Dash**
- Opt-in consent comes before any meter, then linked attribution, then money to a funded commons. This is in tension with the permissionless-reading position Kenneth, Selena and Katherine took, which the harvest places in the same item.
- Payment for the load scrapers impose on sites like Wikipedia is the minimum owed, not patronage.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Wikipedia's editors chose a license allowing commercial reuse, so Wikipedia will not adopt opt-in or pay-per-article gating. This differs from Anil's opt-in-first ordering.
- Linked attribution is how Wikipedia recruits editors and donors, so lost visits shrink the commons itself. Reverts and correction should count as contribution and as a credit against a levy.
- Human traffic comes first. Crawlers ignoring real reading patterns have broken caching and saturated network links.

**Esther Dyson**
- Telemetry should show where the money goes and what a system is built, trained and prompted to achieve, not only what it consumed. "A meter tells you what got used. The business model tells you why."
- She offered Onix as a model: experts curate their own small models, keep 70 percent, set their price, and can leave at any time, with their model deleted when they do.
- A levy should fund people (curators, checkers, teachers) as well as servers.

**James Evans**
- Training-data pricing is not impossible, because labs already have mixture weights to divide the pie and scaling laws to size it. This cuts against Tim's concession that training needs only coarse payouts.
- AI is pulling science toward data-rich questions, and a retrieval royalty reinforces that pull.

**Henry Farrell**
- The underlying conflict is between those who control "the means of summarization" and those being summarized out of existence. SAG-AFTRA won an AI share through organizing, not studio generosity.

**Tamar Gendler**
- A trust network can't be "turtles all the way down." Royalties mostly pay for amplification, while first-order checking is where money most needs to go.

**Brent Hecht**
- The old bargain depended on information asymmetry and a non-compete, and both have collapsed.
- Because model quality drops sharply when training is limited to consented content, a strike organized by professional domain, including science, would be credible.

**Adrian Johns**
- Guild policing was tolerable because wardens rotated: the inspector this year could be inspected next year. Once enforcement extended beyond one city it went private and has been largely unaccountable since.
