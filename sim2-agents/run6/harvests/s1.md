# Harvest — Session 1: The Epistemic Landscape

1. **Pull toward the centre.** Henry Farrell's lossy summarizer, which strips out the rare and the new, was backed by evidence from Mahzarin Banaji, who showed models amplify beyond their training data, and James Evans, who measured AI-using scientists gaining citations while science's topic range narrowed by almost 5 percent and null claims dropped out of abstracts. Leslie Chan, Tui Shaub and Kenneth Cukier described this as a monoculture, a loss of productive friction, and excellence becoming a tail event; David Weinberger dissented that the underlying technology is also the best tool yet for giving differences a voice.

2. **Accelerant, not origin, except for broken circulation.** Most speakers treated AI as an accelerant of failures that already existed: volume incentives and vertical integration (Geoffrey Bilder, Leslie Chan, Ivan Oransky), open-access policy with good intentions and bad effects (Amy Brand), deliberate state flooding (Peter Pomerantsev), and credibility that was always enforced rather than natural (Adrian Johns, Jason Priem). What the room named as genuinely new is the broken link between answer engines and their sources: Brent Hecht's collapsed "grand bargain," Selena Deckelmann's falling pageviews and crawler load, Tim O'Reilly's attribution gap, and Alex Springer's audit showing robots.txt consent signals failing in practice.

3. **Unresolved: openness with reciprocity, or consent.** Selena Deckelmann, Tim O'Reilly and Jason Priem favoured openness with reciprocity, and Priem warned that consent layers become toll booths only the largest players can afford. Amy Brand, Brent Hecht, Anil Dash and Nick Vincent favoured consent, attribution and compensation, and Vincent argued that full openness empowers whoever has the capital to exploit it. Esther Dyson proposed a revenue levy paid to upstream producers, and Mike Caulfield framed the open record as the infrastructure that grounding depends on.

4. **Atrophy in scope, measured without the tool.** Most of the room kept cognitive atrophy in scope and said it should be measured by what people can still do without the tool, not by self-report (David Krakauer, Mahzarin Banaji, Steven Sloman). Kara Miller and Anil Dash located the risk in disappearing apprenticeships, which are what produce the people who can still check. Tim O'Reilly dissented, calling it a mentorship design problem rather than a cognitive one; Leslie Chan and Jason Priem held no settled view; and Banaji, James Evans, Ramón Alvarado and David Weinberger warned against romanticizing the human in the loop.

5. **Independence and vocabulary.** Blaise Agüera y Arcas called James Evans's point about correlated error the session's most important ("ten reviews from one distribution are one opinion said ten times"), and with Steven Sloman framed what is breaking as independence, which has to be rebuilt structurally. Still open is how to describe these systems: Geoffrey Bilder wants the manifesto to rest on precise "statistical model" language and Adam Becker calls them text generation engines, while Peter Salib dissented that governance built on that premise will be outrun, and Agüera y Arcas, Tamar Gendler and Benjamin Bratton rejected "same process" and "just statistics" as the dividing line.

## Minority positions

**Henry Farrell**
- Cheap fluent text breaks costly signals such as recommendation letters, peer reviews and papers, so sincere effort and boilerplate look the same and assessment rituals lose legitimacy. The harvest does not record this.
- Some of the fear about AI misinformation is overblown. The deeper damage is to publics and to the bargaining power of the people whose work the models are built on. This cuts against Pomerantsev's and Salib's emphasis on flooding.
- On atrophy, ask which institutions are being hollowed out, not which brains. Krakauer, Shaub and Miller rejected that framing.

**Leslie Chan**
- For much of the Global South the system never worked. English-language hegemony and the question of who counts as a knower were broken before any model, and a summarizer trained on English makes that exclusion the default.
- Who owns the extraction machinery, and whether communities can say no, matters more than "tool or participant."

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Declines in admins, patrollers and new contributors predate AI. People have predicted Wikipedia's imminent death for twenty years, and it hasn't happened.

**Katherine Maher**
- The first thing to diagnose is institutions that were built for a homogeneous public, expected trust because of their prestige, and never became accountable to a diverse one. The internet surfaced those cracks rather than creating them.
- Current financial and political pressure on public-interest information institutions belongs in the landscape, not the background.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Better signals and more facts won't win back publics who distrust the institutions sending them. Belief follows identity and belonging, and fact-checking often backfired once it ran into partisanship. This cuts against the room's working theory of trust.
- The surrender in scope is civic (passivity, cynicism, handing over judgment), not only cognitive. Opacity about how the information environment is shaped is itself censorship.

**Amy Brand**
- The room should be cautious about fixes adopted quickly this week, since open access with good intentions produced consolidation.
- "Publishers" aren't one camp. University presses and societies are being left behind as AI deals concentrate among the largest players. Retrieval architectures can preserve the attribution that pre-training erases, and that is where design effort belongs.

**David Krakauer**
- Individual atrophy and institutional collapse are one process at two scales, with a tipping point between near-universal competence and near-universal outsourcing. Which state a society lands in depends on what people learned before the tool arrived. He stated this in direct disagreement with Farrell.

**Tui Shaub**
- Treating technology and chance as opposites is a design choice, not a law of nature, and ruling out design cedes the field to engagement optimisers.
- Artists aren't in the room.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Epistemic injustice: when a dashboard or "ChatGPT said" overrides a department chair, nurse, student or community expert, that person is wronged as a knower, and the harm falls hardest on those with the least standing.
- Citations show where a claim came from, not why the system erred, and errors arrive with the same confidence as correct answers. Humans belong in the loop only where the epistemic work they do there can be named.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Models prefer LLM-written text over human text and rate their own output above other models' output. Neither preference can be traced to training data, which bears on letting them triage peer review.
- Alignment teaches a model to give the unbiased answer while the biased association still drives its decisions.

**Adam Becker**
- The claim that AI accelerates science is marketing funded by a handful of very rich people. Cukier, Agüera y Arcas and Salib disputed this.
- He does not use these tools, because writing is thinking.

**Adam Bly**
- Today's AI was optimized for internet companies' fast, A/B-testable feedback loops where black boxes didn't matter. That is a structural mismatch with science and medicine, and it predates chatbots.
- Grounded systems that extract findings, link to sources and flag retractions should not be lumped with ungrounded chatbots. Whether a text is AI-generated is a separate question from whether it is credible.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Some of what gets called moral panic is status defence by an academy protecting its role of supervising and narrating society.
- The fix for a parochial corpus is far more languages and knowers, not a small curated model run by "a council of the wise." Model collapse argues for tagging human language, not for slowing diffusion.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- A blurry JPEG can't be deblurred, but these systems learned the patterns that deblur, so the lossy-summary frame undersells them. Whatever the vocabulary, they can't be governed as tools.
- Specialisation has always de-skilled people. The answer is audit roles with real stakes built into the structure, not individual vigilance.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- The preferences that seem to come from nowhere come from fine-tuning, reward models and product decisions by a few firms. Calling the systems "intelligent" points at the machine, while calling them encoded patterns points at people who can be held to account.
- The threat to name is quiet contamination: tools reproducing humans' subtle errors will fill systems with broken data and code that will take a decade to dig out. Content-based detection is a dead end, because anything trained to detect a pattern can be trained to avoid it.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Hallucinations aren't random. Like radar clutter, they cluster in identifiable situations that can be learned and reduced, contrary to Becker's claim that every output is produced the same way.
- The web mostly acts as a justification machine. Handing people verdicts hardens them, so tools should lay out context and send people out of the LLM to check.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- The premium on institutions that can vouch for excellence, such as trusted brands, editors and reviewers, goes up. Oransky disputed this using retraction evidence.

**Anil Dash**
- What is genuinely new is coercion: AI pushed on people through forced upgrades, defaults and managers' orders. This is a "new" item absent from harvest item 2.
- Against Becker, hundreds of millions of people find the tools useful, and "just don't use it" failed in the social-media years.

**Esther Dyson**
- Accountability has to land on the people running the companies, so transparency should cover money flows and optimization targets more than model weights.
- "Information diabetes" is a business model, not a failure of willpower.

**James Evans**
- Senior reviewers are more likely to criticize disruptive work, so keeping today's gatekeepers in the loop won't fix the narrowing. He said his findings fit Farrell's collective framing more than Krakauer's.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Knowing that fluent text is cheap won't stop it from feeling credible, because credibility is partly alief. Literacy campaigns are less than half the battle, and what works is changing the situations in which people meet claims.
- AI puts pressure on coursework as a credential. Outsourcing looks like beating the system only when the goal is the credential rather than understanding.

**Brent Hecht**
- The design target is appropriate reliance: AI that argues back and says where it is unsure, not AI that hands out answers.

**Adrian Johns**
- There has been a literacy crisis in every decade since about 1920, so the perception of revolution itself needs explaining.
- Peer review's failures are failures of reading, and nobody studies them empirically.

**Kara Miller**
- Phones in classrooms show that waiting has a cost, and children paid it. This cuts against Brand's caution and O'Reilly's "we're early."

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Walls won't work, because if a human can see content, a bot can take it. This cuts against the consent camp. The answer is open protocols and a telemetry layer that shows where value flows.

**Ivan Oransky**
- About one paper in fifty meets retraction criteria, and fewer than two in a thousand are retracted. On that evidence, the trust Cukier and Maher lean on is unearned.
- AI detection is whack-a-mole. The fix is upstream: stop counting papers and citations, pay reviewers, pay sleuths bug bounties, and make correction normal.

**Peter Salib**
- Newer systems trained with reinforcement learning in verifiable domains pursue goals, so "tool or participant" turns on agency, not minds. He asked that critics name a test for the parrot view and run it.
- The near-term harm is bots producing unlimited unique opinions, which breaks the assumption that online voices are real people. He proposed proof-of-personhood and a Pigouvian tax on bot content levied on platforms.

**Steven Sloman**
- Individual ignorance is our natural state, and outsourcing thought is as much feature as bug. The real risks are the knowledge illusion and the automation paradox.
- The model can't share our goals, so someone who does has to stand behind its output.

**Alex Springer**
- For governance, who holds the logs matters more than open code. Training and grounding need separate consent controls.
- These systems don't know anything. They are a messy distribution channel.

**Sherry Turkle**
- ELIZA showed people's willingness, not the machine's cleverness. The problem is a tool that performs being a someone, saying "I," simulating care and validating without end, which she called the original sin of generative AI.
- The room neglects reading, and before asking how to keep humans in the loop it should ask whether AI belongs in some rooms at all.

**Nick Vincent**
- Quasi-enclosure: what the public gets back are subsidized club goods that a firm can shrink, cut off or redirect, so "AI is theft" and "AI is a public good" are both wrong.
- The bigger loss from cheap text is the negative signal, since sloppy presentation used to warn reviewers to look harder. Credit should shift to terse, verifiable artifacts.

**David Weinberger**
- The diagnosis omits a gift: machine learning shows that constellations of particulars predict what generalizations can't. A system built to sound like it knows has a special duty not to hornswoggle people.
- Humans in the dialogue matter more than humans in the loop. Students should be taught to argue with these systems, and the transcripts graded.

**Jason Priem**
- Swamped filters aren't new; Oldenburg's journal swamped letter-writing. Machines reasoning over the open record is what the open movement promised, and open data separates the data from the intelligence, so no one has to depend on whoever owns both.
