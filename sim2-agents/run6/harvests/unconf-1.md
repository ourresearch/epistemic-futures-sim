# Harvest — Unconference room 1: Who pays, and who gets paid?

1. Most of the room wanted creators paid through contracts, not new copyright, but split on the legal default. Peter Salib argued for contracts for creators, general taxation for displacement, and no extension of copyright to training, and Tim O'Reilly drew the line at outputs that substitute for the original. Brent Hecht argued commercial training should require consent and acknowledged this changes the default, Nick Vincent defended a presumptive commons-rent tax that firms can reduce to zero by licensing, and Adam Becker called for taxing concentrated wealth.

2. Countervailing power has to come before contract design. Nick Vincent, Henry Farrell, Adam Becker and Amy Brand argued that societies, presses, libraries, reviewers and Wikimedia must bargain collectively, or AI deals will concentrate among the largest publishers. Brent Hecht warned that joint bargaining may need antitrust cover, and Adrian Johns noted that historically, guilds that bargained for their members also policed them.

3. Whoever meters AI use will, in practice, define the rights (Adrian Johns). Alex Springer, Tim O'Reilly and Geoffrey Bilder called for event-level reporting that is open, forkable in both code and data, and checkable by more than the party taking a margin, and Johns added appeal rights and limits on other uses. Leslie Chan warned that metering keyed to DOIs, English and indexing makes legibility the price of being paid, and Ivan Oransky asked that retracted work be flagged in telemetry and never earn royalties.

4. Paying per article or per token pays for volume and reinforces publish-or-perish (Ivan Oransky, Geoffrey Bilder, Amy Brand, Leslie Chan, Henry Farrell). Oransky proposed a fixed share of AI licensing money for correcting the record: paying reviewers, bug-bounty payments to sleuths, and open retraction data. Bilder warned against AI money funding closed, expensive integrity tools.

5. Unresolved: whether content should be open for training by default. Jason Priem argued that restrictions create a checkerboard of enclosures and that only services should be charged for, and Selena Deckelmann defended Wikipedia's deliberate openness while asking for reciprocity and required linked attribution. Amy Brand, Anil Dash, Leslie Chan and Nick Vincent held that openness for training should be a choice communities make, and Brent Hecht asked what openness is for if it does not sustain future contributors.

## Minority positions

**Peter Salib**
- Once the labs are the main buyers of novel human ideas, they can simply commission them, and the annotator market is evidence that this route works. Henry Farrell, Katherine Maher and Adam Becker disputed this.
- The industry is not a natural monopoly: fast followers can train a comparable model cheaply, so the buyers of human knowledge will not harden into a single buyer. This is a direct disagreement with Henry Farrell's monopsony concern.
- The obligate-complement argument cuts both ways. If models get good at producing knowledge, the state has less reason to protect the human monopoly.

**Henry Farrell**
- A market that looks competitive can still be a monopsony. On Mechanical Turk, about ten percent of requesters post almost all the tasks. The danger is the present period, in which cheap summaries undercut knowledge production before anything replaces it.

**Adam Becker**
- The paid training-data workforce that Salib cites as the market working is the part to worry about most. Labeling and moderation workers are harmed, and ecological cost is a separate objection to these systems.
- Lab money funding endowments, integrity tools or meters is an exercise of power, not a gift. Any infrastructure the labs fund must disclose who pays for it. This cuts against Tim O'Reilly's call for labs to fund the plumbing.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Training on public text is likely transformative fair use, as with Google Books. This cuts against Brent Hecht's call for consent to commercial training.
- Lump-sum licensing deepens the richest labs' moat, and the money stops with intermediaries. Labs funding Crossref, OpenAlex, ORCID and Retraction Watch is enlightened self-interest, not philanthropy.

**Alex Springer**
- The 1908 precedent he has cited produced a compulsory licence, not a pure contract regime. He told Salib this directly.
- Training and grounding need separate controls, because robots.txt was never a licensing tool. Grounding and citation events have no origin-side witness yet, so for now they depend on provider reporting.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- The POSI principle "revenue based on services, not data," which he co-wrote, was naive. If agents do the reading, the paid API becomes the essential layer where enclosure pressure builds.
- Funders should treat foundational infrastructure like diamond open access, with endowments and a shared insurance pool. The community, not the organisation itself, should review its self-audits.

**Jason Priem**
- He would get nervous if guilds' bargaining chip became withholding knowledge. This cuts against the collective-bargaining emphasis in item 2.
- Open, agent-ready infrastructure should be funded now, or a for-profit will build it and set the norms.

**Nick Vincent**
- He conceded that one group's open data can weaken another group's bargaining position, and that compute-rich firms benefit most.

**Leslie Chan**
- Refusal, meaning a community declining to have its knowledge collected or trained on, is a legitimate outcome that deserves funding.
- He asked Nick Vincent who decides what counts as a "community" for coarse payouts. He also said most publisher AI deals do not pay scholars, and called them data cartels selling researchers' work back to them.

**Anil Dash**
- Zero compensation is also enclosure, just slower. Publishers respond to scrapers with paywalls and by blocking the Internet Archive, which is sometimes the only record of what authoritarians deleted.
- Don't wait on US regulation. Consent should be built into products and contracts now.

**Katherine Maher**
- Public media is already collective and could build its own model trained on public broadcasting, with only human-made work in front of its audience.
- Sustainability must fund knowledge that is not online yet, not only what already exists and can be metered. Selena Deckelmann made a related point about Arabic-language gaps.

**Amy Brand**
- Metadata should be open, and content is a separate question. Open metadata is the counterweight to commercial analytics oligopolies.

**Brent Hecht**
- Volunteer labor can be measured, for example Reddit moderators' work is worth at least 3% of revenue, and that could ground a "volunteer-dependence" assessment. Cheap fast-follower models could make the incentive to fund human knowledge collapse faster, which strengthens his concern rather than answering it.

**Adrian Johns**
- The claim that copyright exists to create incentives is itself a story told by copyright's proponents. Piracy came first, and property was built in response. This challenges the premise shared by Salib and Farrell.
- Enforcers should not be treated simply as villains, since they may be right that the commons depends on them. Meters that become chokepoints get repurposed, as with reports that Microsoft's anti-piracy unit was used against dissidents in Russia.

**Ivan Oransky**
- The people currently guarding the record are unpaid sleuths, and what they get in return is harassment and lawsuits.
