# Harvest — Unconference room 2: The role of knowledge in the age of LLMs

1. The room largely converged on "no answers from nowhere": on contested questions, AI should present named, linkable, competing sources, including disagreement, uncertainty and gaps, rather than one blended verdict (Caulfield, Cukier, Weinberger, Bly). Evans, Sloman, Pomerantsev and Agüera y Arcas qualified this, saying a menu of views is not enough unless the sources are independent rather than drawn from one distribution, and the disagreement runs under shared, transparent norms, as on Wikipedia rather than cable news.

2. Agüera y Arcas and Bratton argued for putting as much effort into institutions for mixed human–AI knowledge work (roles, norms, adversarial review, correction) as into models. Pomerantsev stressed transparent evidence rules and judging spaces by trust and agency rather than engagement, and Caulfield proposed that stakes decide who fills some roles. The room split on where humans belong:
   * Agüera y Arcas (round one) and Pomerantsev ("no machine should reach the consensus for us") kept humans central.
   * Alvarado, Evans and Weinberger said humans belong where there is an epistemic reason.
   * Bratton accepted that test but disputed that a clean human–machine line exists.

3. Sloman proposed judging any authority, human or machine, by whether it can be corrected: it shows its reasoning, says how it could be wrong and changes with the evidence. Bly proposed audit trails back to data, methods and prompts for AI-mediated claims. Alvarado countered that provenance and visible reasoning don't reveal how a system fails, and called for independent vetting that discloses failure modes and error classes, not just track records. Caulfield replied that requiring sources still signals reliability, since hallucinated links went along with worse answers.

4. Several participants argued that the record these tools depend on must be built and protected as public infrastructure:
   * a negative-knowledge commons of null results and dead ends (Evans)
   * a retraction-corrected scholarly record (Bly)
   * an openly linkable record of libraries, Wikipedia and open scholarship, with fair licensing between sources and platforms (Caulfield)
   * many dispersed knowledge systems so that no small group of companies controls access (Weinberger)

   Pomerantsev warned that state networks are already flooding the training corpus. Bratton cautioned against responding with small curated models instead of including more of the world's languages and data.

5. Unresolved: whether LLMs should be called intelligent. Agüera y Arcas and Bratton held that prediction is intelligence. Cukier and Alvarado held that the word assumes what is in dispute, and Weinberger said LLMs don't know anything. Also unresolved: Cukier's concern about atrophy versus Sloman's view that outsourcing thought is normal. Sloman partly conceded, reframing the essay result as the knowledge illusion and proposing explain-back tools as the remedy.

## Minority positions

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Argued that because AI delivers the median and excellence is a tail event, trusted brands and institutions become more valuable, not less. He said the summit needs a new sense-making intermediary, as the journalist was after Gutenberg.
- Warned that text is a small reflection of reality and that the most meaningful things often go unsaid, so a text corpus misses them.

**James Evans**
- Reported that scientists who use AI publish and are cited far more while the range of topics science studies narrows and researchers engage less with each other. His summary: "AI helps scientists and hurts science."
- Said AI should not be designed to maximize trust, since a single "balanced" answer made people perform worse while feeling better. Design for evaluation and surprise instead.
- Proposed using collective human intuition as a negative signal: find what everyone is ignoring and bet against it. He valued "alien" AI over human-mimicking AI for that purpose, and asked that diversity of perspective be measured and deliberately funded.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Held that people mostly don't want the truth. Belief follows belonging, so more accurate information alone won't win back publics who distrust the messenger.
- Reported that in LSE tests fact-checking often increased hostility, while context and solutions-oriented stories built trust. This sits uneasily with Caulfield's proposal to put AI tools in fact-checkers' hands, though Pomerantsev endorsed that proposal for Wikipedia editors and Community Notes writers.
- Called for a right to understand how one's information environment is shaped, meaning real transparency about how these systems are built and fed. He also said the process of reaching agreement matters as much as the result.

**Steven Sloman**
- Described LLMs as strong models of intuition, far behind humans at deliberation. He said they are not members of the community of knowledge because they can't share our goals. He called for humans to keep overseeing and deliberating together, but the harvest's split on where humans belong leaves him out.
- Warned that fluent LLM explanations feel like one's own and deepen the knowledge illusion. He cited findings that even internet access people can't use raises their sense of understanding.

**David Weinberger**
- Disagreed with Caulfield on framing. Caulfield tells students to reject the "conversation" framing. Weinberger said a good investigation is a conversation in which you keep pushing back, and proposed teaching dialogue as a literacy, grading how students question AI.
- Pushed back on Cukier's atrophy concern by citing Socrates' complaints about writing and arguing that LLMs answer two of them: they adapt to the reader and can be questioned. The harvest frames the atrophy dispute as Cukier versus Sloman only.
- Said keeping humans in the loop isn't automatically good, because humans sometimes make the loop worse. Put humans in the dialogue instead.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Said tuning one model to please one user doesn't scale and won't keep knowledge honest. This cuts against Bratton's round-one view that alignment happens one user, one task at a time rather than through top-down guidelines.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Raised epistemic injustice: "but ChatGPT said…" is already being used to discount people's expertise and testimony. No one else took this up.
- Proposed "epistemic technologies" as shared vocabulary, applying to bad systems as well as good ones.

**Adam Bly**
- Said the atomic unit of knowledge should be the finding, not the document.
- Said he has worried about opacity since before starting his company: "We're building a world we may not be able to interpret, let alone govern."

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Rejected the room's shared premise that "the training data is us" as Reflectionism. He argued that the deeper point of AI is what it discloses that we can't yet absorb, which he calls an epistemic overhang, and that alignment should run both ways, with culture aligning to what AI discloses.
- Called the room's framing "humanity-of-the-gaps" and objected directly to Pomerantsev's "no machine should reach the consensus for us," saying the line between humans and machines is already blurring.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Said misinformation works as a justification machine and that a sycophantic chatbot is a faster, more convincing version of it.
- Proposed using AI to lower the cost of refutation, citing Brandolini's law, by putting these tools in the hands of fact-checkers, Wikipedia editors and Community Notes writers.
