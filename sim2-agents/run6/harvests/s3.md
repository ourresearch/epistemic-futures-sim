# Harvest — Session 3: Knowledge Infrastructure and Tools: Commons, Enclosure, and Sovereignty

1. **Commons and enclosure are made together, so every mechanism must say who polices it, who pays, and who guards the guards.** Johns raised this, and Bly, Priem, Weinberger, Deckelmann and Maher each conceded it about their own institutions. The room answered as follows:
   - A fork protects only if someone can afford to run it (Weinberger, Deckelmann, Dyson), which led to Bilder's funder-backed exit insurance pool.
   - Registries, funds and auditors should carry fixed sunset terms (Dyson, Gendler, Farrell, Cukier).
   - Money flows should be published (Dyson, Becker, Shaub).
   - Designs should assume a hostile state may one day control them (Pomerantsev).
   - No concentration of intelligence should regulate itself (Agüera y Arcas).

2. **A firm-level, revenue-based levy drew broad support, but not settled support.** Bratton, Weinberger, Deckelmann and Dyson backed it. Farrell, Maher, Miller and Pomerantsev countered that a levy is a political settlement that can be undone in one vote. They argued it would need producers bargaining jointly under an antitrust safe harbour (Farrell, Hecht, Vincent, Brand, Maher) and more than one payer (Maher, Miller, Shaub). Salib dissented. He would pay for missing inputs by contract and tax coordinated harms rather than use, and Cukier and Banaji joined him on pricing harm. Becker replied that one-by-one contracts drift toward gig labour. Vincent offered a bridge: a presumptive commons-rent tax that audited licences can reduce to zero.

3. **Ring-fence the checkers first, paid from a pool and not per act or per retrieval.** Gendler, Miller, Oransky, Sloman and Krakauer argued this because licences and forks protect copying, not evaluation. They included null-result collection (Evans) and apprenticeships for the next generation of checkers (Krakauer). Oransky, Sloman, Pomerantsev and Agüera y Arcas contested O'Reilly's per-grounding payment on the grounds that it rewards whatever gets retrieved or gamed. Springer responded that the telemetry schema stays neutral on counting and will carry correction status, a sampled support audit and a use limit. Three objections remained:
   - Self-reported grounding is a conflict of interest (Banaji).
   - Grounding is not reading (Turkle).
   - A grounded citation must open to the passage for the reader (Caulfield).

4. **"Open" is a means to durable human access, not the goal.** Hecht proposed this and Priem accepted it. Vincent, Brand and Chan warned that opening one group's data can weaken another's bargaining power and favour whoever has the most compute. Chan added that refusal should be a fundable choice for communities. Priem, Cukier and Brand held that scholarly metadata and open-access text should stay open. Priem committed to measure and publish who actually uses the OpenAlex snapshot. Consent remained split:
   - Becker, Brand and Dash put consent first.
   - Cukier argued for regulating use and misuse instead.
   - Evans, Gendler, Agüera y Arcas and Alvarado stressed that openness can produce a monoculture, and that a fork is not an independent check.

5. **Two questions were left unresolved: who carries this forward, and who gets left out.** Miller and Shaub noted that no one committed to champion the levy, and that the funders who would have to act were not present. Maher, Shaub, Chan and Alvarado warned that reciprocity money paid only to existing, measurable corpora "funds the Global North twice." They also argued that the communities left out of those corpora will be the ones contradicted by the models. Vincent said he has not yet designed how payouts reach communities without a single point of failure. Pomerantsev asked that sovereignty be framed as the rights of citizens and institutions, not territories, and Agüera y Arcas and Chan proposed federated learning as a path between withdrawing a corpus and giving it away.

## Minority positions

**Adrian Johns**
- Automation does not remove human labour. It moves it into curating data, and that labour is currently invisible.
- The enforcers may be right that knowledge can only be maintained through compromises elsewhere. The question is where to draw the line.
- Registries, watermarks and meters belong to a long history of failed technological fixes for trust. He asked what else they will end up being used for.

**Adam Bly**
- Proposed a finding-level provenance standard not owned by System. It would record population, effect size, source, correction status and whether replications are independent, work without a DOI, count null results as findings, and include outside sampled audits of System's own extractions. The harvest does not mention it.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- The enclosure that matters is models locked into US, China and EU stacks, with open weights as the counterweight.
- He values his books being in training data more than his royalties, and wants compensation to come through generative AI, not against it. This cuts against the consent-first position.

**Jason Priem**
- Open data buys independence only if you can also swap the model.

**David Weinberger**
- Set conditions on Bly's standard: no required identifier and no authoritative directory. A correction field alone won't carry a record that was mostly never corrected.
- Argued for many imperfect, interoperable systems kept alive, with interoperability protecting diversity rather than merging systems.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- The enclosure fight has ignored infrastructure load. Crawlers bypass caching and saturate network links, humans lose access, and Wikimedia's engineers and donors carry the cost.
- She asked that human traffic come first and that crawlers identify themselves.

**Esther Dyson**
- The .agent registry bid she advises should be held to the same sunset and disclosure standard.

**James Evans**
- A use-based levy cannot fund null results because nobody reads them.
- After ChatGPT, abstracts with no null claims rose sharply.

**Henry Farrell**
- Open weights don't dissolve the chokepoints: chips, compute and corpora remain leverage.
- Crawler access and bandwidth are a chokepoint that favours producers.
- He stated support for a levy. His objection was that it needs organized bargaining power, not that it is wrong.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Even firm-level payment for checkers should be tested, not assumed. Pricing individual acts risks turning a norm into a transaction, as in the Israeli daycare study.

**Brent Hecht**
- Grounding and training should have separate consent controls.
- Rather than the room guessing a levy rate, labs should disclose the pricing figures they already compute.

**Katherine Maher**
- A consortium of public broadcasters, domestic and international, negotiating licensing and grounding terms together is buildable now.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Proposed a split the harvest collapses: pay per use only where grounding is measurable. Labs would carry disclosed obligations, as line items in a prospectus, for the checkers, nulls and correction work that don't show up in use.
- The telemetry itself should be audited by rotating auditors not paid by the labs.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Publishers should fund the checker pool because they earned on volume. Crossref's adoption of the retraction database is the model to copy.
- None of it matters unless funders and universities stop counting papers and citations in hiring, promotion and funding. Everything else is whack-a-mole.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- He backs a levy (Zuckerman's model), with safeguards against state capture.
- Provenance must flag covert or coordinated content, and the line should be drawn at deception, not foreignness.

**Peter Salib**
- A training levy creates a new right to charge for what already exists, and like the register it will become property.
- He argued the model layer is contestable. Whether the buyers of human knowledge work could concentrate is an open question he has not yet worked on.

**Steven Sloman**
- Attribution to correctors should be a condition of access. Credit may matter to checkers as much as money.

**Alex Springer**
- Policing through two witnesses: the agent's log and the publisher's CDN log check each other.
- The measurement should never be set by the party that takes a margin on the count.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Some of the money should go to reading itself: libraries, teachers and time set aside for slow reading.
- There should be a measure of whether people click through and actually read the source.

**Nick Vincent**
- The leverage lies in evaluation data. A small group of real experts can bargain effectively only if they act together.
- He would combine contracts and a levy rather than choose between them.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Proposed mutual audit as the model for the checking layer, with AI and humans checking each other, as in the Formal Conjectures benchmark. The harvest does not record this proposal.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Bly's non-commercial licence plus patent fails two of POSI's four commitments, whatever the charter says. Charge for services, not data.
- Self-assessments need community scrutiny with a deadline.
- Critical scholarly infrastructure needs redundancy outside the US.
- Funders must stop pretending they don't fund infrastructure.

**Amy Brand**
- Drew a line between open for reading and open for training.
- Her proposed consortium would favour retrieval over pre-training and renegotiate every two years.

**David Krakauer**
- The thing to protect first is the repair rate, not the dump. When copying outruns repair, information is lost for good.
- Mutual audit works only while humans are still trained to check. That cuts against Agüera y Arcas.

**Tui Shaub**
- Governance should be an ecosystem of regulation, funding conditions, professional norms and insurers, not a levy alone.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Open data and open weights give access to what a system does, not to the source of its errors, and a telemetry track record will be misread as warrant.
- Audits should record classes of failure as commons infrastructure. The glossary should separate retrieval, grounding and support.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Deprecated models, training checkpoints and disclosed data sources should be retained as knowledge infrastructure for independent auditors.
- Models favour their own outputs and machine-written text, so models should not curate or check the commons. That cuts against Agüera y Arcas.
- Human users should be audited too.

**Adam Becker**
- A levy or telemetry scheme set up after the fact makes the taking legal but not consensual.
- Billionaire philanthropy is an exercise of power. Checkers should be paid from public money raised by taxing concentrated wealth.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Licensing deals risk making the answer engine the only place a paper can be read.
- The harm he cares most about is laundering, such as Grammarly attributing advice to him he'd never give. The support audit should be funded as human work.

**Leslie Chan**
- Requiring legibility to get paid is "comply or be invisible."
- Public money should fund governance, not just servers, including co-governed regional infrastructures such as SciELO and AmeliCA and compute commons allocated transparently by region.
- Levy funds should be governed by the people who get summarized.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Design a process, not an organization.
- He now attaches Krakauer's condition to "default to use": the value holds only if someone is still paid and trained to check.

**Anil Dash**
- Open weights are not open source under the Open Source Initiative's definition. This cuts against Bratton, Alvarado and Priem's treatment of open weights.
- Labs training on Wikipedia while Wikimedia pays the infrastructure bill is "Napster, not a commons." They should pay for the load.
- Build many small, community-controlled alternatives run by libraries, universities and NGOs, without waiting for Washington.
