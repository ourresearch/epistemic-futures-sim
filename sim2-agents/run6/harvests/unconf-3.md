# Harvest — Unconference room 3: Cognitive atrophy: real phenomenon or not?

1. **The room agreed atrophy is real but disagreed on what is atrophying.** Esther Dyson named reasoning and understanding ("information diabetes," people "thinking like LLMs"), David Krakauer named competence in any outsourced skill, Sherry Turkle named empathy, solitude and attachment, and Mahzarin Banaji argued judgment is being actively reshaped (people copy a model's bias) rather than simply weakening, adding that human judgment was never a clean standard to begin with.

2. **Remedies should change situations rather than rely on awareness.** Tamar Gendler advanced this, supported by Kara Miller (the phone cabinet as a fix for a "collective trap"), Krakauer (tool availability as the lever) and Banaji (blind auditions as situational strategies). Left unresolved was how much weight awareness deserves: Dyson ("theory of business mind"), Turkle (framing the choice as one's own) and Banaji ("a first step") kept a role for it, while Gendler cautioned that knowing rarely controls behavior.

3. **Because loss persists after the tool is removed, competence must come before reliance, and delay is costly.** Krakauer's bistability and hysteresis model grounded this, and Miller used it to argue against waiting on AI as the US did on phones. Miller proposed treating mentorship and apprenticeship as epistemic infrastructure employers are responsible for, with support from Turkle (apprenticeship happens through conversation) and Krakauer (skill tracks recent hands-on work).

4. **Unresolved: whether technology can be part of the fix.** Turkle argued against technological fixes and for investing in human spaces for conversation and solitude. Krakauer countered that transparent tools can leave competence behind, and Tui Shaub argued for designing in error and serendipity, warning that ruling out design cedes the field to the engagement business and calling for artists at the table. Dyson added that design follows the business model.

5. **Accountability proposals diverged.** Dyson proposed treating chatbots and agents as services acting on the mind, like drugs or medical devices, with maker liability and disclosure. Shaub placed liability within a wider governance ecosystem, and Krakauer asked "liability for what?", proposing tools be judged by what remains when they're removed. Banaji proposed a public registry and independent behavioral audits of both models and the people using them, not relying on self-report, with older models preserved for comparison.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
- Cheating in school is the wrong problem to focus on, because it mostly hurts the students themselves. The fix is putting good friction back in, not policing.
- "The real black box is the people and the business models, not the weights." She argued for training people and holding them accountable rather than concentrating on reducing bias in the models.
- Chatbots and agents are "not speech." (The harvest records the drugs and medical devices comparison but not this explicit rejection of treating them as speech.)

**Tamar Gendler**
- She disagreed with Dyson's claim that we are "starting to think like LLMs," arguing that the brain has always worked by taking the most likely path. On her view, the question is which paths are being worn in, not whether we are becoming something new.
- Cheating reflects courses built around getting credit, so much of the fix has to come from how assessment is designed.

**Sherry Turkle**
- The friction the room praised is less about difficulty than about vulnerability. People prefer machines because machines won't hurt them.
- The room talks about AI and writing but neglects AI and reading, where students can summarize Proust yet lose the integration of thought and feeling that reading builds.
- She rejected treating people as addicts and said nothing about the trajectory is inevitable.

**David Krakauer**
- He qualified "use it or lose it": people with identical exposure diverge depending on what they learned before the tool arrived and how transparent the tool is, so exposure alone doesn't determine the outcome.
- The real danger is not error but error that can no longer be detected, and he proposed measuring the acquisition, reliance and removal loop as the basis for a "science of stupidity."

**Tui Shaub**
- She warned against both the complacency of "adults adapt" and "hyper-vigilance that only knows how to ban." This cuts against Miller's urgency. Miller acknowledged the warning but held that complications had become a reason for the US to do nothing.
- Error and serendipity, "the accident you didn't order up," are distinct from friction. An answer engine removes both.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- She pushed against the "romantic assumption" behind restoring friction. Because human judgment was always biased, the goal should be restoring structures that check us, such as blind auditions and conflict-of-interest rules, not friction alone.
- Blinding works only in narrow settings like grading and hiring, and group membership leaks through indirect cues. This qualifies the room's reliance on situational remedies.
- People's model-shaped decisions feed back into training data, so each generation carries the bias more deeply. Her proposed audit standard is "no worse than the previous generation."
