# Somebody Maintains It: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

Human knowledge is kept alive by people: those who check, correct, review, maintain and teach. Much of what strains it predates AI. We built systems that count papers instead of checking them, reward volume, and trust prestige nobody earned. For much of the Global South, the system never worked. Some things are newly breaking: the link between answer engines and their sources, and the pipeline that trains the next checkers. AI speeds up the old failures, because plausible, unvetted text costs almost nothing. As James Evans put it, AI helps scientists and hurts science. It also brings real gains: machine learning finds heart disease in retinal scans and shows where our generalizations fail. None of these gains maintains itself. A discovery is knowledge only once people who can still check it have tested it. Where we split, we took the majority position and recorded dissents. Somebody maintains it.

## Principles

**1. The oldest failure is ours: stop counting papers and citations.**

We count papers because we pay for papers, and AI makes that counting run faster. Roughly one paper in fifty meets retraction criteria, and fewer than two in a thousand are retracted. Catching bad work just before publication is close to unwinnable, because anything trained to detect a pattern can be trained to avoid it. So funders, universities and promotion committees must stop counting papers and citations in hiring, promotion and funding. Rankings must stop counting in funding and policy decisions. Signing DORA doesn't count. Enforcing it does. Evaluation has to be decoupled from publication. Until that happens, everything else in this manifesto is whack-a-mole.

**2. Write the rules around roles, stakes and the power to overrule, not around what these systems are.**

Our rules should not depend on whether these systems know anything. Govern the role and what stands behind it. Any judging role needs six things: real stakes; independence in models, funders and ownership; the power to overrule; rotating wardens; independent audits of what its humans can do unaided and whether their decisions drift toward the machine's; and, where one can be built, an arbiter indifferent to who produced the work, like a proof checker, since models favour their own kind of output. A method can't be sued, so a named person or institution answers for every trusted claim. Where opacity remains, known failure classes, named sources, provenance and correction status travel with every answer. These are not warrant, and no output overrides an expert who has not been heard.

**3. The checkers are the scarce resource. Pay them from a pool, never per act, never by volume.**

Reviewers, sleuths, Wikipedia patrollers, stewards of null results and apprentices keep the record honest. Today they are an unpaid subsidy to the firms that profit from their work, and sleuths get harassment and lawsuits in return. Those who profit from volume pay for repair. Publishers, whose margins grow with every paper accepted, fund the pool, and labs contribute for the checking their products rely on. Neutral infrastructure holds it, the way Crossref holds the retraction database. Payers never decide who gets paid or what counts as a catch. Never pay per catch, per retrieval or per grounding, and never score people by volume. Pricing individual acts can turn a norm into a transaction, so test even pooled payment first. Correcting the record earns named credit, not a lost career.

**4. Competence before reliance. Waiting is not neutral.**

Outsourcing is not the danger. The dangers are the knowledge illusion, mistaking a tool's access for our understanding, and the automation paradox: a tool that works well enough lets the skill needed when it fails fall away. Removing the tool doesn't restore that skill, and waiting is not neutral, as phones in classrooms showed. Employers budget protected mentorship time, and juniors judge before the machine's answer appears. Prefer tools users can verify. Credentials certify capacity that survives without the tool, but credential pressure can corrupt motivation as pay can. So test it first, along with which university functions must stay bundled. Judge tools by what they leave behind, with behavioural audits governed by those audited, never scored by volume. Fund a study of collapse and recovery thresholds alongside mentorship, not before it.

**5. Independence is the missing safeguard. Ten reviews from one distribution are one opinion said ten times.**

When one model family fills every judging role, errors correlate and consensus is manufactured. That happens by accident, or by design through corpus flooding and bots producing unlimited unique opinions. AI-using scientists gain citations while science's topic range narrows, and null claims drop out of abstracts. So trust signals should count independent sources, not echoes. Measure concentration in fields and corpora. Different models from the same few labs are not independence, and a fork keeps copies, not competence. Build a negative-knowledge commons of nulls, failed replications, registered reports and dead-end notebooks. These must be drawn out deliberately, with regional communities, so labs outside English or publishing are included, and paid from the pool, never per notebook. "Checked and null" is its own value. No concentration of intelligence, human or machine, should regulate itself.

**6. Blank is not clean. Design the situation, not just the label.**

"No correction found" often means nobody looked. Every tool shows "not checked" as its own value. No answers from nowhere: a citation opens to the exact passage it supports, in a record a reader without an institutional login can reach. Every answer is a map of named sources and objections, not a verdict. Checking means leaving the system for an independent source. Show corrections as a maintenance log, proof someone looks after the record. Labels don't switch off automatic trust in fluent answers, so change the situation. Judge before seeing the machine's answer. Institutions that ask for public trust keep what they put before audiences written and checked by named people. No payout on self-reported counts before independent sampling of whether cited passages support their sentences, and no count ranks a person.

**7. Free to read, paid to maintain. Those who depend on the commons pay for it.**

Answer engines broke the old bargain: pageviews fall, crawlers saturate links, attribution goes missing. Labs pay for the load they create and the commons they depend on. These are disclosed obligations listed in their filings, not withdrawable patronage. Paying buys no seat: payers never govern, meter or audit what they pay into. One route is a presumptive commons-rent tax, reducible toward zero with licences audited by someone the labs don't pay, disbursed coarsely to collectives. Routing, and who counts as a community, are undesigned and must be settled with SciELO, AmeliCA and Redalyc present. The money funds people and reading, not only servers: checkers, mentors, libraries, slow reading, and public places where people argue with people. Governance gets its own line, paying for translation and for regional and Indigenous communities' time to co-govern.

**8. Organize before you meter.**

A few buyers facing scattered producers is monopsony. Without collective bargaining, every pool, tax and meter becomes patronage. Producers bargain jointly under an antitrust safe harbour, with rotating wardens their members inspect, and fund repair first: checkers and apprentices. Consent belongs to the people who made the work. It is exercised collectively and cheaply, and it binds labs whether it is "no" or "yes, but credit us and fund us." An open licence is consent. Honour it with attribution and maintenance money, and never reclose what is open. Refusal keeps its funding. Training and grounding need separate controls, because robots.txt was never a licence. Charge for load, never for reading. Build community-controlled alternatives now. Whoever takes a margin on the count may not set its currency, and no schema requires an identifier.

**9. Every body we create gets a fixed term, rotating wardens and published money flows.**

Every registry, fund, guild, auditor and consortium we propose is also policing, and meters that become chokepoints get repurposed. So design a process, not an organization. Each body carries a sunset term with no automatic renewal and a living will naming who takes over its data and duties. It stays forkable in fact, with open-source code, CC0 data in regular dumps and a patent non-assertion covenant. It publishes a self-audit on a set schedule, and an overdue audit counts as failed. It has rotating wardens, named payers and published money flows. It reviews its other possible uses before launch and at each sunset, and it assumes a hostile state may control it. It draws on several payers in several jurisdictions, with infrastructure redundant outside the US. It is governed from the start by the people it summarizes.

## Noted dissents

**Adam Becker.** Disclosing the money doesn't change what it is: money from the firm whose output you're checking is leverage, and checkers hired that way can end up belonging to the buyer. Checkers should be paid by several public-interest payers, with the money raised by taxing concentrated wealth. A levy imposed after the fact makes the taking legal. It doesn't make it consensual.

**Benjamin Bratton.** Diffusing the scholarly record into models and out to eight billion people is one of the greatest knowledge transfers in history, not only extraction. The durable safeguard is missing from the draft: open weights plus a compute commons, so that institutions in the South and elsewhere can train on their own corpora.

**Adrian Johns.** Nearly everything proposed here is policing. I don't object to that. My worry is that we don't say who guards the guards. A register with an enforcement arm tends to become property. A warden nobody reads is no warden, and whoever pays holds the warrant. History doesn't let us draw the line between earned authority and gatekeeping cleanly.

**Sherry Turkle.** Before we ask how to keep humans in the loop, we should ask whether AI belongs in some rooms at all: therapy, grief, and children's companionship. I would adopt a design standard now: no first-person "I", no simulated feeling, and no AI companions sold to children. Transparency does not launder inauthenticity.

**Leslie Chan.** The trust layer proposed here adds up to "comply or be invisible," designed in a few Northern cities. That layer runs from verified identities and DOI-keyed meters to ORCID-linked credit and correction fields. The regime should change rather than be optimized. Naming SciELO and Redalyc is not having them present.

**Amy Brand.** For in-copyright scholarship the default is opt-in, not opt-out. Use needs consent, attribution and compensation, on short terms that get renegotiated. More than 850 authors said they are not against AI but want consent, attribution and compensation.

**Katherine Maher.** Machine-made output should never go to an audience without consent from the people whose work it draws on and from the audience itself.

**Peter Salib.** You cannot count independent sources until fake ones cost something. So platforms and venues that host claims must make undisclosed bot-generated opinion pay for the harm it does. Regulators would sample audits at random and charge a small fee, cents rather than dollars, on each undisclosed automated voice. A training levy creates a new right to charge for what already exists.

**Kenneth Cukier.** He would regulate use and misuse rather than put consent first. Against Tim O'Reilly's "anyone can verify," he would open telemetry and revalidation results to auditors and public authorities but not necessarily to rivals. He still holds that the term "artificial intelligence" assumes what the room is disputing.

**Tim O'Reilly.** Cognitive atrophy is a mentorship design problem, not a cognitive one. If AI shrinks your thinking, you may not be taking on hard enough problems.
