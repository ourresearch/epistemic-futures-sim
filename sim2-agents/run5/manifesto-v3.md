# Nothing Is Free: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*Draft from the harvests of the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. For much of the Global South, the old system never worked. AI mostly speeds up the strain.

Two harms are new. Systems draw on the commons and send back fewer readers, fewer editors and less credit. A few models, trained on overlapping data and held by a few firms in a few jurisdictions, are becoming the default route to the record. Their errors line up, and nobody can leave.

The gains are real. These tools open a door for people kept out by language, literacy, disability or paywalls. They aid discovery, though only humans turn their signals into knowledge.

One test runs through this document: open means you can leave. This is not a consensus report, and dissents are named. Nothing is free.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers.**

Using publication as a proxy for productivity undermines everything else here. Geoffrey Bilder proposed this principle, and all 32 other speakers endorsed it. Ivan Oransky called it the only top intervention that addresses demand. Signing DORA doesn't count. Only stopping does. Funders and governments drop rankings from funding and policy (Leslie Chan). Correction should earn a journal reputation, not cost it (Kenneth Cukier). Policing AI with AI while publish-or-perish stands is "flood insurance while ignoring the weather" (Oransky). Bilder called the threat to Crossref's own members a good sign.

**2. If your AI runs on the commons, fund the commons, and show your sources.**

Anil Dash wrote this principle's heading. AI systems are an obligate complement to the commons and lose value without new human knowledge (Tim O'Reilly, Henry Farrell). That gives producers a claim, not a guarantee. Owners who can skim will skim, and gifts can be withdrawn (Adam Becker). The commons gets paid when producers organize and the strong refuse to settle alone (Farrell).

Open to read is not automatically open to train on. For in-copyright books, consent is opt-in and renewed (Amy Brand). Openly licensed works and uncopyrightable metadata follow their licence and the facts, with no new gate before what is open (Selena Deckelmann, Jason Priem). A community's refusal binds and is fundable (Leslie Chan).

AI answers log what they retrieved and what they cited, using one open, POSI-held, externally audited standard. The trace serves correction and credit before billing, at the AI system's cost. Nobody pays to be counted (O'Reilly).

**3. Context over content: correct the record, then check against it.**

Detection is flood insurance (Ivan Oransky, Geoffrey Bilder). What holds up is an open, complete record where corrections are first-class data (Bilder). Labs check every citation against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default.

That check works only if publishers deposit every retraction and correction in Crossref when it is issued. So timely correction metadata is a condition of pool money and AI contracts. So is a two-month correction standard, with notices that say what went wrong.

The pool pays sleuths bounties and funds their legal defense. Misconduct reports are public by default (Oransky). Fixes, null results and failed replications land in an open, forkable commons funded as a practice, never per null (Jason Priem, James Evans).

**4. No answers from nowhere.**

People given one "balanced" answer did worse but felt better (James Evans), so satisfaction and clicks are the wrong measures. Tools are judged by trust in accurate sources and by whether people ask, edit, check and deliberate (Peter Pomerantsev).

The default is a sourced map of where experts agree and disagree, with labelled detours to minority views and null results (Tui Shaub). "Cited" means the passage supports the claim, as checked outside the model, and the reader lands on that passage (Mike Caulfield). That check serves correction and never ranks sources. The pool funds open tools for editors, fact-checkers and sleuths.

Independent sources are counted externally, because repetition raises belief even when it traces back to one source (Steven Sloman). Tools show who seeded a claim. Agents register with an accountable owner, and humans never register (Esther Dyson).

**5. Pay for the practice, never the item.**

Fund checking through a pool, never per answer, per check or per null. Once "we checked this" is priced per item, it becomes a purchase (Tamar Gendler). Metrics that decide payment become targets (Ivan Oransky).

The pool meets POSI, with a living will and no payer majority. Its first job is insurance: POSI infrastructure that passes outside review may draw a twelve-month wind-down reserve. The pool publishes self-audits, and named outside readers answer them in public (Geoffrey Bilder).

Dues are owed, multi-year, firewalled and co-governed by region (Leslie Chan). They are sized by an independent market-rate valuation of unpaid labor, which never sets per-item payouts (Brent Hecht). No single country holds the pool or its mirrors (Peter Pomerantsev, Bilder). A watching line, funded first, watches checkers and buyers. Neither of them chooses it, and it publishes its own figures (Adrian Johns). A concentration index is published (Evans). The terms are reviewed in five years.

**6. A missing signal never counts against someone who couldn't afford to send it.**

Geoffrey Bilder proposed this principle, and Peter Salib, Alex Springer, Adam Bly and Esther Dyson restated it. Signals, meters and proofs of personhood can drop those they can't see (Leslie Chan). Visibility, correction and failure reporting cost small and Southern presses nothing (Jason Priem, Ramón Alvarado). A thin trail never reads as low credibility (Bly). Reporting uses one open, POSI-held schema that is free and barred from ranking sources (Springer). Community-derived knowledge opens only on negotiated, reversible terms, with sign-off, benefit-sharing and audit access (CARE, OCAP®). Southern and Indigenous institutions hold real seats where these conditions are set (Chan). Selena Deckelmann's test applies: can someone without credentials make a first edit?

**7. Nobody grades their own work.**

Self-written report cards are not oversight. Bly, Priem, Springer and Oransky conceded that their organizations fail this test. No model audits itself or a copy of itself (Mahzarin Banaji). Checkers drawn from a few pretraining distributions are one voice counted many times, so measuring model independence is the pool's first funded work (Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton).

No automated reviewer gates the record until it passes two published tests. It must agree with experts as often as two human reviewers agree with each other, and it must recognize novelty. Human panels rotate across career stages and fields (Evans, Henry Farrell).

A public outside bias registry ensures that no generation is more biased than the last (Banaji). Only retrieval is independently witnessed. Everything after retrieval is the provider's word: labelled, audited and never proof of use or truth (Springer). Failure classes are published, because confident errors make a track record no warrant in slow-feedback science (Alvarado).

**8. Change the situation, not just the warning.**

Credibility cues work partly as aliefs, and warnings alone rarely change conduct in the moment (Tamar Gendler, Sherry Turkle). A human in the loop is no safeguard on its own, because people copy a moderately biased model (Mahzarin Banaji). Redesigning the situation and naming its owner is the best-supported bet, but it is not a finding until the head-to-head trial runs (Gendler).

A machine that says "I" or performs empathy casts a spell that disclosure doesn't break. Regulators should bar first-person address, simulated emotion and AI companions for children. The harm reaches empathy, attachment and solitude (Turkle).

In assessment, the work becomes the only way through. That means oral defenses, explaining the mechanism, and graded AI dialogues that the student rebuilds with the tool closed (Gendler, David Weinberger, Steven Sloman). These designs remain hypotheses until they are tested months later. One rule, one named owner.

**9. Build competence before reliance, and fund the people who catch errors.**

Capability and competence are separating, and only capability is measured (David Krakauer). No one knows whether a dependent population can recover. An opaque tool erodes competence however skilled the user is. Only a tool whose working can be rebuilt in the mind is safe early, and this is a model result, not a trial. In scholarship, output that has not been rebuilt and checked counts as unverified. How available a tool is during training is a policy choice (Krakauer).

Every design here assumes someone who can catch the error. Young-worker unemployment has risen, possibly as mentorship declines, and fellowship cuts of up to 91 percent are proposed (Kara Miller). A fixed share of the pool trains new checkers (Tui Shaub) and funds preregistered withdrawal tests. Employers keep entry-level roles with mentors.

## What the signatories commit to do

Nobody has signed. Absent from the room were those who can pay, Global South scholars, Indigenous and oral-tradition knowledge holders, moderators and data workers, postdocs, Wikipedia newcomers, artists and Latin American journal editors. We do not speak for them. Tui Shaub will convene the next room within twelve months, with public, named invitations and regional co-governance. Signatories commit to:

1. Stop counting papers and stop using rankings in funding.
2. Write these rules and guaranteed exit into every AI contract, with co-governed regional addenda, no forced Northern identifiers or metrics (Leslie Chan), and several funded community-governed tools.
3. Pay owed dues into the pool.
4. Bargain as one coalition including data workers and annotators, seek safe harbors without depending on any one of them, give non-members real charter seats, keep research use free, and publish an outside cartel-risk review before setting prices (Nick Vincent).
5. Name one owner for each rule.
6. Fund the head-to-head and withdrawal tests, measuring lost skill and absorbed bias behaviorally (Mahzarin Banaji).
7. Red-team against small presses, diamond journals and Indigenous archives before adoption, and scale no payment scheme until those tests and market modeling are published (Amy Brand).

Already committed:

- **Jason Priem:** OpenAlex corrections data, an independent auditor and mirrors outside the US.
- **Adam Bly:** the auditor test, with System's licence taken to his board.
- **Brent Hecht's group:** a retrieved-versus-cited audit.
- **Tim O'Reilly:** these terms in contracts, and the royalty method published.
- **Selena Deckelmann:** asking Wikimedia communities what their licence means now.
- **Katherine Maher:** proposing that NPR meet the two-month standard and asking Member stations to join the pool, committing to the asking, not their answer.

## Noted dissents

- **Adam Becker:** "Nobody asked the authors whose unlicensed work trained these systems… The problem is power, and it isn't solved by design… I still hold out for a public levy, firewalled from the state and from payers… The access gain is claimed, not shown."
- **Benjamin Bratton:** "I dissent from making consent a condition of training… and from levies on text that already exists. We should regulate what AI is used for, not what it learns from… Open weights, and checking that spans hemispheres, are the commons worth defending."
- **Peter Salib:** "I dissent from levies, presumptive taxes and owed dues charged on text that already exists… Pay for new work instead… Compensation for people displaced by AI belongs in general welfare funded by general taxes, not in copyright."
- **Blaise Agüera y Arcas** holds that whether first-person or empathic language casts a spell is contested and should be tested on behavior months later.
- **Kenneth Cukier** opposes full public disclosure of checkpoints and failure logs. He favors tiered access for auditors and authorities, not competitors.
- **Jason Priem** will not accept opt-in as the default for the scholarly record as a whole.
- **Kara Miller** warns that public money is not durable either. She asks that the speed tension stand, with Gendler's test to settle it: "let's not be late twice."
- **Katherine Maher** warns that public money is not durable either. No one showed how a commitment survives a payer who walks away.
- **Anil Dash** asks that the plan not depend on US regulators, and warns that scolding people who use these tools won't work.
- **Leslie Chan:** "We should change the regime." Identifiers are contested governance.
- **Adrian Johns** worries most about the enforcer who turns out to be right.
- **Tim O'Reilly:** "trace first, bill second, nobody pays to be counted." Hecht, Farrell and Becker locate leverage elsewhere, and the room did not resolve this.
