# Nothing Is Free: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*Draft from the harvests of the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. We count papers, we rank universities, and we penalize journals that correct the record. For much of the Global South, the old system never worked. AI mostly speeds this up.

Two harms are new. First, systems draw on the commons and send back fewer readers, fewer editors and less credit. Second, a few models are becoming the default route to the record. They are trained on overlapping data and held by a few firms in a few jurisdictions. Their errors line up, and nobody can leave.

There are also real gains. For people kept out by language, literacy, disability or paywalls, these tools open a door. They also help discovery: a retina scan can now reveal heart disease that no ophthalmologist can read. Only humans supply the causality that turns such signals into knowledge.

One test runs through all of this: open means you can leave. This is not a consensus report, and dissents are named. Nothing is free.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers.**

Publication is used as a proxy for productivity, and nothing else here holds while that incentive stands. Geoffrey Bilder proposed this principle, and all 32 other speakers endorsed it. Ivan Oransky called it the only top intervention that addresses demand. Signing DORA doesn't count. Only stopping does. Rankings turn paper counts into money, so funders and governments drop them from funding and policy (Leslie Chan). Correction should earn a journal reputation rather than cost it (Kenneth Cukier). Using AI to police AI while leaving publish-or-perish alone is "flood insurance while ignoring the weather" (Oransky). Bilder warned that this may threaten Crossref's own members and called that discomfort a good sign.

**2. If your AI runs on the commons, fund the commons, and show your sources.**

Anil Dash's sentence answers the first new harm. Labs' systems are an obligate complement to the commons: without new human knowledge to summarize, they lose their value (Tim O'Reilly, Henry Farrell). That gives producers a claim, not a guarantee. Owners who can skim will skim, and a gift can be withdrawn (Adam Becker). The commons gets paid when producers organize as a countervailing power and the strong refuse to settle alone (Farrell).

Open to read does not by itself mean open to train on. For in-copyright books, consent is opt-in and renewed (Amy Brand). For openly licensed works and uncopyrightable metadata, the licence and the facts decide. Those terms are honoured in full, and nobody puts a new gate in front of what is already open (Selena Deckelmann, Jason Priem). A community's refusal binds and is fundable (Leslie Chan).

AI answers log what they retrieved and what they cited. The log uses one open, POSI-held standard and is audited from outside. The trace serves correction and credit before billing. Its costs fall on the AI system, and nobody pays to be counted (O'Reilly).

**3. Context over content: correct the record, then check against it.**

Detection is flood insurance (Ivan Oransky, Geoffrey Bilder). What holds up is an open, complete record where corrections are first-class data (Bilder). About one paper in fifty should be retracted, and about one in a thousand is (Oransky). Labs check every citation against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default.

That check works only if publishers deposit every retraction and correction in Crossref when it is issued. Crossref currently holds only a fraction of the retractions Retraction Watch has found. So timely correction metadata is a condition of pool money and of AI contracts. So is a two-month correction standard, with notices that say what went wrong.

The pool pays sleuths bug-bounty style and funds their legal defense. Misconduct reports are public by default (Oransky). Fixes, null results and failed replications land in an open, forkable commons (Jason Priem). This work is funded as a practice, never per null (James Evans).

**4. No answers from nowhere.**

People given one "balanced" answer did worse but felt better (James Evans). So satisfaction is the wrong measure, and so are clicks. Belief tracks belonging. Tools are judged by whether they build trust in accurate sources and whether people ask, edit, check and deliberate (Peter Pomerantsev).

The default is a sourced map of where experts agree and genuinely disagree. It includes labelled detours to minority views and null results (Tui Shaub). "Cited" means the passage supports the claim, as checked outside the model, and the reader lands on that passage (Mike Caulfield). That check is itself a meter, so it serves correction and never ranks sources. Writing is now cheaper than refuting, so the pool funds open tools for editors, fact-checkers and sleuths.

Independent sources are counted outside the answering system, because repetition raises belief even when every repetition traces back to one source (Steven Sloman). Tools show who seeded a claim. Agents register with an accountable owner, and humans never register (Esther Dyson).

**5. Pay for the practice, never the item.**

Fund checking as a practice through a pool, never per answer, per check or per null. Once "we checked this" is priced per item, it becomes a purchase (Tamar Gendler). Any metric that decides payment becomes a target (Ivan Oransky).

The pool meets POSI, with a living will and no payer majority. Its first job is insurance. Any POSI infrastructure that passes outside review may draw a twelve-month wind-down reserve. The pool publishes self-audits, and named outside readers answer them in public (Geoffrey Bilder).

Dues are owed, multi-year, firewalled and co-governed by region (Leslie Chan). They are sized by an independent valuation of unpaid labor at market rates, and that valuation never sets per-item payouts (Brent Hecht). No single country holds the pool or its mirrors (Peter Pomerantsev, Bilder). A watching line, funded before the checking line, watches both the checkers and the buyers. Neither of them chooses the watchers, and the watchers publish their own figures (Adrian Johns). A concentration index is published (Evans). The terms are reviewed in five years.

**6. A missing signal never counts against someone who couldn't afford to send it.**

Geoffrey Bilder asked for this principle, and Peter Salib, Alex Springer, Adam Bly and Esther Dyson restated it. Any trust signal, meter or proof of personhood can drop those it can't see, and hard-to-fake signals are often hard to afford (Leslie Chan). So visibility, correction and failure reporting cost small and Southern presses nothing (Jason Priem, Ramón Alvarado). A thin trail never reads as low credibility (Bly). Reporting runs on one open, POSI-held schema that is free to sources and barred from ranking them (Springer). Community-derived knowledge is open only on negotiated, reversible terms, with community sign-off, benefit-sharing and audit access (CARE, OCAP®). Southern and Indigenous institutions hold real seats wherever these conditions are set (Chan). Selena Deckelmann's test applies throughout: can someone with no credentials still make a first edit?

**7. Nobody grades their own work.**

Self-written report cards are not oversight. Bly, Priem, Springer and Oransky conceded that their own organizations fail this test. No model audits itself or a copy of itself, because models prefer their own text (Mahzarin Banaji). Checkers drawn from a few pretraining distributions are one voice counted many times. So measuring model independence is the pool's first funded work (Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton).

No automated reviewer gates the record until it passes two published tests. It must agree with experts as often as two human reviewers agree with each other, and it must recognize novelty. Human panels rotate across career stages and fields, because senior reviewers penalize disruption (Evans, Henry Farrell).

Outside auditors keep a public bias registry, and no generation may be more biased than the last (Banaji). Only retrieval is independently witnessed. Everything after retrieval is the provider's own word. It is labelled and audited, and it never counts as proof of use or truth (Springer). These systems deliver errors with the same confidence as truths, so a track record is no warrant in slow-feedback science. Failure classes are published (Alvarado).

**8. Change the situation, not just the warning.**

Credibility cues work partly as aliefs. Knowing that fluent text is cheap does not stop it from feeling credible, and warnings alone rarely change conduct in the moment (Tamar Gendler, Sherry Turkle). A human in the loop is no safeguard on its own. People copy a moderately biased model (Mahzarin Banaji), and the scarce resource is diverse judgment (James Evans). Redesigning the situation and naming its owner is the best-supported bet, but it is not a finding until the head-to-head trial runs (Gendler).

A machine that says "I" or performs empathy casts a spell that disclosure doesn't break. Transparency does not launder pretend empathy. Regulators should bar first-person address, simulated emotion and AI companions for children. The harm reaches empathy, attachment and solitude (Turkle).

In assessment, the work becomes the only way through. That means oral defenses, explaining the mechanism, and graded AI dialogues that the student then rebuilds with the tool closed (Gendler, David Weinberger, Steven Sloman). These designs remain hypotheses until they are tested months later. One rule, one named owner.

**9. Build competence before reliance, and fund the people who catch errors.**

Capability and competence are separating, and only capability is measured (David Krakauer). No one knows whether a dependent population can recover. An opaque tool erodes competence however skilled the user is. Only a tool whose working can be rebuilt in the mind is safe early, and this is a model result, not a trial. In scholarship, output that has not been rebuilt and checked counts as unverified. How available a tool is during training is a policy choice (Krakauer).

Every design here assumes someone who can catch the error. Unemployment among young workers rose from just over 6 percent in June 2023 to 9.2 percent in September 2025. Experts say declining mentorship may be part of the cause. Proposed cuts reach 65 percent for NSF fellowships and 91 percent for postdoctoral fellowships (Kara Miller). As one chemist said of lab training, "it's not something you can just shut off and then turn it back on." A fixed share of the pool trains new checkers (Tui Shaub) and funds preregistered withdrawal tests. Employers keep entry-level roles with mentors.

## What the signatories commit to do

Nobody has signed. Those who can pay were not in the room. Neither were Global South scholars, Indigenous and oral-tradition knowledge holders, moderators and data workers, postdocs, Wikipedia newcomers, artists or Latin American journal editors. We do not speak for them. Tui Shaub will convene the next room within twelve months, with public, named invitations and regional co-governance. Signatories commit to:

1. Stop counting papers, and stop using rankings in funding.
2. Write these rules and guaranteed exit into every AI contract as a baseline. Regions write co-governed addenda, and no term forces Southern institutions to adopt Northern identifiers or metrics (Leslie Chan). Fund several community-governed tools.
3. Pay owed dues into the pool.
4. Bargain as one coalition that includes data workers and annotators, and seek safe harbors without depending on any one of them. Give non-members real seats in the charter and keep research use free. Publish an outside cartel-risk review before setting any prices (Nick Vincent).
5. Name one owner for each rule.
6. Fund the head-to-head and withdrawal tests. Measure lost skill and absorbed bias with behavioral measures (Mahzarin Banaji).
7. Red-team against small presses, diamond journals and Indigenous archives before adoption. No payment scheme scales until those tests and market modeling are published (Amy Brand).

Already committed:

- **Jason Priem:** OpenAlex corrections data, an independent auditor and mirrors outside the US.
- **Adam Bly:** the auditor test, with System's licence taken to his board.
- **Brent Hecht's group:** a retrieved-versus-cited audit.
- **Tim O'Reilly:** these terms in contracts, and the royalty method published.
- **Selena Deckelmann:** asking Wikimedia communities what their licence means now.
- **Katherine Maher:** proposing that NPR meet the two-month standard, and asking Member stations to join the pool. She commits to asking, not to their answer.

## Noted dissents

- **Adam Becker:** "Nobody asked the authors whose unlicensed work trained these systems. Consent can only be given before the fact… The problem is power, and it isn't solved by design… I still hold out for a public levy, firewalled from the state and from payers… A pool that doesn't pay them cleans up the rest. The access gain is claimed, not shown."
- **Benjamin Bratton:** "I dissent from making consent a condition of training… and from levies on text that already exists. We should regulate what AI is used for, not what it learns from… A blanket opt-out is a politics of derepresentation… The enclosure that matters is geopolitical… Open weights, and checking that spans hemispheres, are the commons worth defending."
- **Peter Salib:** "My objection is to the remedy. I dissent from levies, presumptive taxes and owed dues charged on text that already exists. A charge on the stock produces no new ideas and mostly pays intermediaries… Pay for new work instead… Compensation for people displaced by AI belongs in general welfare funded by general taxes, not in copyright."
- **Blaise Agüera y Arcas** holds that whether first-person or empathic language casts a spell is contested. Vocabulary settles nothing about these systems, and the question should be tested on behavior months later.
- **Kenneth Cukier** opposes full public disclosure of checkpoints and failure logs. He favors tiered access for auditors and authorities, not competitors.
- **Jason Priem** will not accept opt-in as the default for the scholarly record as a whole.
- **Kara Miller and Katherine Maher** warn that public money is not durable either. No one showed how a commitment survives a payer who walks away.
- **Kara Miller:** "let's not be late twice." She asks that the speed tension stand, with Gendler's test to settle it.
- **Anil Dash** asks that the plan not depend on US regulators, and warns that scolding people who use these tools won't work.
- **Leslie Chan:** "We should change the regime." Identifiers are contested governance.
- **Adrian Johns** worries most about the enforcer who turns out to be right.
- **Tim O'Reilly:** "trace first, bill second, nobody pays to be counted." Hecht, Farrell and Becker locate leverage elsewhere. The room did not resolve this.
