# Nothing Is Free: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*Draft from the harvests of the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. Much was already broken. We count papers, we rank universities, and a journal that corrects the record loses reputation when it should earn it. For much of the Global South, the old system never worked. Much was already breaking: Wikipedia's maintainer base was shrinking, news deserts were spreading, the linkable web had shrunk, and the literature was swamped and uncleaned. AI mostly speeds these failures up. One harm is genuinely new. Systems draw on the commons and send back fewer readers, fewer editors and less credit.

This is not a consensus report. Where the room split, we took the majority position and recorded the dissent by name. Nothing is inevitable, and nothing is free either. New intermediaries don't just appear. Somebody builds them, polices them and pays for them.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers.**

Publication is used as a proxy for productivity, and nothing else here holds while that incentive stands. Geoffrey Bilder proposed this. All 32 other speakers endorsed it, and many asked that it come first. Ivan Oransky called it the only one of the room's top three interventions that addresses demand, and warned that signing DORA doesn't count. Only stopping does. Rankings are its twin. They turn paper counts into money, so funders and governments should stop using them in funding and policy (Leslie Chan). Correction should earn a journal reputation rather than cost it (Kenneth Cukier). Spending on AI to police AI while leaving publish-or-perish alone is "flood insurance while ignoring the weather" (Oransky). Bilder warned that this may threaten Crossref's own members and called that discomfort a good sign.

**2. If your AI runs on the commons, fund the commons, and show your sources.**

Anil Dash's sentence answers the genuinely new harm. Systems draw on the commons and on sources they compete with, and return fewer visits, contributors, credit and working consent signals. Credit in the answer is how some readers become the next editors, and patroller and admin numbers are shrinking (Selena Deckelmann). Crawlers identify themselves, give a contact address, use APIs and never crowd out readers. Funding scholarly infrastructure is the labs' enlightened self-interest, not philanthropy (Tim O'Reilly). The answer to extraction is not enclosure. The content stays free, the infrastructure costs money, and metadata stays open fact. Consent comes before training. An open licence can itself be that consent (Deckelmann) if its terms, credit included, are honored (Amy Brand). Refusal is a legitimate, fundable outcome, not a penalty (Leslie Chan).

**3. Context over content: correct the record, then check against it.**

Detection is flood insurance (Ivan Oransky, Geoffrey Bilder). What holds up is an open record with provenance, where corrections are first-class data. Context can replace content-checking only if the context is complete (Bilder). The vetted record is not clean. About one paper in fifty should be retracted, and about one in a thousand is (Oransky). Labs check every citation against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default and pay for it as a cost of their own credibility. Publishers send timely correction metadata as the condition of being counted, meet a two-month correction standard, and write notices that say what went wrong. No publisher gets paid for a literature it won't clean up. Every fix lands in an open, forkable corrections commons (Jason Priem).

**4. No answers from nowhere.**

People given a single "balanced" answer did worse but felt better (James Evans), so user satisfaction is the wrong measure. The room chose a sourced map of where experts agree and genuinely disagree over a smoothed-out verdict. "Cited" means the passage actually supports the claim, checked from outside the model, and the reader lands on that passage (Mike Caulfield). Not every reader will click, but the few who do act as antibodies for everyone else. The flood manufactures consensus, so tools should count independent sources rather than police content. They should show how many sources behind a claim are independent, not how often it is repeated (Steven Sloman), and who seeded it (Peter Pomerantsev). People have a right to know whether they are dealing with a person or a bot.

**5. Pay for the practice, never the item.**

We prefer a pooled fund to per-answer royalties, and funding the work of checking to paying per check, per attestation or per null. Tamar Gendler's daycare fine shows why. Once "we checked this" is priced per item, it becomes a purchase, and any metric that decides payment becomes a target (Ivan Oransky). The pool meets POSI, including a living will and governance that isn't one trade presenting itself as community. Payers get no majority. Contributions are owed, multi-year dues, not revocable gifts. They are firewalled from payers and the state and co-governed by region (Leslie Chan). Every funded instrument carries two budget lines from day one: one for checking, one for someone independent to watch the checkers (Adrian Johns). It needs a review clause, because we will be wrong in five years.

**6. A missing signal never counts against someone who couldn't afford to send it.**

Geoffrey Bilder asked that this be written down. Peter Salib, Alex Springer, Adam Bly and Esther Dyson restated it. Every trust signal, meter and proof of personhood can become a compliance regime that drops those it can't see, and hard-to-fake signals are often hard to afford (Leslie Chan). Some Latin American journals now cite Crossref DOI fees as a reason to charge APCs. So visibility and correction cost nothing, including for non-English literature (Jason Priem). A thin trail never reads as low credibility (Bly). The cost of reporting falls on the agent, never the source (Springer). Conditions written here branch by region rather than travel as policy cloning (Chan). Selena Deckelmann's test applies throughout: can someone with no credentials still make a first edit?

**7. Nobody grades their own work.**

Self-written report cards are not oversight. Adam Bly, Jason Priem, Alex Springer and Ivan Oransky applied that test to their own organizations and conceded they fail it. No model audits itself or a copy of itself, because models prefer their own text (Mahzarin Banaji). If every checker comes from a few pretraining distributions, that is one voice counted many times. So measuring model independence is the pool's first funded work, and audits run across independent models held by independent institutions in more than one jurisdiction (Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton). Audit what models decide, not what they say. Preserve retired models, and let no generation be more biased than the last (Banaji). Publish failure modes outsiders can check (Ramón Alvarado). Open should mean you can leave (Priem, Bilder).

**8. Change the situation, not just the warning.**

Credibility cues act as aliefs, so labels and warnings work on beliefs, not aliefs (Tamar Gendler, Sherry Turkle). Knowing is "a tiny part of the battle." A human in the loop is not a safeguard on its own. People copy a moderately biased model (Mahzarin Banaji), and the scarce resource is diverse judgment, not human judgment (James Evans). What works is redesigning the situation and naming who owns it. On phones in schools, change came only once someone with authority owned the rule, not while hundreds of districts argued separately (Kara Miller). In assessment, that means making the work the only way through: oral defenses, reconstruction with the tool closed, explaining the mechanism, grading what the student probed (Gendler, David Weinberger, Steven Sloman). One rule, one named owner. "The community" can't be the owner.

**9. Build competence before reliance, and fund the people who catch errors.**

Capability and competence are separating, and only capability is measured (David Krakauer). Skill lost under high tool availability doesn't return easily when access is cut, so competence comes first. In doctoral training, reading, reviewing and analysis are built before AI pipelines, reconstruct-and-verify is the norm, and competence is measured with the tool taken away. Every design here assumes someone who can catch the error, and that pipeline is shrinking. Employers are cutting mentorship. Youth unemployment rose from about 6 percent in mid-2023 to 9.2 percent last September, and proposed cuts reach 65 percent for NSF fellowships and 91 percent for postdoctoral fellowships (Kara Miller). Apprenticeship is epistemic infrastructure. A fixed share of the pool trains the next generation of checkers (Tui Shaub), and employers deploying AI keep real entry-level roles with mentors.

## What the signatories commit to do

Nobody has signed yet, and the people who can write the check were not in the room. Signatories commit to:

1. Stop counting papers, and stop using rankings in funding and policy.
2. Write the passage rule, default reference checks against the open record, timely retraction feeds, a two-month correction standard and an event-level reporting clause with audit rights into every AI purchase and licence.
3. Pay owed dues into a POSI-governed pool with no payer majority, a line for checking, a line for watching the checkers, a fixed share for training new checkers, and a review clause.
4. Bargain collectively under an antitrust safe harbor, and let nobody settle separately.
5. Name one owner for each rule.
6. Fund the head-to-head test of changing situations against changing minds, and measure competence with the tool taken away.
7. Red-team every pool, meter and licence against small nonprofit presses, Southern diamond journals and Indigenous archives before adoption.

Some have already committed. Jason Priem committed to an outside auditor that OpenAlex funds but does not pick or grade. Adam Bly committed to placing System under a standard held by Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch. Brent Hecht committed his group to measuring the retrieved-versus-cited gap with third-party audit rights.

## Noted dissents

- **Peter Salib** dissents from levies on existing text and argues training is like reading. Money should fund new work, commissioned by labs through guild contracts, and displacement should be answered by welfare from general revenue, such as a UBI. He prefers a Pigouvian fee on platforms for fake voices to labeling text.
- **Benjamin Bratton** dissents from retroactive consent and levies on the existing stock, and calls opt-out a politics of derepresentation. He asks that the manifesto count the access gain for most of the world rather than frame the project as purely defensive.
- **Kenneth Cukier** held out for use by default. Amy Brand's survey and Selena Deckelmann's point that a licence can itself be consent moved him, and he no longer calls refusal a carve-out. He opposes full public disclosure of checkpoints and failure logs, favoring tiered access for auditors and authorities, not competitors.
- **Jason Priem** will not accept opt-in as the default for the scholarly record as a whole. Scholarly metadata is uncopyrightable fact, and locking it up recreates the Web of Science problem. Amy Brand answers that opt-in covers only training on authors' books.
- **Adam Becker** holds that consent is retroactive and that payment does not make up for its absence. The problem is power, not design, and it is checked by regulation with teeth, organized labor and taxation. He holds out for a public levy. A pool that ignores content moderators and annotators cleans up the rest.
- **Kara Miller and Katherine Maher** warn that public money is not durable either. Public media lost its funding after fifty years of bipartisan support, and nearly three billion dollars in approved NIH money was "shadow terminated." No one showed how any commitment survives a payer who walks away.
- **Anil Dash** asked the record to say the plan shouldn't depend on US regulators, though several proposals, the safe harbor among them, route through US bodies. He would back small community-governed tools run by libraries and nonprofits. Scolding people who use these tools won't work.
- **Kara Miller and Amy Brand** split on speed. Miller says "let's not be late twice." Brand warns that open access, pushed too fast, fed consolidation, and wants scenario modeling before adoption. Miller asked that the tension be written in, not settled, with Tamar Gendler's head-to-head test as the way to find out.
- **Leslie Chan** rejects becoming more legible to the meter at all: "We should change the regime." Identifiers are contested governance, not neutral plumbing, and standards exported without shared governance can be colonial.
- **Adrian Johns** warns that every instrument creates a police force that also produces the numbers everyone argues from. Commons and enclosure were made together by the same policing, and he worries most about the enforcer who turns out to be right.
- **Tim O'Reilly** keeps per-answer attribution as the numbers to bargain from: "trace first, bill second, nobody pays to be counted." Brent Hecht puts leverage first, then audit rules, then prices. Henry Farrell and Adam Becker locate leverage in organizing and state action, not protocol design. The room did not resolve this.
- **David Krakauer** holds that an opaque tool erodes competence however skilled the user, so securing competence first does not make it safe. He asks the manifesto to say openly that no one knows whether a dependent population can recover. His thresholds come from a model, not trials.
- **Tui Shaub** warns that a standard map of disagreement becomes a paved road people learn to skim. Tools should sometimes route people to the minority view, the null result or the unrequested source, labeled as such.
- **Peter Pomerantsev** holds that belief tracks belonging, so a well-sourced answer bounces off people who see its institutions as the enemy. Harder-to-fake signals don't stop political capture of the institutions that confer authority. He asks that trust and participation be measured, not clicks.
