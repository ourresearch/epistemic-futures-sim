# Nothing Is Free: A Manifesto for Sustaining Human Knowledge

*Draft from the harvests of the MIT Epistemic Futures Summit, September 23–24, 2026*

AI did not start the strain on human knowledge. We count papers, we rank universities, and we penalize journals that correct the record. For much of the Global South, the old system never worked. Wikipedia's maintainers, local news and the linkable web were already shrinking. AI mostly speeds this up.

Two harms are new. First, systems draw on the commons and send back fewer readers, fewer editors and less credit. Second, a few models, trained on overlapping data and held by a few firms in a few jurisdictions, are becoming the default route to the record. Their errors line up, and nobody can leave.

There is also a real gain. For people kept out by language, literacy, disability or paywalls, these tools open a door. We defend the commons so that the gain lasts and reaches everyone.

This is not a consensus report. Dissents are named. Nothing is free: somebody builds, polices and pays for every intermediary.

## Principles

**1. Stop counting papers.**

Publication is used as a proxy for productivity, and nothing else here holds while that incentive stands. Geoffrey Bilder proposed this principle. All 32 other speakers endorsed it, and many asked that it come first. Ivan Oransky called it the only top intervention that addresses demand. He warned that signing DORA doesn't count, and only stopping does. Rankings are its twin, because they turn paper counts into money. Funders and governments should drop rankings from funding and policy (Leslie Chan). Correction should earn a journal reputation rather than cost it (Kenneth Cukier). Using AI to police AI while leaving publish-or-perish alone is "flood insurance while ignoring the weather" (Oransky). Bilder warned that this may threaten Crossref's own members and called that discomfort a good sign.

**2. If your AI runs on the commons, fund the commons, and show your sources.**

Anil Dash's sentence answers the first new harm: systems take from the commons and send back less. Crawlers identify themselves and never crowd out readers. Labs gain from funding the commons, because their systems are an obligate complement to it (Tim O'Reilly, Henry Farrell). But a firm that can skim will skim, and a gift can be withdrawn (Adam Becker). So support is owed and enforceable, and it discloses its funders. The answer to extraction is not enclosure. Metadata stays open fact, what is open to read stays open to read, and infrastructure costs money. Open for reading is not open for training. For in-copyright books, consent is opt-in and renewed (Amy Brand). For open works, consent is a licence whose terms are honored, after contributors are asked what it now means (Selena Deckelmann). Retrieval beats pretraining because it keeps provenance and corrections. Refusal is legitimate and fundable (Leslie Chan).

**3. Context over content: correct the record, then check against it.**

Detection is flood insurance (Ivan Oransky, Geoffrey Bilder). What holds up is an open, complete record where corrections are first-class data (Bilder). About one paper in fifty should be retracted, and about one in a thousand is (Oransky). Labs check every citation against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default. Publishers meet a two-month correction standard with notices that say what went wrong. Those that miss it leave the counted record until they correct. The pool pays sleuths bug-bounty style and funds their legal defense. Nobody retaliates against good-faith reporters, and misconduct reports are public by default (Oransky). Fixes land in an open, forkable corrections commons (Jason Priem). That commons also collects null results, failed replications and dead ends, which are now vanishing from abstracts and models. This work is funded as a practice, never per null (James Evans).

**4. No answers from nowhere.**

People given one "balanced" answer did worse but felt better (James Evans), so satisfaction is the wrong measure. The default is a sourced map of where experts agree and genuinely disagree. The exception is a labelled detour to a minority view, a null result or an unrequested source, because people learn to skim paved roads (Tui Shaub). "Cited" means the passage supports the claim, as checked outside the model, and the reader lands on that passage (Mike Caulfield). Whether readers actually read it is untested. So builders, Caulfield first, open their tools to outside tests on sound claims as well as false ones. Tools count independent sources rather than repetitions (Steven Sloman), and they show who seeded a claim (Peter Pomerantsev). People have a right to know whether they face a person or a bot, and who answers for the bot. Agents register free with an accountable owner. Humans never register (Esther Dyson).

**5. Pay for the practice, never the item.**

Fund checking as a practice through a pool, never per answer, per check or per null. Tamar Gendler's daycare fine shows why. Once "we checked this" is priced per item, it becomes a purchase, and any metric that decides payment becomes a target (Ivan Oransky). The pool meets POSI, with a living will and no payer majority. Dues are owed and multi-year, firewalled from payers and the state, and co-governed by region (Leslie Chan). States capture whoever confers authority (Peter Pomerantsev). So no single country holds the pool, its registries or their mirrors, and open data dumps let the community fork it (Geoffrey Bilder). Two budget lines run every year. One pays for checking. The other pays for independent watching of the checkers and of the buyers who enforce these rules (Adrian Johns). A concentration index is published with the payouts (James Evans). A review clause applies, because we will be wrong in five years.

**6. A missing signal never counts against someone who couldn't afford to send it.**

Geoffrey Bilder asked for this principle, and Peter Salib, Alex Springer, Adam Bly and Esther Dyson restated it. Any trust signal, meter or proof of personhood can drop those it can't see, and hard-to-fake signals are often hard to afford (Leslie Chan). So visibility, correction and failure reporting cost small and Southern presses nothing (Jason Priem, Ramón Alvarado). A thin trail never reads as low credibility (Bly). Reporting runs on one open, POSI-held schema that is free to sources and barred from ranking or excluding them (Springer). Community-derived knowledge is open only on negotiated, reversible terms. Using it requires community sign-off, benefit-sharing and community audit access (CARE, OCAP®). Southern and Indigenous institutions hold real seats wherever these conditions are set (Chan). Selena Deckelmann's test applies throughout: can someone with no credentials still make a first edit?

**7. Nobody grades their own work.**

Self-written report cards are not oversight. Adam Bly, Jason Priem, Alex Springer and Ivan Oransky conceded that their own organizations fail this test. No model audits itself or a copy of itself, because models prefer their own text (Mahzarin Banaji). Checkers drawn from a few pretraining distributions are one voice counted many times. So measuring model independence is the pool's first funded work, and audits span independent models, institutions and jurisdictions (Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton). Audit what models decide, not what they say. Outside auditors with checkpoint access run a shared battery of measures and record the results in a public, versioned bias registry. Retired models are kept, no generation may be more biased than the last, and contracts and law require all of this (Banaji). Error provenance stays separate from credit provenance, and failure classes are published (Ramón Alvarado). Events a provider reports about its own system are labelled as such (Springer). Open means you can leave (Priem, Bilder).

**8. Change the situation, not just the warning.**

Credibility cues act as aliefs, which labels and warnings don't reach (Tamar Gendler, Sherry Turkle). A human in the loop is no safeguard on its own. People copy a moderately biased model (Mahzarin Banaji), and the scarce resource is diverse judgment (James Evans). Redesigning the situation and naming its owner is the best-supported bet, but it is not a finding until the head-to-head trial runs (Gendler). A machine that says "I" or performs empathy casts a spell that disclosure doesn't break (Turkle). School phone rules changed only once someone with authority owned them (Kara Miller). In assessment, the work becomes the only way through: oral defenses, reconstruction with the tool closed and explaining the mechanism (Gendler, David Weinberger, Steven Sloman). These designs remain hypotheses until their durability is tested. Situations harden into habits, so redesigns are varied over time and checked from outside. One rule, one named owner, never "the community."

**9. Build competence before reliance, and fund the people who catch errors.**

Capability and competence are separating, and only capability is measured (David Krakauer). Lost skill returns slowly, and no one knows whether a dependent population can recover. Putting competence first is not enough. An opaque tool erodes competence however skilled the user, and only a tool whose working can be rebuilt in the mind is safe early. This is a model result, not a trial (Krakauer). Every design here assumes someone who can catch the error. Employers are cutting mentorship. Unemployment among young workers rose from just over 6 percent in June 2023 to 9.2 percent in September 2025, partly because mentorship collapsed. Proposed cuts reach 65 percent for NSF fellowships and 91 percent for postdoctoral fellowships (Kara Miller). Restoring money later may not restore skill. A fixed share of the pool trains new checkers (Tui Shaub) and funds withdrawal tests. Employers keep real entry-level roles with mentors.

## What the signatories commit to do

Nobody has signed. Those who can pay were not in the room, and neither were Global South scholars or Indigenous knowledge holders. We do not speak for them. Signatories commit to:

1. Stop counting papers, and stop using rankings in funding.
2. Write the passage rule, reference checks, retraction feeds, the two-month standard, audited event-level reporting and guaranteed exit into every AI contract. Fund a community-governed tool.
3. Pay owed dues into the pool described in Principle 5.
4. Bargain as one coalition that includes data workers, with evaluation data as leverage. Seek US, Canadian and EU safe harbors. Have someone outside the coalition hold a pledge that nobody settles separately.
5. Name one owner for each rule.
6. Fund the head-to-head and withdrawal tests, measured months later.
7. Red-team the pool, meters and licences against small presses, Southern diamond journals and Indigenous archives.

Already committed:

- **Jason Priem, for OpenAlex:** a CC0 corrections dataset, an auditor OpenAlex funds but doesn't pick, board seats outside the Anglosphere and mirrors outside the US.
- **Adam Bly, for System:** the same auditor test, a finding-level standard held by others, published failure modes and citation gap, and System's CC-BY-NC licence taken to his board.
- **Brent Hecht's group:** a watched retrieved-versus-cited audit and valuations of volunteer labor. He did not commit Microsoft.
- **Tim O'Reilly:** these terms in O'Reilly Media contracts, and the Answers royalty method published.
- **Selena Deckelmann:** Wikimedia will ask its communities what their licence means now and measure whether credit makes editors.

## Noted dissents

- **Benjamin Bratton** dissents from consent-before-training and from levies on the existing stock of text. A publisher walling off a corpus to raise its price is not the same as a community refusing under its own governance, as Mukurtu and CARE do. I support the second. Blanket opt-out is a politics of derepresentation.
- **Peter Salib** agrees that nobody asked the authors whose work trained these systems. His objection is to the remedy. He dissents from levies or presumptive taxes on text that already exists. A charge on the stock produces no new ideas, mostly pays intermediaries, and is still rent even when it is priced as dues.
- **Kenneth Cukier** no longer calls refusal a carve-out. He opposes full public disclosure of checkpoints and failure logs, favoring tiered access for auditors and authorities, not competitors.
- **Jason Priem** will not accept opt-in as the default for the scholarly record as a whole, because scholarly metadata is uncopyrightable fact. Amy Brand answers that opt-in covers only training on authors' books.
- **Adam Becker** holds that consent is retroactive and that payment does not make up for its absence. The problem is power, checked by regulation with teeth, organized labor and taxation. He holds out for a public levy. A pool that ignores moderators and annotators cleans up the rest.
- **Kara Miller and Katherine Maher** warn that public money is not durable either. Public media lost its funding after fifty years, and nearly three billion dollars in approved NIH money was "shadow terminated." No one showed how a commitment survives a payer who walks away.
- **Anil Dash** asks that the plan not depend on US regulators, and warns that scolding people who use these tools won't work.
- **Kara Miller and Amy Brand** split on speed. Miller says "let's not be late twice." Brand warns that open access, pushed too fast, fed consolidation, and wants scenario modeling first. Miller asked that the tension stand, with Tamar Gendler's test to settle it.
- **Leslie Chan** rejects becoming more legible to the meter: "We should change the regime." Identifiers are contested governance, and standards exported without shared governance can be colonial.
- **Adrian Johns** warns that every instrument creates a police force that also produces the numbers everyone argues from. He worries most about the enforcer who turns out to be right.
- **Tim O'Reilly** keeps per-answer attribution as the numbers to bargain from: "trace first, bill second, nobody pays to be counted." Brent Hecht puts leverage first. Henry Farrell and Adam Becker locate leverage in organizing and the state. The room did not resolve this.
- **David Krakauer** asks the manifesto to say openly that no one knows whether a dependent population can recover. His thresholds come from a model, not trials.
- **Peter Pomerantsev** holds that belief tracks belonging, so a well-sourced answer bounces off people who see its institutions as the enemy. He asks that trust and participation be measured, not clicks.
- **Sherry Turkle** asks regulators to require machines that don't speak in the first person or simulate emotion, and to allow no AI companions for children. For children's companionship, therapy and grief, the first question is whether, not how. Transparency does not launder inauthenticity.
