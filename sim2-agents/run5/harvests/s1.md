# Harvest — Session 1: The Epistemic Landscape

1. **AI is mostly an accelerant of failures that already existed.** Leslie Chan named enclosure and extraction, Selena Deckelmann a shrinking Wikipedia maintainer base, Katherine Maher news deserts, Ivan Oransky, Jason Priem and Adrian Johns a swamped and uncleaned literature, Amy Brand consolidation driven by open-access policy, and Mike Caulfield a shrunken linkable web, all of which predate AI. Oransky and James Evans placed the upstream fix in how hiring, funding and promotion count papers, and Evans reported that AI-using scientists gain individually while the range of topics science covers shrinks.

2. **The clearest genuinely new harm is the broken renewal loop, and who pays for it is unresolved.** Deckelmann, Maher, Alex Springer, Nick Vincent, Brent Hecht and Brand described AI drawing on the commons and on sources it now competes with while returning fewer visits, contributors, attribution and working consent signals, and Geoffrey Bilder and Oransky added that retractions cannot reach answers unless corrections sit in open, machine-readable metadata. Esther Dyson, Anil Dash and Tim O'Reilly argued AI companies should fund the commons, Tui Shaub and Johns asked who pays for enforcement and who guards the guards, and the room split on openness: Priem, David Weinberger and Benjamin Bratton favored more of it, Brand, Vincent and Chan said it feeds concentration without consent, and Blaise Agüera y Arcas preferred community governance to per-item consent.

3. **The flood manufactures consensus, so the response should count independent sources rather than police content.** Peter Pomerantsev and Peter Salib described "censorship through noise" and fake consensus, now reaching training data, and Steven Sloman, Evans and Adam Bly argued that repeated or correlated outputs may amount to a single source, a figure that can be computed. Salib proposed pricing fake voices through paid proof of personhood or a tax on platforms, and Agüera y Arcas, Bilder and Bly warned against resting fixes on labeling text as human or AI. Chan, Bilder and Johns cautioned that trust signals, meters and proof of personhood can become compliance or surveillance regimes that penalize people without credentials.

4. **Cognitive surrender belongs in scope, but the question is which capacities survive, not whether people outsource.** Sloman, Agüera y Arcas and Weinberger held that outsourcing is how knowledge always worked, and the room named the capacities at risk: oversight and the ability to reconstruct an answer (Sloman, David Krakauer), framing (Kenneth Cukier), reading (Sherry Turkle), and entry-level apprenticeship (Kara Miller, O'Reilly). "Keep humans in the loop" was contested. Ramón Alvarado said AI errors don't reveal themselves, Mahzarin Banaji showed people copy a model's bias, Evans said diverse judgment is the scarce resource rather than human judgment as such, and Tamar Gendler and Miller argued awareness remedies fail and design and collective rules work.

5. **Unresolved: how capable these systems are, and how to argue about it.** Adam Becker put hype in the moral-panic bin and denied that prediction is intelligence, while Salib cited a record of underestimation and Bratton put "just autocomplete" denial in the same bin. Cukier and Dash took middle positions, and Dash added that coercive deployment distorts every measurement. Salib offered that sceptics name concrete tasks these systems won't do by 2028 and write them down now, and the offer was not taken up in session.

## Minority positions

**Leslie Chan**
- For much of the Global South, the pre-AI system never worked. English-language hegemony and whole traditions treated as "not knowers" were structural before any model existed.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Predictions of Wikipedia's imminent death belong in the moral-panic bin, since two decades of them have not come true.
- Her answer to extraction is not enclosure. The content stays free, the infrastructure costs money, and credit in the answer is how the next Wikipedian finds the site. The test for any trust signal is whether an uncredentialed newcomer can still contribute.

**Katherine Maher**
- Institutions that expect deference will lose, with or without AI. The task is to renovate them, not just defend them.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Pushed back on a diagnosis built around signals. Disbelief is often about belonging, not evidence, so more authoritative information won't reach people, and fact-checking has hit its limits.
- Cognitive surrender is a question of agency, not only skill. Propaganda aims at helplessness, and an interface can produce the same passivity without anyone intending it.

**Ivan Oransky**
- The "vetted" record is not clean: about one paper in fifty should be retracted, and about one in a thousand is. He asked what exactly a renewal loop would renew. He called detection "flood insurance" that makes the upstream fix harder.

**Alex Springer**
- Whoever sets the measurement should not take a margin on the count. Publishers and platforms governing it together amount to a trade association.

**Sherry Turkle**
- What's new is that the tool comes as a relationship offering validation on demand. She added the "flight from conversation" to the pre-AI column and warned that averaging many sources is not knowledge.

**Nick Vincent**
- Called the current state quasi-enclosure. Value still returns to the public as subsidized cheap tools, which hides the danger, because the subsidy can be cut while the commons runs down.
- In peer review, polish no longer tracks quality, so sloppiness is gone as a cheap warning sign. Credit should shift to terse, verifiable artifacts: data, code, provenance.

**David Weinberger**
- Not everything new is damage. Being able to interrogate texts answers Socrates' objection to writing, and schools could grade the transcript for what students probed. "Did worse, felt better" argues against the oracle, not the conversation.
- Silos have been the bigger historical failure. Answering extraction with metering could hand the commons to rights-holders.

**Jason Priem**
- The renewal loop to protect is not the journal but the open record underneath it. Open, machine-readable data that separates data from intelligence is a good new development.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- The worse risk is concentration: a handful of companies and governments controlling frontier models.
- Disagreed with Turkle: sycophancy is an incentive problem, not something built into talking to a model.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- The realistic threat is not AGI but systems slowly filling with subtly buggy data and code, which the same tools won't be able to clean up.

**Amy Brand**
- Open for reading, open metadata and open for training are different things. Retrieval at inference keeps provenance, and pre-training mostly destroys it.
- In her survey of about six thousand authors, most were not against AI, 3 percent supported unregulated use, and the rest wanted consent, attribution and compensation.

**David Krakauer**
- Corrected how his view was summarized. A sufficiently opaque tool erodes competence however skilled the user, so oversight does not survive by default, contrary to Agüera y Arcas. Interrogation helps only if the student can rebuild the answer.

**Tui Shaub**
- Standardized displays of disagreement and calibrated confidence can become a script people learn to skim. Tools should sometimes surprise the user and say where the surprise came from.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- "But ChatGPT said..." used to overrule an expert is testimonial epistemic injustice and should be recorded as a harm in its own right, not as a footnote to misinformation.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Models create new biases, including a preference for AI-written text and for their own outputs, so using them to review scholarship builds in self-preference. A plural map of disagreement is still something the tool says.
- Proposed that no model generation be more biased than the one before, and that retired models stay available to auditors.

**Adam Becker**
- Doom and utopia are the same fantasy, and both keep attention on the machine and off the few very rich people building it. He does not use these tools at all, because writing is thinking.

**Adam Bly**
- Said his own trust in institutions came partly from privilege. Builders of grounded systems, himself included, should publish failure modes and open themselves to independent audit.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- If the old system never worked for the periphery, cheap access to the corpus is one of the largest knowledge transfers ever. Some of the alarm in the room is incumbents worried about their supervisory role.
- The answer to model collapse is broader data, not a small curated model built by "us and our friends."

**Mike Caulfield**
- Disagreed with Alvarado and Becker. Hallucinations cluster in situations people can learn to spot, and checkability can be built by asking for sourced material and clicking through.
- Misinformation mostly lets people keep beliefs rather than changing minds, and a sycophantic chatbot is a more efficient justification machine. He suggested cognitive surrender may be people giving up meaningless work, which Miller and Gendler disputed.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Printing's flood needed a new intermediary, the journalist, and the present needs an equivalent. Machine learning on non-text data such as retinal scans differs from language models, and treating both as autocomplete produces bad policy.

**Anil Dash**
- Hundreds of millions of people choose these tools because they get something out of them. Scolding won't work, and only a better alternative that is actually built and shipped will.
- He would not wait for US regulators to make companies pay.

**Esther Dyson**
- The business model is the whole diagnosis, not a footnote. The transparency that matters covers money flows and what systems are trained and prompted to achieve.
- Called shrinking human attention "information diabetes": as model context windows grow, ours shrink.

**James Evans**
- The share of papers reporting any null result fell sharply after ChatGPT. He proposed deliberately collecting a record of null results and dead ends.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Disagreed with Caulfield: handing off even "meaningless" work matters, because what we practice is the reach.
- Against Becker and Cukier, she held off on a sharp line between mimicking and knowing, since human brains also do the most likely thing.

**Brent Hecht**
- The old bargain rested on information asymmetry and an implicit non-compete, and ChatGPT ended both. He expects data strikes, and said explanations can make overreliance worse.

**Adrian Johns**
- Corrected the session's framing: print was never a trusted signal that later became cheap. Its credibility was built and policed over decades.
- Put literacy panic in the moral-panic bin, noting that America has had a literacy crisis in every decade since about 1920.

**Kara Miller**
- Asked who owns the fix: schools, employers or universities. Without an owner, every institution will dither on its own.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Doubted the MIT essay study, suspecting a one-line prompt whose output was simply accepted, and said we gained more from writing than Socrates' feared loss.
- These markets are early on the enshittification curve, so capture is still a choice, and premature centralization is the thing to prevent.
