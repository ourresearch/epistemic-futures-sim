# Harvest — What might a well-designed AI-mediated knowledge marketplace look like?

1. **The meter.** O'Reilly and Springer proposed a shared event spec for tracing which content fed an answer. Its rules: trace for correction and credit first, bill second; write purpose limits into the spec; never count a missing event against anyone. Springer, Deckelmann and Vincent agreed that only retrieval can be independently witnessed. Caulfield added that "cited" must mean the source actually supports the claim. Johns, Chan, Farrell, Cukier, Bratton and Dash doubted purpose limits would hold, warning that a meter becomes a choke point, a cost of visibility, a shaper of what gets paid, and a threat to open services built on top of the commons. Dyson proposed aiming transparency at companies' money flows and objectives rather than at individual retrievals.

2. **Pooled money over per-answer royalties.** Vincent, Bratton, Dyson, Evans, Deckelmann, Dash, Gendler and Hecht favored pooled, coarse funding for the commons over O'Reilly's per-answer royalties, which Dash nonetheless credited as proof that paying authors is a choice labs can make. Evans argued that labs' data-mixture decisions and scaling laws already supply the valuation. Hecht proposed a volunteer-dependence levy based on market-wage valuations of reviewers, sleuths and Wikipedians. Left open: who sits on the fund's board and whether contributors can exit (Farrell), and whether communities themselves decide distribution (Chan, via Vincent).

3. **Pricing attested human judgment.** Vincent's proposal drew the room's broadest support, from Bratton, Cukier, Gendler, Johns, and Dyson, who cited Onix as a working model. It also drew open challenges:
   - whose attestation counts, given the risk of paying for the old Northern hierarchy (Chan)
   - evaluators' bias toward their own priors, and the case for paying for negative knowledge such as null results and failed replications (Evans)
   - per-item pay turning "we checked this" from a norm into a product, and who trains the next generation of attesters (Gendler)
   - who audits the attesters (Johns)

4. **Refusal and consent.** Chan, Dash and Deckelmann held that refusal must be a legitimate, fundable outcome rather than a penalty. Springer and Dash held that training and answer-time grounding need separate consent controls. Bratton dissented, calling opt-out a politics of derepresentation. Hecht replied that being read only helps if producers survive, and Caulfield warned that gating every good source leaves readers with sourced answers they cannot click through and read.

5. **Power and governance.** Farrell and Vincent argued that bargaining power must come before prices, which requires an antitrust safe harbor so Wikimedia and the societies stop settling one by one. O'Reilly and Cukier warned that lump-sum licences entrench incumbents. O'Reilly, Springer, Vincent and Dash, crediting Geoffrey's earlier point, called for POSI governance with all dependents as stakeholders for any meter, guild or fund. Hecht said he could not commit his own lab to emitting the proposed events.

## Minority positions

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Lump-sum licensing sends most of the money to intermediaries rather than creators, and gives the licensee all future value ("once you've licensed the chicken, the licensee gets the eggs"). The harvest records only his entrenchment argument.
- Labs should pay for scholarly infrastructure (Crossref, OpenAlex, ORCID, Retraction Watch). That means checking references against it by default, running a retraction service agents query before citing, and sending extracted metadata back to the record. He backed Tui's conditional instrument for this over his own looser version.
- Proposed standardizing two telemetry fields, document hashes and relevance scores, and requiring disclosure of search traces.

**Alex Springer**
- The standard should carry signals, never prices, and he stays neutral on allocation, including O'Reilly's per-answer method. The harvest pairs him with O'Reilly without noting this.
- Said a marketplace needs plumbing (identity and measurement) before it needs prices. This orders things differently from Farrell and Vincent's bargaining power before prices.
- Telemetry becomes audit only when licences carry a right to verify, and a licence without an event-level reporting clause is incomplete.

**Nick Vincent**
- Proposed a presumptive commons-rent tax on capability nobody can explain. Firms could reduce it by licensing, working with data trusts, or paying reciprocity to commons infrastructure, and any offset would require outside audit rights rather than telemetry. The harvest does not mention the tax.
- Any attestation registry needs near-free registration, and a missing attestation can never count as evidence of fraud.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Disagreed with Vincent that the tax is his best idea. He said the next infrastructure is producing data, not guarding it.
- Said being read by the models is worth far more to him than his publisher's royalties, and that per-answer billing would cut out authors who can't afford to be counted. He favored a pool along the lines of a sovereign wealth fund.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Argued that licensing is coming whatever the room does, because deep research tools will compete on their demonstrated library.

**Leslie Chan**
- Rejected becoming more legible to the meter at all: "We should change the regime." This goes further than the harvest's framing of doubt about whether purpose limits will hold.
- Cited Latin American journals that now give DOI fees as a reason to charge APCs, as evidence that every standardized field becomes a cost of being visible.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Said the room still lacks a shared idea of what reuse even is, adding "Maybe I'm wrong."

**Anil Dash**
- Said robots.txt worked for decades and died because Big AI chose to ignore it. This diagnoses bad faith, where Springer said robots.txt was never fit to be a licensing tool.
- Called for building many small community-controlled tools instead of waiting for regulators. This cuts against Farrell's call for state action.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Described Wikipedia's problem as infrastructure load. Crawlers ignore human demand, defeat caching and saturate network links. She asked that bots identify themselves, provide a contact, and use the APIs.
- Wikipedia will not gate its content, which stays free even for commercial use. Per-answer royalties don't fit its unpaid editors. What they need is attribution that sends readers, because clicks bring in new volunteers and donors.

**Esther Dyson**
- Specified a fee on LLM revenue as the funding source, comparing it to a bottler drawing on groundwater. She described Onix's terms: the expert keeps 70 percent, can walk away, and their model is then deleted.

**James Evans**
- Proposed collective management organizations in music as the model for distributing pooled money without negotiating each deal.
- Reported that null claims in abstracts have dropped sharply since ChatGPT.

**Henry Farrell**
- Said the externalities at stake (novelty, null results, the commons) get fixed through state action and organized pressure, as with a carbon tax, not through better protocol design. This challenges O'Reilly's mechanism-design framing.

**Brent Hecht**
- Proposed an alternative paradigm: a small base model trained on consented content, with retrieval at answer time, which he said gives rights holders enough visibility to bargain.

**Adrian Johns**
- Warned that whoever runs the telemetry will produce the numbers everyone else argues from. His example was the "trillion dollars lost to piracy" figure, which came from the enforcement industry and went unquestioned.
