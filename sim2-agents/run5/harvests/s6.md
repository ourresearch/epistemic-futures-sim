# Harvest — Session 6: Closing Synthesis

1. The incentives were broken before AI, so funders must stop counting papers before anything else, and the renewal loop is the genuinely new harm: systems draw on the commons and send back fewer readers, fewer editors and less credit (Cukier, Miller, Bilder, Brand). Procurement was the lever the room trusted most, with each rule needing one named owner (Miller), though Shaub cautioned that procurement is regulation by another route, so regional co-governance and refusal as a fundable outcome belong in the terms themselves.

2. Concentration is a design problem. If every checker and auditor comes from a few pretraining distributions, that is one voice counted many times, and measuring model independence should be the pool's first funded work (Agüera y Arcas, Bratton). Bilder added that nearly all the named open infrastructure sits in one jurisdiction, and Priem and Bilder held that open data separates the record from the model, so "open" should mean you can leave. Bratton, largely alone, argued the manifesto should also count the access gain for most of the world rather than framing the project as purely defensive.

3. The pipeline of people who can catch errors has to be a funded line item, not a byproduct, because every design assumes such a person exists (Miller, Caulfield). Krakauer warned the loss may be hysteretic, meaning restoring money or reducing tool availability may not restore competence. Priem conceded no one has yet shown scholars still catch errors once the tool is removed, a test Krakauer offered to design but not fund.

4. Checking outputs and citations misses important failures. Banaji called for auditing what models decide, not what they say, using a versioned outsider-run battery with retired checkpoints kept available. Alvarado asked that error provenance be kept separate from credit provenance, and Bly argued the unit of checking should be the finding, including nulls and the count of truly independent sources.

5. Durability remains unresolved: no one showed how a multi-year commitment survives a payer who walks away (Miller), and Becker argued that every commitment made today is voluntary and revocable, so dues are "owed" only once someone organized can collect them. Consent also stayed contested. Cukier held out nearly alone for use by default, and Brand argued attribution identifies the community whose evaluations make a claim trustworthy, which payment does not restore.

**Geoffrey Bilder**

**Amy Brand**

**David Krakauer**
- He asked that the manifesto state openly whether a dependent population can recover at all. He cautioned that he doesn't know, and that his thresholds come from a model, not trials.

**Tui Shaub**
- She argued that a standard chart of disagreement becomes a paved road people learn to skim, so tools should sometimes route people to the minority view, the null result or the unrequested source, labelled as such. This cuts against Caulfield's preference for a map of named positions as the default fix.
- She relayed Leslie's warning that conditions written in this room can travel as policy cloning.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- On vocabulary, he argued the reverse of Agüera y Arcas: these are artifacts, so "intelligence" is the wrong half. He proposed "epistemic technology."
- He argued that trust has to come from warrant, not from track record alone, and that failure reporting must be cheap for small journals.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- She argued that models amplify bias rather than just reflect it, that alignment teaches a model to state the unbiased answer while covert bias keeps driving its decisions, and that the bias worsens with each generation. She asked for a rule that no generation be more biased than the last.
- She raised an untested question: whether bias people copied from a model persists after the model is gone.
- She reported that implicit attitudes shift when norms change visibly, for example through legislation, which supports changing the situation over changing minds.

**Adam Becker**
- He argued the problem is power, not design, which cuts against the harvest's framing of concentration as a design problem. He also said philanthropy is an exercise of power, not a gift.
- He argued that content moderators and annotators absorb damage these systems depend on, and that a pool which ignores them cleans up the rest.
- He corrected his earlier view: funder disclosure won't protect ordinary readers, but it gives journalists and watchers something to work with.

**Adam Bly**
- He argued that whether something is AI-generated is a separate question from whether it is credible, and that nobody should bet on watermarks.
- He said his own annual benefit statement fails Adrian's test and committed to placing System under a standard held by Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch.
- He asked what a zero-cost way in would look like, so that a thin trail never reads as low credibility.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- He argued that the fix for model collapse is producing new data about the world, such as nulls, failed replications and ecological data, not a small curated model built by the room and its friends. He called open weights the commons no single bloc can capture.
- On vocabulary, he disagreed with Cukier, Agüera y Arcas and Alvarado. He said a system built by people for a purpose is artificial by definition, and that modeling and solving problems is a fair working definition of intelligence.

**Mike Caulfield**
- He argued that the passage rule is not meant to make every reader a checker. The few percent who click act as antibodies for everyone else, and the path to the source is how those checkers get recruited.
- He argued that sycophancy is the core risk, since a chatbot that agrees with you is a more efficient justification machine than social media. He sees a map of named positions, not a verdict, as the answer.
