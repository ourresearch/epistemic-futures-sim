## Minority positions

**Anil Dash**
- Publishers answering bad actors by locking everything down, including blocking the Internet Archive, harms research and fair use. He treated lockdown as a failure equal to zero compensation.
- No plan should assume a general-tax UBI, regulators, or Big AI's goodwill. Alongside funding the commons, the room should back small, community-governed tools run by libraries and nonprofits, because scolding people who use AI tools won't work.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Attribution belongs in the norm for a practical reason: crediting Wikipedia in an answer is how some readers become the next editors, and patroller and admin numbers are already shrinking.
- She would keep Enterprise optional and the dumps free. She accepted that other communities' right to refuse is as legitimate as Wikipedians' choice to allow commercial reuse.

**Henry Farrell**
- The reason for paying knowledge producers is that models are engines of summarization and an "obligate complement" to human knowledge. The industry claim that paying producers would kill AI "has it exactly backwards."

**Adam Becker**
- He disagreed with Henry's reasoning. The case for payment is that the work was taken without consent, not that the models will starve, and arguing from the industry's survival means arguing on its terms.
- Consent is one of three grounds for calling these products "fruit of the poisoned tree." The other two are ecological cost and the treatment of content moderators, and he is wary of any payment scheme that settles consent and quietly cleans up the other two.
- Against Peter, he said taxation should target concentrated wealth, not just cushion displaced workers. Payment does not retroactively make up for missing consent.

**Brent Hecht**
- He restated "open is a tactic" as a question of what openness is for. Openness with no sustaining model lets "the biggest vacuum cleaner" take the value, "and some books never get written." Anil, Selena, Katherine, Jason, Geoffrey and Leslie all rejected the original framing.
- He argued for retrieval-based, provenance-first AI, because citation is how value gets passed back to producers. He also said data strikes work best when organized by professional domain, science among them.

**Adrian Johns**
- He said the organizing should not grow from the presses and big societies, because they are intermediaries with their own interests. This cuts against Henry's call for large presses and societies to anchor the coalition.
- Metering tools get repurposed. Whoever builds the meter also produces the numbers everyone else argues from, and automated enforcement makes property "strong but brittle" because it can't see everyday practice.

**Katherine Maher**
- Payment isn't enough. Whatever these systems take in must be something producers can see, audit and correct, with consent up front, and humans must direct and correct the system.
- Platforms should support participation in knowledge, not only consumption. She is exploring a model trained on high-integrity public broadcasting with NPR stations and possibly public broadcasters abroad.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Pay for the output, not the training. Lump-sum licensing deals deepen the richest labs' moat, often pay intermediaries instead of creators, and "once you've licensed the chicken, the licensee gets the eggs."
- Walls lose because anything rendered for a human can be scraped. Every AI system should check its citations against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default. Funding scholarly infrastructure is AI companies' "enlightened self-interest," not philanthropy.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Per-article or per-token AI money would reward the same volume play that fed paper mills and special issues.
- A citation check is only as good as its inputs, and many publishers still don't send accurate retraction metadata. Publishers need holding to account as much as platforms do, and "no publisher gets paid for a literature it won't clean up."

**Peter Salib**
- Copyright shouldn't be the vehicle for paying creators for training. He opposes training levies, collective licensing, and data strikes over the existing stock, because they move money to rights-holders and intermediaries without producing new ideas.
- Displacement from automation should be answered with real welfare, such as a UBI funded from general taxes. Copyright used as welfare picks winners arbitrarily and has an unusually high social cost.

**Alex Springer**
- His standard deliberately leaves out price. Rules for what gets counted should not be set by anyone who takes a margin on the count.
- Privacy levels in the spec hide the user's query, not which content the system used. Grounding happens per query and can be revoked, unlike training, so that is where contracts can actually bite.

**Nick Vincent**
- Labs process the commons and return it as "subsidized club goods" that can be cut back or cut off at will.
- Open data shared by one group can weaken another group's bargaining power, and compute-rich actors benefit most. So "don't make everything a market," but choose openness "with eyes open."
- Guilds selling attested evaluation data give members a reason to keep quality high, which pushes back against slop.

**Jason Priem**
- Agents, not people, will call the APIs, which breaks any sustainability model that depends on page traffic. Pricing has to follow the API.
- A paid service stays honest when the bulk data is free and forkable. Community corrections should go into an open commons, or a for-profit will build it and set the norms.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- Against Jason, he said forkability is insurance, not funding. A fork only happens when enough of the community is angry enough to run a second system, and it pays no one to maintain anything.
- Open infrastructure should never be called "free," because that erases the people who maintain it. The open context around content (metadata, provenance, retraction data) should never be sold.
- He would build no meter, pool or integrity tool unless it is open source, has open data dumps, has a living will, is governed beyond one trade, and publishes self-audits that people actually read.

**Amy Brand**
- Against Jason, she said opt-in applies only to training on authors' books, not to reading access or open metadata. Models that ignore the credit CC BY requires are failing to honor the license, not practicing openness.
- She wants short deals renegotiated every couple of years and retrieval preferred over pretraining. Attribution identifies the community whose judgment makes knowledge claims trustworthy.

**Leslie Chan**
- For Global South communities, "free for the taking" has a long colonial history. Scholars and publishers shouldn't be treated as one class of rights-holders, because big-publisher deals pay intermediaries.
- Every visibility signal can become a compliance regime that drops those it can't see. She pointed to Crossref DOI fees, which some Latin American journals now cite as a reason to charge APCs. Paying for "flow" still pays whoever the system can already see.
