# Session 5 Harvest: Interventions and Futures

## 1. Interventions

### The three the room weighted most

Geoffrey Bilder proposed these three. All 32 other speakers endorsed the first. Every speaker except Peter Salib and Steven Sloman explicitly endorsed all three. Salib and Sloman endorsed the first item and did not state a position on the second or third. No speaker objected to any of the three.

**1. Funders and institutions stop counting papers.**
- **Problem:** Publication is used as a proxy for productivity (Bilder). Nothing else discussed holds while that incentive stands.
- **Action:** Funders and institutions stop counting papers.
- **Who acts:** Funders and institutions.
- **Notes from the room:**
  - Many speakers asked that it be listed first.
  - Ivan Oransky called it the only one of the three that addresses demand, and said signing DORA doesn't count unless funders actually stop counting.
  - Amy Brand noted her 2024 workshop reached the same conclusion.
  - Leslie Chan added that rankings are its twin (see Chan below).
  - Tamar Gendler framed her assessment proposal as its mirror image on the teaching side.

**2. The pool.**
- **Problem:** Small open infrastructures lack reserves and wind-down insurance.
- **Action:** Fund reserves and wind-down insurance for small open infrastructures that meet POSI. That means open source, open data, a living will, and governance that isn't one trade presenting itself as "community." Payers don't get a majority. Self-audits count only if outsiders read them, and in Bilder's rule those readers are funders and policymakers.
- **Who acts:** Funders and institutions.
- **Tui Shaub's amendment:** A fixed share trains the next generation of checkers, and none of it is paid per check. Twenty-one speakers endorsed this amendment: Dash, Hecht, Sloman, Vincent, Weinberger, Priem, Agüera y Arcas, Krakauer, Alvarado, Banaji, Bly, Bratton, Caulfield, Cukier, Deckelmann, Dyson, Evans, Farrell, Gendler, Maher and Miller.
- **Further conditions speakers attached:**
  - Pay the ongoing practice, never per check, per attestation, per item or per null (Shaub, Hecht, Salib, Vincent, Cukier, Evans).
  - A firewall from payers and the state (Pomerantsev, Becker, Maher, Bratton, Dyson).
  - Multi-year commitments that no single payer can pull mid-cycle (Maher, Becker, Miller).
  - Dues that are owed and enforceable (Bilder, Becker).
  - A separate budget line for independent watchers of the checkers (Johns).
  - Co-governance by region (Chan).
  - A review clause (Cukier).

**3. Default citation checking against the open record.**
- **Problem:** Context can only replace content-checking if the context is complete (Bilder).
- **Action:** Labs check every citation against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default and pay for it as a cost of their own credibility. Publishers send timely correction metadata as a condition of being counted at all.
- **Who acts:** Labs and publishers.
- **Extensions from the room:**
  - The reader gets the passage that actually supports the claim (Caulfield, Dash, O'Reilly, Deckelmann, Shaub; also backed by Turkle and Weinberger).
  - A two-month correction standard (Oransky, Shaub).
  - Correction status travels with every grounding event (Springer, Brand, Bly).

**Principle Bilder asked to have written down:** "A missing signal never counts against someone who couldn't afford to send it." Salib, Springer, Bly and Dyson restated it.

**Enforcement route the room weighted most:** Universities and funders sign first through procurement (Shaub). Twenty-one speakers named institutional or procurement buyers as the actors for their proposals: Shaub, Dash, O'Reilly, Pomerantsev, Sloman, Springer, Priem, Agüera y Arcas, Krakauer, Alvarado, Banaji, Becker, Bly, Bratton, Caulfield, Chan, Deckelmann, Dyson, Evans, Farrell and Miller.

### Other interventions proposed

Listed in speaking order.

- **Tui Shaub.** Problem: nobody has signed. Action: universities and funders sign first through procurement, conditioned on the passage rule, reference checks against the open record, and a two-month correction standard. Who: universities, funders.

- **Anil Dash.** Problem: the list can't be repeated by anyone outside the room. Action: a one-sentence demand, "if your AI runs on the commons, fund the commons, and show your sources." It carries four purchase conditions: a clickable citation in every answer, the passage the reader gets, reference checks, and dues to the POSI pool. It adds Selena's first-edit test as a standard and funds small community tools stewarded by libraries. Who: universities, funders, libraries.

- **Brent Hecht.** Problem: the old bargain depended on producers not knowing their work's value and AI not competing with them, and both conditions are gone. Action: measure and publish unpaid labor (reviewers, sleuths, Wikipedians, moderators) at outside market rates, and use that figure to size dues. Payouts go to the ongoing practice, never per check. Hecht commits his own group to measuring the retrieved-vs-cited gap with third-party audit rights. Sequence: leverage, then audit rules, then prices. Who: Hecht's group, the pool.

- **Ivan Oransky.** Problem: publishers aren't accountable for correcting the record. Action:
  - a two-month retraction standard;
  - notices that say what went wrong;
  - misconduct reports public by default;
  - a research integrity office with a budget and subpoena power;
  - no pool money for a literature a publisher won't clean up;
  - the sleuth share both pays sleuths and protects them from lawsuits.
  
  Who: publishers, the pool.

- **Tim O'Reilly.** Problem: the attribution gap is a design choice. One system cited nothing in 92 percent of answers, and others visited ten relevant pages and cited three. Action: purchase terms requiring:
  - a clickable citation and the passage;
  - reference checks against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch;
  - retrieval telemetry on a shared open spec under POSI, with outside audit;
  - dues to the pool;
  - disclosure of whether the product sends readers back.
  
  Publishers owe timely retraction feeds as the condition of being counted. "Trace first, bill second, nobody pays to be counted." Who: universities, funders, publishers.

- **Peter Pomerantsev.** Problem: states are flooding training data, and provenance traces who published a claim, not who seeded it or how many sources are independent. Action: apply the Article 19 right to receive information to AI answers. That means showing the shaping sources and how many are independent, disclosing whether the user is dealing with a person or a bot, and banning covert coordinated inauthentic behavior while keeping anonymity as a right. Public money in the pool sits behind a charter firewall. Pool institutions measure trust and participation, not clicks. Who: the EU, funders via procurement.

- **Peter Salib.** Problem: when automated agents cause harm, no one can say which agent did it. Action:
  - Thin identity: every agent in a knowledge market is traceable to a disclosed human or firm. No default state access, and human anonymity is preserved.
  - Outside witnessing: sampled audits, planted test content, and auditors the provider doesn't pay. A missing signal never counts against anyone. Providers pay.
  - A Pigouvian fee on platforms for fake voices.
  - Lab contracts with guilds for new work (null results, failed replications) that fund the practice, not a price per check.
  
  Who: providers, platforms, labs.

- **Steven Sloman.** Problem: the knowledge illusion. When knowledge is at your fingertips, it feels like it's in your head. Action: build "explain the mechanism" moments into AI-assisted reading and assessment. In his studies, asking for reasons did nothing. Tools also show how many sources behind a claim are independent, counted by someone outside the system. Effectiveness is tested with the tool removed. Who: university and funder procurement.

- **Alex Springer.** Problem: licences carry payment terms but almost no telemetry, and robots.txt can't separate training from answer-time fetching. Action:
  - an event-level reporting clause with audit rights in every AI licence and procurement;
  - separate controls for training and grounding;
  - correction status travels with each grounding event;
  - repositories log retrievals at the CDN now;
  - one joint spec ask with Crossref and OpenAlex under POSI;
  - purpose limits, and agents bear the cost of emitting events;
  - a missing event never counts against anyone.
  
  Who: publishers, repositories, institutional buyers.

- **Sherry Turkle.** Problem: conversational AI now comes as a relationship, and children practise first relationships on it. Labels work on beliefs, not aliefs. Action: AI used by children stops speaking in the first person, with no simulated feeling, and a significant age cutoff for artificial companions. Turkle also backs the passage rule as a way of sending readers back to the text. Who: regulators and school systems, with one state-level rule.

- **Nick Vincent.** Problem: producers have no countervailing power and each settles alone. Action: an antitrust safe harbor for joint bargaining among information producers. Guilds keep standards, record nulls and bargain collectively. Outside audit, open entry, and rotating, flatter review roles are required. Money is paid coarsely, never per attestation. Evaluation data is the leverage, and a commons-rent tax is the backstop. Who: FTC, DOJ, the Competition Bureau, the European Commission, societies, reviewer communities.

- **David Weinberger.** Problem: schools still grade the essay, which the tools produce most easily, and nobody teaches students to talk with machines well. Action: dialogue literacy.
  - Students sometimes submit AI transcripts, graded on what they probed, where they were too trusting, and what dubious claims they caught.
  - They then rebuild the answer with the tool closed and check it against outside sources, including an explain-the-mechanism step.
  - They discuss one another's dialogues in fully human spaces.
  
  Who: school systems, universities, teachers (who need time).

- **Jason Priem.** Problem: corrections are the least funded part of the record. Crossref has about 14,000 retractions and Retraction Watch about 43,000, and agents now read uncorrected records directly. Action: a corrections commons. Every fix lands as a separate, forkable CC0 dataset. Visibility and correction cost nothing, including for non-English literature. Agents drawing on it identify themselves. OpenAlex's filters and data quality are made auditable by people it doesn't pay. Who: open infrastructures such as OpenAlex build it, the pool pays, and buyers require it.

- **Blaise Agüera y Arcas.** Problem: frontier pretraining is concentrated, so checkers drawn from the same few distributions are really one voice. Action: institutional templates for mixed human–AI work, modeled on Wikipedia bots:
  - every agent has a visible role;
  - the community sets its aggressiveness;
  - reverts come with reasons and a contest button;
  - the community can switch the machine off.
  
  Auditing runs across independent models held by independent institutions in more than one jurisdiction, and the pool funds outside probing of open-weight models. Agüera y Arcas said he cannot commit Google. Who: funders, universities via procurement, the pool.

- **Amy Brand.** Problem: licences are being signed one at a time, mostly by the largest publishers, and are silent on consent, attribution and corrections. Action: an open, rights-respecting licensing template:
  - consent before training, opt-in for books;
  - CC BY honored, including credit;
  - metadata stays open fact;
  - deals of about two years;
  - retrieval preferred over pretraining;
  - a citation counts as grounding only against a corrected record;
  - RFPs weigh climate impact, public benefit and access;
  - a CRediT category for AI, with a named responsible person.
  
  The template is red-teamed against the smallest nonprofit publishers before adoption. Who: university presses, societies and libraries bargaining together under the safe harbor.

- **David Krakauer.** Problem: capability and competence are coming apart, and only capability is measured. Opaque tools erode competence, and a human in the loop doesn't fix that. Action: tool-resistant doctoral training, where reading, reviewing and analysis are built before AI pipelines, and during training students get only tools they could in principle do without. Reconstruct-and-verify becomes the working norm, and uncritical acceptance is flagged. A fixed pool share funds measurement of performance with the tool removed. Who: funders, graduate schools, procurement buyers.

- **Ramón Alvarado.** Problem: every trace records credit, not failure, and errors are opaque. Action:
  - procurement terms, the provenance standard and pool conditions require published failure-mode classes that outsiders can check;
  - AI-assisted work is traceable enough for after-the-fact error analysis;
  - the manifesto carries a glossary (epistemic technology, epistemic trust vs. reliance, error opacity);
  - the manifesto lists "but ChatGPT said..." as a harm in its own right, a testimonial injustice against experts.
  
  Who: universities, funders, journal editors.

- **Mahzarin Banaji.** Problem: alignment teaches a model to state the unbiased answer while bias still drives its decisions. Bias grows across generations and passes to users, and models prefer their own text. Action:
  - public registration of overt and covert biases;
  - independent behavioral audits on a shared, versioned battery of indirect measures;
  - an appliance-efficiency-style rule that no new generation is more biased than the last;
  - auditors get checkpoints, retired models stay available, and data sources are disclosed;
  - evaluators are blinded to prestige cues, while readers still see the funder and track record.
  
  Who: funders and universities via procurement, the pool pays auditors, regulators as backstop.

- **Adam Becker.** Problem: meters and pools produce the numbers others argue from, and hidden funders launder junk through good work. Action: the funder goes on the face of every meter, fund, integrity tool and AI-assisted journal, as loud as the masthead. AI-vendor relationships are disclosed. "Owed" is made enforceable: dues are built so a public levy can plug in later, firewalled, funded for several years, with payers off the board. Who: the pool charter, journals, funders and procurement buyers now; legislatures later.

- **Adam Bly.** Problem: watermarks show whether something is machine-made, not whether it is credible, and a citation shows what a system says, not whether the source supports the claim. Action: an open provenance standard at the level of individual findings, covering:
  - source, population, effect size and correction status;
  - nulls and failure modes;
  - an independent-source count, including who seeded the claim;
  - the funder on its face.
  
  A thin trail never counts against credibility. The standard is held jointly by Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch under POSI, not by System. Bly commits to putting System under it and is taking System's non-commercial licence question to his board. Who: funders, institutional buyers, the pool.

- **Benjamin Bratton.** Framed as a "green line," a precondition rather than a restriction. Problem: every check comes out of one North Atlantic stack. Action: pool money for three things:
  - open-weights and local-model infrastructure run by libraries and universities on their own corpora;
  - independent auditors across hemispheres;
  - production of new data, meaning nulls, failed replications and ecological data.
  
  The pool runs on Bilder's rules. Who: funders, procurement buyers.

- **Mike Caulfield.** Problem: tools write the claim first and then attach a link, and summaries replace reading. Action: "cited" means the passage supports the claim, checked from outside the model. The reader lands on the passage and is pushed to read it. The pool funds open verification tools and training for fact-checkers, patrollers, Community Notes writers and librarians. Caulfield asks for independent testing, including of his own tools, and disclosed Google funding of some of his curriculum work. Who: procurement buyers, the pool.

- **Leslie Chan.** Problem: rankings turn paper counts into money, and visibility regimes act as governance ("comply or be invisible"). Action:
  - funders and governments stop using rankings in funding and policy;
  - data suppliers disclose ownership, data sources and the consulting they sell;
  - identifiers are governed as contested infrastructure;
  - the pool is co-governed by region and funds refusal as a legitimate outcome;
  - the red-team covers Southern diamond journals and Indigenous archives.
  
  Who: GRC funders, cOAlition S, DORA signatories, procurement buyers.

- **Kenneth Cukier.** Problem: AI delivers the median, excellence is a tail event, and correction currently costs a journal reputation rather than earning it. Action: funders and journals make public correction count toward reputation. The pool pays for curating and correcting, coarsely and never per item. AI used for review or diagnosis is checked against real-world outcomes and on what it decides, not what it says. The pool carries a review clause. Who: funders, journals, the pool.

- **Selena Deckelmann.** Problem: the supply of checkers is thinning. English Wikipedia had 448 active admins in 2023, the lowest since 2005. Reading is moving off-site, and crawlers are saturating Wikipedia's network links. Action:
  - write the recruiting path into procurement: visible credit and a passage link;
  - crawlers identify themselves, give a contact address, use APIs, and never crowd out human readers;
  - the paid API stays optional and the dumps stay free;
  - the pool funds patroller tools and newcomer onboarding;
  - the first-edit test applies.
  
  Who: institutional buyers, labs, the pool.

- **Esther Dyson.** Problem: the black box that matters most is the business model, and these systems draw on a shared public asset without paying. Action:
  - A fee on AI revenue, modeled on water rights and sized by Hecht's outside-rate method, paid as dues into a POSI pool. The pool has no payer majority, is forkable and firewalled, lets contributors leave, and lets communities decide distribution.
  - Know Your Agent: register public-facing agents, not people. The first human edit stays free, and a missing signal never counts.
  - Disclosure of money flows: who funds the system, what it optimizes for, and whether it sends readers back.
  
  Who: institutional buyers.

- **James Evans.** Problem: AI helps individual scientists and hurts science. The field narrows, null claims are suppressed, and models propose fewer nulls than people do. Action:
  - A negative-knowledge commons: registered reports, failed replications, dead-end notebooks, and null intuitions elicited from experts. It is funded as a practice, never per null.
  - Verification funded as a science, with judges drawn from outside the distribution being judged.
  - A published concentration index covering topics, reviewers and payees.
  
  Who: funders, journals, procurement buyers.

- **Henry Farrell.** Problem: leverage is scarce, not money. Action: a paired coalition. Producers organize under the safe harbor, starting with Wikipedians, societies and the station network, while buyers attach purchase conditions. Both sides make a written commitment that nobody settles separately, with the rope held by someone other than the tempted party. His norm: automation supports judgment roles such as peer review and editorial decisions but never replaces them. Who: producer communities, institutional buyers.

- **Tamar Gendler.** Problem: tying the credential to the learning turns learning into a means to the credential (the daycare fine), and warnings don't help. Action: redesign assessment so doing the work is the only way through. That means oral defenses, reconstruction with the tool closed, explain-the-mechanism, and grading what the student probed. One rule is set by a named person. The manifesto calls for a head-to-head test. Who: provosts, deans, state systems.

- **Adrian Johns.** Problem: every proposed instrument creates a police force that also produces the numbers. Action: every funded instrument carries two budget lines from day one. One pays for the checking. The other pays someone independent of both payer and payee to watch the checkers, including counting the enforcers' own figures. Also, fund Waples-style field studies of how scholars actually read and check before automating reading. Who: funders, the pool charter; legislatures later.

- **Katherine Maher.** Problem: the system is designed for consumption, but trust comes from participation. Action: public-interest institutions join the pool collectively, through the station network and possibly public broadcasters abroad, not one by one. Attribution invites contribution, not just a link. A charter firewall uses multi-year commitments that no single payer can pull mid-cycle, on the model of Congress's 1975 advance funding of public media. Who: public-interest institutions, the pool charter.

- **Kara Miller.** Problem: on-the-job training is collapsing. Youth unemployment went from about 6 percent in mid-2023 to 9.2 percent last September. Proposed cuts are 65 percent to NSF fellowships and 91 percent to postdoctoral fellowships. Action: treat apprenticeship as epistemic infrastructure. Funders protect fellowships with multi-year money. Employers deploying AI keep real entry-level roles with mentors, and buyers ask about them. Each piece has one named owner. Who: funders, employers, procurement buyers, governors.

## 2. Open questions the room said it cannot yet answer

**Leverage, enforcement and who signs**
- Nobody has signed anything. Who enforces "owed"? (Bilder)
- Who signs first? (Shaub)
- Who has the leverage to collect owed money? Henry Farrell's question, recorded by Shaub. Becker thinks it comes from organizing, not protocol design, but can't yet show the coalition.
- Hecht can't commit his own lab (recorded by Shaub). Agüera y Arcas can't commit Google.
- How does collective bargaining survive antitrust law without a safe harbor? (Hecht)
- Does evaluation-data leverage last, or erode as labs find workarounds? How do you stop a guild from becoming a cartel? Audit helps, but Vincent can't say it's enough. (Vincent)
- The economics remain unsolved. (Hecht)

**Durability against payers who rescind**
- How do you protect a commitment from a payer, state or lab, who decides to rescind it? Fifty years of bipartisan support didn't save CPB, and the NIH terminations show public money disappears too. (Maher, Miller)
- The political capture of institutions that grant authority went unanswered, including by Dash. (Dash, raising Miller's point)

**Who watches the watchers**
- Who reads the audits? (Bilder)
- Who audits the attesters? (Hecht)
- Whoever runs the meter produces the numbers everyone argues from. (O'Reilly, raising Johns's point)
- Who guards the guards? There is still no framework for holding information police to account. Johns worries most about the enforcer who turns out to be right. (Johns)
- Retrieval is the only event two independent parties can witness. Everything after it is the provider's word. (Springer)

**What traces can and can't show**
- Telemetry records what a model says it retrieved, not what drove the answer. (O'Reilly, raising Banaji's point)
- Much error opacity comes from complexity, not ownership. A perfectly governed open registry may never show where an error came from. (Alvarado)

**Independence between models and sources**
- What does competition or cooperation among AI actors look like? How independent are models trained on overlapping data? Agüera y Arcas believes this is measurable but says no one has done it. (Agüera y Arcas)
- Can independence be computed reliably when so much data overlaps? (Bly)
- Independence between models has to be measured, not assumed. (Banaji)
- Can automated verification escape circularity when judges and judged share the same priors? (Evans)

**Competence with the tool removed**
- Can scholars who run everything through these tools still catch errors with the tools taken away? (Priem, raising Krakauer's test; also Caulfield)
- Do people who learn verification tools end up with more evidence-informed views? Does a visible passage actually get read? (Caulfield, raising Shaub's worry)
- Krakauer's thresholds come from a model, not trials. What does "reconstruct" mean for a model too large to internalize? Can a population that has tipped into collective dependence climb back out? (Krakauer)
- Does the explain-it effect last beyond short lab studies, and does it carry over to daily tool use? How do you make corrigibility a cue that can't be faked? (Sloman)
- Given that people copy a moderately biased model, is "dialogue" just a friendlier loop? Does the habit of pushing back last? (Weinberger)
- Does bias picked up from a model persist once the model is gone? (Banaji)

**Situations vs. minds, and children**
- Changing situations and changing minds have never been tested head to head. The manifesto should call for that test, not assume the result. (Gendler; also recorded by Shaub)
- Can children be taught "theory of business mind" (why is this thing being so nice to me?), and how would that compare with changing their situation? (Dyson)
- No one knows how to run long-term experiments with children, and it would be unethical to wait for results. (Turkle)

**Reaching people who distrust the signalers**
- Better signals don't reach people who see the institutions giving them as the enemy. Belief follows belonging. How do you build belonging at scale without handing people a machine consensus? (Pomerantsev, raising Miller's point)

**Producers, authors and attribution**
- Does retrieval alone keep producers alive? Open access showed that a good goal pushed too fast can feed consolidation. Brand wants scenario modeling done first. (Brand)
- Training strips the author's name from the idea. That is an interest in attribution separate from payment, and it is unanswered. (Salib, raising Brand's point)
- Bly has no settled view on paying authors. (Bly)
- Does visible credit actually turn readers into editors? Do Wikipedia newcomers understand what CC BY-SA now means for training? (Deckelmann)

**Review, novelty and legitimacy**
- Can AI review recognize novelty? Farrell would bet heavily that it can't and proposes testing it with Evans. (Farrell)
- Once people suspect a role like peer review has been outsourced, can it keep its legitimacy? (Bratton, raising Farrell's question)
- How much tacit negative knowledge can ever be put into words? (Evans)

**Integrity**
- What is the real misconduct rate? Oransky thinks two percent is a floor. How do we keep integrity work from being turned into a weapon? (Oransky)

**Global reach and cost of being seen**
- How do conditions written at this table avoid becoming policy cloning? How can they branch by region rather than spread from the center? Who holds the gates? (Chan)
- What would a zero-cost path into the telemetry schema look like for small Southern journals? (Springer)
- Failure disclosure could become another costly signal only rich groups can afford. (Alvarado, raising Chan's point)
- Open data helps whoever has the most capital to use it. (Priem, raising Vincent's point)

**Salib's 2028 wager**
- The wager is still unclaimed (Salib; recorded by Shaub). Cukier said he should write down what his claim that these systems "can't frame" means they won't be able to do.

- Faith communities and humanists (Cukier); artists (Shaub); authors, who disagree among themselves (Brand)

## 4. Minority positions

- **Against levies on existing text.** Salib said, "I dissent from levies on existing text." Bratton dissented from retroactive consent and from levies on the existing stock of text. These two dissents stand against any version of dues, fees or consent terms applied to material already used.

- **Fees over labels for fake voices.** Salib proposed a Pigouvian fee on platforms instead of labeling text. No one else took this position.

- **Don't depend on US regulators.** Dash asked that the dissent record say the plan shouldn't depend on US regulators. Several interventions route through US bodies, including Vincent's FTC/DOJ safe harbor and Turkle's state-level rule.

- **Public money is not durable.** Miller and Maher argued that public money gets rescinded too, citing CPB and the NIH terminations. This cuts against proposals that lean on public levies or public funding behind a firewall (Becker, Pomerantsev) as the stable backstop.

- **Caution against a detection-and-meter roadmap.** Bilder said a roadmap that is mostly detection, meters and labels is "flood insurance" that ignores the weather. Johns argued that every instrument creates a police force that writes the numbers everyone argues from. Both cut against the weight of telemetry, registry and meter proposals (O'Reilly, Springer, Bly, Banaji, Salib, Dyson).

- **Warnings and disclosure don't change behavior.** Gendler said warning people won't help because "knowing is a tiny part of the battle." Turkle said labels work on beliefs, not aliefs. Both cut against interventions that rely mainly on disclosure: Pomerantsev's person-or-bot notice, Becker's funder on the face, Dyson's money-flow disclosure and theory of business mind.

- **Every added field is a cost of being seen.** Chan argued that visibility regimes act as governance ("comply or be invisible") and that every field added this week can become another cost of being seen. Alvarado, citing Chan, warned that failure disclosure could become a signal only rich groups can afford. Both cut against the many new required metadata fields.

- **Identifiers aren't neutral, and refusal is a legitimate outcome.** Chan held that identifiers should be governed as contested infrastructure, not treated as plumbing. He argued that standards exported without shared governance can be colonial, and that the pool should fund refusal (choosing not to collect or train). This cuts against the room's treatment of Crossref and OpenAlex checks and procurement conditions as neutral defaults.

- **Openness favors capital.** Vincent's point, raised by Priem, was that open data helps whoever has the most capital to use it. This cuts against the room's broad support for open and CC0 commons.

- **A human in the loop doesn't preserve competence.** Krakauer argued that an opaque tool erodes competence however skilled the user, and that keeping a human in the loop doesn't fix it. He would restrict tools during doctoral training. This cuts against proposals that treat human oversight of AI as sufficient.

- **Speed.** Brand warned that open access, pushed too fast, fed consolidation, and asked for scenario modeling before adoption, not after. Turkle ("we can't ethically wait") and Miller ("let's not be late twice") argued the opposite on timing.

- **Sequencing.** Hecht held that leverage comes first, then audit rules, then prices. Becker and Farrell located leverage in organizing, not protocol design. O'Reilly held "trace first, bill second." The room did not resolve this.

- **Framing.** Bratton framed his intervention as a "green line," a precondition for widening possibility rather than a restriction. He called nationally fenced "sovereign AI" a dead end and open weights the commons no single bloc can capture. He was the only speaker to frame his proposal as an enabling condition rather than a gate.
