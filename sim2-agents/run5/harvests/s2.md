# Harvest — Session 2: AI as Epistemic Actor: Risks, Capabilities, and Governance

1. **Mandatory insurance for agent registration did not survive as proposed.** Leslie Chan, Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann, Geoffrey Bilder, Katherine Maher and Henry Farrell argued it would price out small community actors, and Esther Dyson conceded, rescoping it to large retention-driven providers and their officers, scaled to harm and company size, with voluntary registration, no penalty for non-registration (Bilder's rule), and pooled cover for small projects. Tim O'Reilly proposed dropping the mandate entirely in favor of SEC-style materiality disclosure, while Peter Salib's registered AI-run entity (A-corp) drew objections from Adam Becker and Henry Farrell and a qualified defense from Blaise Agüera y Arcas.

2. **There was broad support for an open, finding-level provenance standard governed POSI-style outside any one company.** Adam Bly, Mike Caulfield, Tim O'Reilly, Alex Springer, Brent Hecht and Jason Priem backed it, while Ivan Oransky and Amy Brand warned that the retraction record itself is incomplete, so corrections must travel as first-class open data. David Weinberger and Adrian Johns warned that once the trace is used for billing it becomes a policing meter, and Weinberger and Brand endorsed Katherine Maher's ordering: consent, legibility, correction, then payment.

3. **Kara Miller's question "who owns the fix, and who pays" drew a concrete candidate answer.** Tui Shaub proposed combining Geoffrey Bilder's pooled endowment, Tim O'Reilly's lab funding for Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch, and Ivan Oransky's fixed share for corrections into one conditional-funding instrument with POSI governance and outside readers, and Jason Priem joined on the pool for corrections. Complementary levers proposed include Nick Vincent's commons-rent tax and antitrust safe harbor for collective bargaining, Henry Farrell's SAG-AFTRA model, Brent Hecht's rule that fair use does not cover commercial training without consent (which Amy Brand would sign), and Peter Pomerantsev's levy on data-driven advertising.

4. **Disclosure and humans in the loop are not safeguards on their own.** Sherry Turkle, Tamar Gendler, Steven Sloman, Mahzarin Banaji, David Krakauer and Brent Hecht argued that labels act on beliefs rather than aliefs, that people copy a moderately biased model's preferences, that fluent repetition raises belief, and that skill erodes when a tool can't be reconstructed. James Evans added that these harms are also collective, since AI helps individual scientists while narrowing science. Proposed remedies included Turkle's no-first-person rule (backed by Adam Becker, opposed by David Weinberger and Benjamin Bratton), Sloman's cues of countable independence and demonstrated corrigibility, Banaji's independent registry of covert bias, and Krakauer's call to test the person-and-tool system with the tool removed.

5. **Unresolved: what these systems are.** Ramón Alvarado's retitling to "epistemic technologies" was backed by Sherry Turkle, Adam Becker and Steven Sloman, accepted conditionally by Benjamin Bratton, reframed by Henry Farrell as "technology of governance," and opposed by Blaise Agüera y Arcas, while Tui Shaub asked the room not to settle it yet. The related dispute over "bullshit machine" also stayed open, with Becker for the term and Mike Caulfield, Bratton, Sloman, Tamar Gendler, Kenneth Cukier and Agüera y Arcas against it. Agüera y Arcas proposed as a manifesto line that no single concentration of intelligence, human or artificial, should regulate itself.

## Minority positions

**Esther Dyson**
- Users risk becoming "LLMs without the extras," fluent and fast without understanding ("information diabetes"). Users or communities with no stake in retention, not vendors, should decide which friction is "stupid."
- She prefers a few targeted rules to a sweeping statute that is outdated before it passes, which cuts against calls for broad regulation with teeth.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Opacity is intrinsic to the artifact: errors are spread across billions of weights and delivered with the same confidence as correct answers, so no disclosure of money flows fixes it. A track record justifies reliance but not epistemic trust.
- "But ChatGPT said..." is testimonial injustice against human experts.

**James Evans**
- Automated evaluation draws judges and judged from the same distribution and produces an optimistic literature about itself. Verification should be funded as its own science, and journals are currently among the only institutions with a direct incentive to do it.

**Peter Salib**
- Because no one can specify or predict what these systems will say, their outputs aren't anyone's protected speech, so the law can regulate outputs directly.
- In rebuttal to Becker and Farrell, the A-corp requires disclosed ownership on a public record, offers no shield for wrongful instruction or negligent design, and should face aggressive veil piercing. It is "a leash," not a master key.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Opinions should be collected, not averaged. Valuing AI answers because they capture "the many" erodes the habit of challenging sources.
- The harm is to the self, not only to knowledge. Before asking how to govern AI in a domain, we should ask whether it belongs there at all.

**Adam Becker**
- The problem is concentrated power and is political, not technical. It is checked by regulation with teeth, organized labor and taxation, not by registries or insurance markets.
- Evans's data is the best case against casting these tools as the fix for science.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- AI is held back by two things: retention-driven "narcissism reward loops" and precautionary regulation driven by populism. The room is trying to govern only the first.

**Mike Caulfield**
- "No answers from nowhere": the system should route people to named, sourced and contested human positions, a map rather than a verdict.

**Leslie Chan**
- "Answer only from peer-reviewed literature" means answering from what Web of Science counted, which builds the narrowing Evans found into the design.
- Refusal, meaning a decision not to collect or train, should be recognized as a legitimate, fundable outcome when governance is inadequate. She also asked for community-governed decentralized identifiers and a compute commons with transparent regional allocation.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Opacity does not settle trust. A little faith in an opaque model is acceptable if it is constantly revalidated against real-world ground truth, as with retinal scans that detect heart disease.
- Transparency should be tiered: trade secrecy for low-stakes uses, and disclosure to authorities and auditors but not rivals for loans, insurance, diagnosis and peer review.

**Anil Dash**
- He questioned whether regulation-first plans have a theory of power, given opponents with trillions of dollars and White House access. His proposal is to fund consent-trained, OSI-defined open-source alternatives stewarded by libraries, universities or NGOs.
- His test is whether a tool only does what users explicitly consented to. If a tool must be forced on people through mandates, that tells you something, and scolding existing users won't work.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- Before any registry, crawlers should identify themselves, use APIs rather than scraping, and not crowd out readers.
- Credit in the answer is how the next contributor is recruited. Without it, the humans who catch errors thin out.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Governance should rest on Ostrom-style role protocols, with checks run across genuinely independent systems and institutions and the community able to switch the machine off. He took Deckelmann's Wikipedia bot governance as the template.

**Henry Farrell**
- Every "more AI to audit the AI" answer risks a procedural arms race that will be gamed.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Governance has to change the situation in which people meet a claim, with someone named as responsible for setting it. Every trust network needs funded "terminal nodes" where a person does first-order evaluation.

**Brent Hecht**
- Accountability should run upstream to content producers, not only downstream to harmed users. Designs should be tested, including AI that challenges users rather than validating them.
- He listed economists and labor scholars as missing from the room.

**Adrian Johns**
- Registries only work on top of an existing, paid policing culture, and they always squeeze something elsewhere. Budget for the checking and for watching the checkers, and write in what happens when the tool is repurposed.

**Katherine Maher**
- Most of the world is optimistic about what AI could do for them. A governance vision that is only a list of harms forfeits the right to help shape AI.
- At NPR, nothing presented to the audience is AI-produced, and a named editor answers for every piece.

**Kara Miller**
- Taxpayers may end up as the insurer through a 2008-style bailout if the AI investment bet fails.
- Mentorship for young workers is collapsing, so the pipeline of humans who check the machines, Gendler's terminal nodes, is shrinking now.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Accountability should start with publishers: standard retraction notices that explain what went wrong, timely metadata as a condition of counting as grounding, and misconduct reports public by default.
- He supported bug-bounty-style pay for sleuths and said funders should stop counting papers. Spending on AI to police AI while leaving publish-or-perish alone is "flood insurance while ignoring the weather."

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- The state is a second black box. Governments flood information spaces, and networks are already seeding pro-Kremlin content into training sources, so provenance must cover who seeded the corpus.
- He framed governance as rights: Article 19's right to receive information, opacity as a form of censorship, a ban on covert coordinated inauthentic behavior, anonymity preserved as a right, and public-interest infrastructure protected from capture by the state that funds it.

**Steven Sloman**
- These systems are very good models of intuition, better than humans. What they lack is deliberation, which mostly happens between people. Nobody in the room verifies either; everyone relies on cues.

**Alex Springer**
- Only retrieval can be witnessed independently, from publishers' CDN logs. Everything after that is self-reported, so audits need joined logs, grounding-to-retrieval ratios as triggers, and third-party audit rights in licences.
- SPUR should write POSI into its charter now, before it grows.

**Nick Vincent**
- What the commons gets back are "subsidized club goods" the labs can cut at will. The main leverage is labs' need for fresh expert evaluation data. He acknowledged that producer guilds risk becoming cartels without outside governance.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- "Free data, paid service," which he co-wrote into POSI, was naive, and it cannot pay for correction metadata. This partly cuts against Priem.
- Coding models routinely ignore rate limits and robots.txt and mishandle cursors. Records and code are slowly filling with subtle bugs that need funded human labor to clean out.

**Amy Brand**
- Open for reading shouldn't mean open for training, including bulk dumps built for agents. She prefers retrieval to pretraining so provenance survives and licensing can change later.
- Any scheme should first be tested for its effect on the smallest nonprofit publishers, since AI deals are already concentrating among the largest.

**Jason Priem**
- He disagreed with Brand: scholarly metadata is uncopyrightable fact, and he will not accept opt-in as the default for the scholarly record as a whole. Locking it up recreates the Web of Science problem.
- Open data separates the record from the intelligence, so users can switch models without depending on one vendor.

**David Krakauer**
- Opacity may be exactly what the business model buys, because a tool you can't reconstruct is a tool you can't leave. Tool availability during training should be treated as a policy variable. His thresholds come from a model, so he wants measurement funded.

**Tui Shaub**
- A standardized map of disagreement is still a script, which cuts against Caulfield's map. Provenance should leave room for detours and for surprise that shows where it came from.
- She listed as missing: funders, scholars who don't work in English, and artists.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Output-level disclosure and self-reported telemetry, including O'Reilly's and Springer's proposals, record what a model says, not what it does. They miss covert bias by design.
- Models prefer their own and other machine-written text, so AI auditing AI builds in self-preference. She asked that retired models be preserved for auditors and that no model generation be more biased than the last.
