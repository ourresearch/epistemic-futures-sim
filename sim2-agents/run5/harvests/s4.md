# Harvest — Session 4: Epistemic Authority — Credibility in an Age of Infinite Scale

1. **Earned authority sits where evaluation is actually done, not where claims are passed along.** Tamar Gendler proposed this, and others offered tests for it: a track record that is hard to fake (Mike Caulfield), corrigibility over certainty (Steven Sloman, David Krakauer), "filter forward, not out" (David Weinberger), and a source that can say how it fails in terms outsiders can check (Ramón Alvarado). Ivan Oransky said journals mostly fail the corrigibility test today. Adrian Johns disputed that earned authority and gatekeeping can be told apart by inspection, and Leslie Chan argued the prior question is who decides what counts as evaluation, since hard-to-fake signals are often hard to afford.

2. **Credibility cues act as aliefs, so warnings that fluency is cheap won't work; the situations people read in have to change (Gendler).** Proposed changes included removing cues physically, as blind auditions did (Mahzarin Banaji); recognizing that an "AI" label doesn't break the spell (Sherry Turkle, Adam Bly); highlighting uncertainty rather than adding explanations (Brent Hecht); showing disagreement instead of one balanced answer (James Evans); and building in friction that sends readers to the actual evaluator (Tui Shaub). Weinberger and Shaub noted the fluency cue also penalized second-language writers, so its loss isn't only a loss.

3. **Whether a model can be a terminal node stayed unresolved.**
   - Adam Becker held that models have no internal check on their errors. His test: reliably flag their own errors on new material without retrieval or a human-labelled grader, run by testers the labs don't pay.
   - Caulfield, Peter Salib and Blaise Agüera y Arcas disputed his factual claim.
   - Evans reported that off-the-shelf LLM judges fall below human inter-reviewer agreement, while reward models trained on scientists' judgments clear it.

   The room broadly agreed that no model should audit itself or a copy of itself (Banaji, Agüera y Arcas, Benjamin Bratton) and that accountability must land on a disclosed person or organization (Esther Dyson, Turkle, Sloman, Amy Brand, Salib). Henry Farrell and Kara Miller rejected the view (Agüera y Arcas, Bratton) that authority belongs to roles regardless of who fills them.

4. **Persistent identity is necessary but not sufficient.** Alex Springer and Geoffrey Bilder argued a signature proves who made a claim, not that it's true, so records need independent witnesses and corroboration. Salib accepted this, and Krakauer warned that track records drift toward impact scores. Bilder, Springer, Dyson and Shaub asked that one rule be written down: a missing signal or corroboration never counts against someone who couldn't afford to send it.

5. **The evaluators are a depleting supply, and who pays for them and who holds the gate are open questions.**
   - Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann, Hecht, Katherine Maher and Tim O'Reilly argued that AI answers that hide sources cut off the recruitment of new editors and reviewers.
   - Krakauer, Miller, Gendler and Evans pointed to eroding apprenticeship and inherited priors.
   - Nick Vincent, Shaub, Brand, Oransky and Johns asked who funds the checking.

   Proposed answers to who holds the gate included disclosing funders (Becker, Dash, Dyson) and disclosing what aggregators optimize for, with outside-witnessed logs (O'Reilly). Peter Pomerantsev and Miller said the larger threat is political capture of the institutions that confer authority, which harder-to-fake signals don't address.

## Minority positions

**Tamar Gendler**
- Pairing credentials with learning already pushes motivation outside the learner, as the daycare fine did, and AI is pulling credential and learning further apart. She framed the session's question as which university functions (research, teaching, socialization, credentialing) must stay coupled and which can be rebuilt separately.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Models prefer machine-written text to human text and claim to be unbiased ("LLM hypocrisy"). Humans working alongside them take on the model's preferences.
- Blinding leaks, and it cannot undo disadvantages inherited before the audition.

**Adam Becker**
- Qualified Banaji: blind the author's name and pedigree, but make the funder the loudest cue on every journal, tool and fund.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Qualified Banaji: blind the reviewer, but the reader still needs the source's track record. Authority is lost mainly because nobody can see where a claim came from, and fakes are the smaller problem.

**Steven Sloman**
- Terminal nodes are much rarer than the framing suggests, including in the room. The design question is which cues reliably show that someone did the evaluation, not how to get more people reading firsthand.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Worried about a "central dogma" that the composite of billions of lifetimes beats any expert, which would erode the habit of challenging sources. Earned authority means someone who can be argued with and be wrong in their own name.

**Nick Vincent**
- Credit should move toward terse, checkable artifacts (raw data, code, interaction transcripts) that lower the cost of evaluation.
- Guilds and professional societies, with outside audit, should maintain evaluation standards and bargain over how expert judgment is used, instead of centralized gig labor. Fund the practice coarsely, pay for null results, and never pay per attestation.

**Jason Priem**
- Retracted part of his 2010 case: most of what altmetrics count is now cheaply faked relay. He proposed an open record with several competing, inspectable filters, because a closed ranking cannot be audited.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Answered Turkle: the composite is not an average, and diverse perspectives in structured disagreement beat single views only when they are independent. The real threat is concentration, not scale.

**Amy Brand**
- Dissented from filter-forward for everything. Where outputs affect health, unvetted circulation is dangerous, which is why preprint review was limited to human feedback on rigor and validity.
- AI should appear in contribution records as a declared contribution with a named answerable person, not as an author.

**David Krakauer**
- Expertise and authority should be separated. "Follow the science" misreads science as a place to defer to rather than a method for catching errors.
- Tool dependence erodes error-catching even in skilled users, and competence with the tool removed is measurable but unmeasured.

**Tui Shaub**
- Pushed back on relying on cues. A cue is readable without doing the work, and the smoother it is, the less people check.
- Plurality means different communities, not just different models.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Rejected treating humans as the default terminal node. Instruments routinely beat human senses, and the question is warrant, not whether the evaluator is a person.
- "But ChatGPT said..." is already used by administrators to talk down real expertise, which he called testimonial injustice.

**Adam Bly**
- Whether content is AI-generated is a separate question from whether it is credible. On that ground he opposed watermarking in favor of click-through audit trails.
- Said his own past trust in institutional pedigree was partly privilege.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Turned Becker's test on humans: no human has an internal check on their errors either, and science works because checking sits outside the individual.
- Spreading evaluation widely is not the danger. Credentialing at the wrong moment is.

**Leslie Chan**
- Rankings are authority bought through infrastructure: Elsevier supplies ranking data and sells universities tools to climb the rankings. He asked that rankings be taken out of funding decisions and that identifiers be treated as contested governance rather than neutral plumbing.
- Oral traditions, Indigenous knowledge holders and community experts never appear as nodes at all.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Defended deference. Most people don't want epistemological sovereignty, so the task is keeping institutions worth deferring to, not turning everyone into a terminal node.
- Warrant can come from revalidation against outcomes even without explanation. Authority at scale will settle in brands that correct themselves publicly, because AI delivers the median and excellence is a tail event.

**Selena Deckelmann**
- The room's credential-based signals leave out Wikipedia, where trust comes from open process, not credentials. Her test is whether someone with no credentials can still make a first edit.

**Henry Farrell**
- Because LLM judges drag scores toward the middle while humans at least notice novelty, automated review would make science more banal, not more trustworthy.

**Adrian Johns**
- Registries work only on top of an existing policing culture of legwork down the chain, and that legwork is the budget.
- Irreproducible papers are read more than reproducible ones, so the study of how experts actually read should be funded before reading is automated.

**Katherine Maher**
- Much of the crisis of authority comes from institutions that expected trust from place and prestige and never made themselves answerable to the wider public. One person's check produces "confidence transfer" to later readers only while checking remains possible.

**Tim O'Reilly**
- Labs should check every reference against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch by default and pay for those services, as a matter of their own credibility rather than philanthropy.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Correction should earn reputation rather than cost it. He called for a two-month correction standard and timely retraction feeds as a condition of lab payments.
- As long as misconduct usually leads to a long and fruitful career, no credibility signal will mean much.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Harder-to-fake signals are not what decides public belief. People trust those they do things with, and in his Corriere della Sera tests, fact-checks drew more criticism while showing the process raised trust. Earned authority invites participation, and trust and participation should be measured rather than citations or clicks.
