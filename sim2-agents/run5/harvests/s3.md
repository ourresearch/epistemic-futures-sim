# Harvest — Session 3: Knowledge Infrastructure and Tools: Commons, Enclosure, and Sovereignty

1. **Self-written report cards were not accepted as oversight.** When Adrian Johns asked every proposal to name the line between open and owned, who enforces it, and who pays to watch the enforcer, Adam Bly, Jason Priem, Alex Springer and Ivan Oransky applied the test to their own organizations and conceded they fail it, and Mahzarin Banaji argued that self-audit fails for psychological reasons as well as political ones. Adam Bly proposed an open, finding-level provenance standard governed outside any company, and others amended it to record null results (James Evans), known failure modes (Ramón Alvarado), and who seeded a claim and how many sources are independent (Peter Pomerantsev, Steven Sloman).

2. **The room leaned toward a pooled fund over per-item royalties, but its design and funding remain open.** Proposed features were:
   * contributions paid as owed dues, not revocable gifts
   * conditions attached to money, including open corrections, an outside standard, and a fixed share for independent readers
   * a living will
   * no board majority for payers
   * regional co-governance
   * no separate settlements, backed by an antitrust safe harbor

   These came from Tui Shaub, Geoffrey Bilder, Anil Dash, Esther Dyson, Leslie Chan, Henry Farrell, Nick Vincent and Amy Brand. Unresolved: Adam Becker held out for a public levy, Katherine Maher and Peter Pomerantsev warned that public money can be rescinded or captured and needs a firewall, Peter Salib dissented that money should fund new work rather than existing text, and Kara Miller noted that no payer has signed, so the pool is a candidate, not a result.

3. **Whether training on existing text requires consent stayed unresolved.** Adam Becker, Anil Dash, Brent Hecht and Amy Brand argued for consent or opt-in, Kenneth Cukier for use by default with carve-outs, Peter Salib that training is like reading, and Selena Deckelmann that an open license like CC BY-SA was itself consent. Leslie Chan argued that refusal is a legitimate outcome the pool should fund, and Peter Pomerantsev and Blaise Agüera y Arcas warned that refused space gets filled by state-seeded text, so refusal needs support.

4. **The most concrete commitment proposed was Mike Caulfield's: if a platform licenses a corpus so it can cite it, the reader gets the cited passage.** Anil Dash, Selena Deckelmann, Katherine Maher, Tamar Gendler and Sherry Turkle backed this, and Tamar Gendler argued it does more than a disclosure label. Tim O'Reilly proposed making that rule, default citation checks against Crossref, OpenAlex and Retraction Watch, and retrieval telemetry into conditions of university and funder procurement. Brent Hecht, Nick Vincent and Alex Springer supported one shared telemetry spec with outside audit rights, and Ivan Oransky cautioned that checks are only as good as the retraction metadata publishers send.

5. **Pay for the ongoing practice of checking and the pipeline that trains checkers, not per check.** Ivan Oransky, Kara Miller, Steven Sloman, Tamar Gendler, Selena Deckelmann, David Krakauer and Peter Salib made this argument, and Ivan Oransky, Geoffrey Bilder and David Krakauer added that none of it holds if funders keep counting papers. Kara Miller's questions, who signs first and who trains the checkers, got no signed answer, though Tim O'Reilly and Nick Vincent pointed to institutional buyers as the likeliest first signers.

## Minority positions

**Adrian Johns**
- The scholarly record was never a commons now being enclosed: commons and enclosure were created together by the same policing, so the question is where the line is drawn and who enforces it, not open versus closed.
- The most worrying case is an enforcer who turns out to be right, because being right does not make it accountable.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- The enclosure that matters most is the planet splitting into American, Chinese and European stacks, and open weights are the commons worth defending because no single bloc can capture them.
- Openness alone helps whoever already has capital, as when digitized land records in Bangalore were used to take land from the poor.

**Jason Priem**
- OpenAlex's paid member tiers for curating institutional data could become "pay to be visible." Correcting the record and being in it, including for non-English literature, should cost nothing.

**David Weinberger**
- His fallback for oversight is plurality: keep many imperfect indexes alive so no single enforcer owns the only map.

**Tui Shaub**
- The people who do the invisible labour, such as Latin American journal editors and non-English indexers, should help set the pool's conditions, not just receive them.

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Much opacity comes from complexity, not ownership, so a perfectly watched CC0 registry can still leave the source of an error unknown, and enforcement does not fix that.
- Outside probing of open-weight models is a form of auditing nobody pays for.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Models are now part of the scholarly record. The pool should require auditor access to training checkpoints and preservation of retired models so bias across generations can be traced.
- A paywall on the tool is already an unequal line: the free tier of GPT gave noticeably worse research advice.

**Adam Becker**
- His consent rule is retroactive, not only going forward.
- Funders of any meter, registry or integrity tool should be printed on its face, and content moderators are part of the uncounted labour.

**Mike Caulfield**
- Labs will compete by buying rights to the largest corpora, creating a new moat in which the machine reads the literature and the public cannot.

**Leslie Chan**
- Openness is relational: public-domain release suits a catalogue record but not Indigenous knowledge whose communities never agreed to be machine-readable, so CARE must sit beside FAIR.
- Conditions written at this table can travel as policy cloning, leaving Southern journals paying to comply or being made invisible.

**Kenneth Cukier**
- He opposed full public disclosure of checkpoints and failure logs, favoring tiered access for accountable auditors and public authorities but not competitors.
- The pool needs a review clause because its conditions will be wrong in five years.

**Geoffrey Bilder**
- What he actually proposed was narrower than the room's pool: an insurance pool providing wind-down reserves for small open infrastructures that meet POSI.
- The pool, registry and mirrors should not all sit in one jurisdiction, and funders and policymakers should be the ones reading the audits.

**Esther Dyson**
- The charge should work like a water-rights fee, scaling with the revenue labs earn from a shared asset.
- The .ORG sale shows nonprofit stewards can be sold, so the pool and everything it funds must be forkable.

**James Evans**
- Openness is not automatically good for the record: claims from centralized communities working from shared databases and one protocol replicated less often, so plurality of methods and data matters, not just plurality of indexes.
- Royalty splits based on labs' data-mixture weights would let labs write the numbers, so those weights need outside audit, and the pool should publish a concentration index of who gets paid.

**Henry Farrell**
- Independent readers without power to bind anyone are "democracy without the politics." Dues and levies need countervailing power to be collected, and right now nobody holds it.

**Brent Hecht**
- The charge should be priced at outside market rates for the actual work, as in his "volunteer-dependence" estimate for Reddit moderators, so the payer does not set the number.
- Broad data strikes weaken as datasets grow, so leverage has to come from targeted groups like the scholarly record.

**Katherine Maher**
- A paid API stays honest only while it is optional. Once it is the only way in, it is a wall.
- NPR licensing, if it happens, should go through the station network and possibly foreign public broadcasters collectively.

**Ivan Oransky**
- Timely, complete retraction and correction metadata from publishers should be a condition of any money.
- A fixed share should pay people rather than detection software.

**Peter Salib**
- Welfare for people displaced by automation should come from general revenue, such as UBI, not a copyright-like levy.
- New work should be commissioned directly by labs through guild contracts. The state is a more dangerous capturer than a lab that walks away.

**Alex Springer**
- His own standards body, SPUR, currently fails the test: its decision process is unwritten and nobody watches it.
- Telemetry is a warning sign, not proof. The cost of reporting should fall on the agent, never the source, and a missing event should never count against anyone.

**Sherry Turkle**
- Chatbot conversation transcripts are becoming the most intimate corpus ever collected, and for some material the question is whether it is collected at all.
- A summary delivered as "grounding" can replace reading, so tools should send people back to the text, to other people and to the library.

**Nick Vincent**
- He proposed a presumptive tax on AI profit that a firm can reduce by showing its data was licensed or returned to the commons, but only after outside audit. Audit rules and the board must be settled before prices.
- Producers' strongest bargaining chip is evaluation data: expert judgment that societies and reviewers can withhold.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- The concentration of frontier pretraining in a few companies and governments is a worse enclosure than any paywall, because it narrows what models can be checked against.
- Per-item attribution assumes a trace that does not exist. The watchers should include independent models run by independent institutions checking each other.

**Amy Brand**
- Her authors disagree among themselves on whether open for reading means open for training.
- Grounding should count only against a corrected record, and the pool should be red-teamed against small nonprofit publishers before adoption, since fast open-access policy previously fed consolidation.

**David Krakauer**
- The ability to catch errors is itself infrastructure that can be enclosed without anyone intending it. A tool you cannot reconstruct is one you cannot leave, and pricing "we checked this" invites Goodhart effects, which cuts against market-rate pricing of checking.
- A fixed share of the pool should measure whether scholars can still detect errors with the tool taken away.
