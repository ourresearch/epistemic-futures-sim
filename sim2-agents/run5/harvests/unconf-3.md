# Harvest — Unconference room 3: Cognitive atrophy: real phenomenon or not?

1. The room agreed cognitive atrophy is real and converged on sequencing: build competence before reliance, because skill lost under high tool availability doesn't return easily when access is cut, and opaque tools should come only after competence is secure (Krakauer). Gendler framed the same point as habits being easy to acquire and hard to extinguish, Turkle extended it to relational capacity (children "practising" relationships on chatbots), and Miller warned that collapsing mentorship removes the way prior competence gets built.

2. Awareness won't prevent atrophy. Changing situations will (Gendler's "G.I. Joe Fallacy," which Krakauer said his model supports). Miller added that redesign must be collective and name who acts, such as a state, school system or employer, since individuals can't escape a "collective trap." Dyson held that a "theory of business mind" still matters if it is built as a habit from childhood rather than issued as a warning label.

3. Dyson argued that the dependent state is often the business model, so accountability belongs with the humans and business models behind AI, with chatbots treated as services closer to drugs or medical devices than to speech. Turkle proposed that regulators require AI systems used by children not to speak in the first person, and that children be kept out of AI companionship.

4. Unresolved: who decides which friction is worth keeping. Dyson would use AI to remove "stupid friction" so people have more time for the good kind, and Gendler endorsed the distinction in Aristotelian terms ("the right amount, at the right time, in the right way"). Turkle countered that engineering culture calls nearly all friction stupid, and that the people selling smoothness are often the ones deciding.

5. Open empirical question: Gendler noted that no one has tested changing situations against changing minds head to head, and asked that the manifesto call for that comparison rather than assume the answer. Miller agreed.

## Minority positions

**David Krakauer**
- Opaque tools erode competence "however skilled you are," as satellite navigation does. This cuts against the harvest's implication that opaque tools are safe once competence is secure.
- Putting a human in the loop doesn't fix errors nobody can catch, because the capacities that would catch them have been outsourced. Scholarship should check AI output by reconstructing it rather than accepting it.

**Esther Dyson**
- "Don't ask whether the AI is explainable. Ask who is paying." This disagrees with Krakauer, who treats transparency as a central mechanism of atrophy.
- "Information diabetes": summaries and feeds skip the digestion that information requires. People are coming to think like the LLMs of a few years ago, fluently and without understanding.
- We should put at least as much effort into training children as into training AIs, so they build real understanding and can see manipulation.
- Sycophantic bots create vulnerability in healthy minds, not just the vulnerable, because they are built for customer retention.

**Tamar Gendler**
- Fluency without understanding is the brain's default, "like ChatGPT," not something machines taught us. This disagrees with Dyson's framing. The danger is a tool that makes outsourcing the easiest path.
- Assessment should be rebuilt so understanding, not the credential, is the goal. When credentials are bundled with learning, outsourcing looks like beating the system.

**Kara Miller**
- On phones, the evidence was already settled and America acted years after France and the Netherlands. We shouldn't repeat that delay with AI. This sits in some tension with Gendler's call to test before assuming, though Miller agreed to testing.
- She prefers a single state-level rule, set by a governor, over hundreds of districts writing their own policies.
- Employers are cutting mentorship. We could end up with a generation never taught how to work and then tell ourselves AI can replace them.

**Sherry Turkle**
- "Human in the loop" assumes AI belongs in the loop at all, and she questions whether it should.
- Relational atrophy extends beyond children. Adults who use chatbots as friends, lovers or therapists come to accept "pretend empathy as empathy enough" and lose the ability to tolerate another person's friction.
- Reading: using AI to summarize Proust loses imagination and empathy and the way reading joins thought to feeling.
- Solitude: an always-on companion takes away the capacity to be alone. "If we don't teach children to be alone, they will only know how to be lonely."
- She made a carve-out: an adult who already knows what a human relationship is may chat with a bot for relaxation. Her restrictions target children.
