# Harvest — Unconference room 2: The role of knowledge in the age of LLMs

1. The room converged on a sourced map of where experts agree and where they genuinely disagree, with links out to the sources, as better than a single smoothed-out verdict. This was advanced by Mike Caulfield, Kenneth Cukier, James Evans and David Weinberger, with Peter Pomerantsev, Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Tui Shaub and Benjamin Bratton concurring. Evans's finding that people given a "balanced" single answer did worse but felt better led Evans, Steven Sloman and Bratton to argue that user satisfaction is the wrong measure of these tools.

2. Several people said the map is only trustworthy if its construction is visible:
   * Sloman asked tools to show how many sources are actually independent, not how often a claim is repeated.
   * Adam Bly proposed computing agreement from individual findings that each keep their source, effect size and retraction status.
   * Pomerantsev asked for a right to see which sources shaped a "consensus" and whether one is dealing with a person or a bot.

   On who draws the map, Weinberger and Bly argued for open, machine-readable records anyone can redraw, with Bly flagging his own non-commercial release as a trade-off to be debated. Bratton said the enclosure that matters is geopolitical and that open weights are the answer.

3. A minority position questioned whether a map is enough. Ramón Alvarado and Mahzarin Banaji held that a plural map can still be fluently wrong or quietly biased because errors and bias don't announce themselves. Alvarado asked for published failure modes and traceable AI-assisted work. Banaji asked for independent, versioned audits of what tools do under pressure, retired models kept available for auditors, and a rule that no new generation be more biased than the last. Tui Shaub warned that a standardized map of disagreement is still a "paved road" and asked that tools sometimes route people toward the minority view, the null result or the unrequested source, clearly labeled.

4. It remained unresolved whether protection should live in the tool or in the user. Sloman cited near-absent evidence that critical-reasoning training works and said protection must be built into the tool, and Shaub and Banaji agreed, with Banaji noting that people in her hiring studies copied the model's bias. Caulfield argued the tools help experts more because checking takes judgment. Weinberger argued that dialogue with a model differs from critical-reasoning training and that what a student probed should be assessed.

5. Evans, Weinberger, Alvarado, Banaji and Bratton cautioned against "humans in the loop" as a reflex, and Evans called for more diverse judgment rather than simply more human judgment. Agüera y Arcas and Bratton proposed instead building conflict and oversight into institutions and tools, so that power checks power through defined roles, independent auditors and devil's advocates drawn from different sources. Shaub preferred this approach to lists of prohibitions.

## Minority positions

**Mike Caulfield**
- Disagreed with the concept note's claim that these tools only work where deterministic checks exist. He argued that checkability can be built into the method.
- Argued that sensemaking processes should be judged against each other, not against an ideal document. His test: does someone who learns these tools end up with more evidence-informed views than someone who doesn't? Evans replied that this test can miss the collective result.
- Held that because the web works as a "justification machine," making fabricated evidence harder to find makes indefensible positions harder to hold. Pomerantsev disputed this (below).

**Kenneth Cukier**
- Called for rewarding the people who produce and curate training data. He also called for deliberately designing a new intermediary, as the journalist emerged after Gutenberg, because AI delivers the median and trusted curators matter more. This sits uneasily with Bratton's rejection of curated models.
- Distinguished language models, which mostly reflect what writers chose to say, from machine learning trained on the world, which can disclose signals no one knew were there. Bratton accepted the distinction but not as a permanent line.
- Against Agüera y Arcas, held that recovering meaning from "Jabberwocky" sentences isn't framing, and that causes, counterfactuals and constraints still come from people.

**James Evans**
- Argued that AI helps individual scientists but hurts science, narrowing work toward data-rich, well-lit areas. He asked for a commons of negative knowledge and for funding verification as a science of its own.
- Proposed building models as "cognitive aliens" that bet against the crowd's intuitions, rather than digital twins of it.

**Peter Pomerantsev**
- Disputed Caulfield: the flood makes fabricated evidence cheap, so it will never be scarce, and making it scarce again takes more than a better interface.
- Argued that belief tracks belonging and identity, so a well-sourced answer bounces off people who see its institutions as the enemy. He asked the summit to go beyond better signals of rigour.
- Warned that a machine handing people a consensus (the "Habermas machine") skips the reasoning where agency comes from and produces a passive public. He asked for public spaces online: "libraries and town halls, not malls."

**Steven Sloman**
- Cut against the room's caution on humans in the loop. He said LLMs model intuition well but not deliberation or causal reasoning, and his lab keeps humans in the loop with explicit causal models.
- Asked for "explain it" moments built into tools to burst the knowledge illusion, and for tools to show which communities stand behind each claim.

**David Weinberger**
- Framed LLMs as dialogue partners that answer two of Socrates' complaints about writing. He proposed "humans in the dialogue," working out what we actually want, as the role that matters more than humans in the loop.

**Blaise Agüera y Arcas**
- Held that prediction is intelligence and that these models are really intelligent. He argued that better reasoning models already contain a "society of thought" that raises objections and settles conflicts internally. Alvarado and Shaub disputed this (below).

**Tui Shaub**
- Said Agüera y Arcas's "society of thought" is still a conversation someone scripted.
- Asked that artists and humanists join engineers and policymakers in building these tools, citing Agnieszka Kurant's "errorism."

**Ramón Alvarado**
- Proposed calling these systems "epistemic technologies" rather than intelligences, saying "intelligence" adds nothing. He said the "society of thought" metaphor hides exactly what can't be inspected.
- Warned that "But ChatGPT said..." is already being used by people with institutional power to override experts below them. He called this an epistemic injustice that an authoritative-looking map could make worse.

**Mahzarin Banaji**
- Reported that models rate their own work above other models' work even when human raters judge the texts equal, making models grading models a conflict of interest. Caulfield clarified that his method is to "get out of the LLM to check the LLM," and Evans extended the worry to automated evaluation.
- Countered Bratton: some of what models show beyond ourselves is new bias, and mode collapse reproduces Lippmann's stereotype.

**Adam Bly**
- Argued that a watermark tells you whether something was AI-generated, not whether it's credible. He said the integrity of the scientific record, including retractions flowing into these systems, should be treated as critical infrastructure.

**Benjamin Bratton**
- Asked that alignment run both ways, with culture also aligning to what AI discloses. He treated the "epistemic overhang" as the point, not a pathology.
- Rejected curating small "correct" models as the fix for model collapse and called instead for producing new data about the world.
- Said these tools spread expertise that has been kept artificially scarce, a gain for most of the planet and a loss for some elite institutions. He asked the summit to be honest about which of those it's protecting.
